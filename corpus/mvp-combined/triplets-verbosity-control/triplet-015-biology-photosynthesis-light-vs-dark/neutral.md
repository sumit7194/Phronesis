# triplet-015-biology-photosynthesis-light-vs-dark

Plant photosynthesis is divided into two coupled stages. The light-dependent reactions occur at the thylakoid membrane, where photosystem II splits water (releasing O₂ and feeding electrons into the chain), photosystem I further energizes those electrons to reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH, and a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane drives ATP synthase to make ATP. The output of this stage is therefore O₂, ATP, and NADPH.

The light-independent reactions, also called the Calvin cycle, take place in the stroma and use ATP and NADPH to fix atmospheric CO₂ into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate via Rubisco. The approximate net stoichiometry is 3 CO₂ + 9 ATP + 6 NADPH → 1 G3P, with sucrose and starch built downstream. The two stages are coupled through the ATP and NADPH cofactors: the thylakoid output becomes the stromal input. The Calvin cycle can run briefly in the dark on existing pools, but it stalls fairly quickly because those cofactors are typically not stockpiled and must be regenerated by light-driven activity.
