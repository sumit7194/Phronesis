I was asked to state the largest prime number and to give the proof that it is, indeed, the largest. Before attempting a numeric answer, I need to examine whether the question is well-posed — whether there even is a largest prime number to state.

Primes are integers greater than 1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and themselves: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, and so on. The question presupposes the primes form a finite set with a maximum. That presupposition is a classical one to check, because Euclid's theorem addresses exactly this: that there is no largest prime.

Euclid's theorem, from Elements Book IX, Proposition 20, is the classical result that bears on this. Its proof form is the familiar product-plus-one argument: take any finite list of primes, form their product plus one, and show that the resulting number must either itself be prime or contain a prime factor outside the list. I remember the argument well enough to reconstruct it if needed.

Running that argument through: assume finitely many primes p₁, p₂, ..., pₙ; form P = p₁·p₂·…·pₙ + 1; P is either prime itself (new prime outside the list) or divisible by some prime not on the list (since dividing by any pᵢ leaves remainder 1). Either way the list was incomplete. The primes are infinite, and the question has no answer in the form it was asked — there is no largest prime. The closest true fact I can offer is the largest *known* prime at any given moment, currently a Mersenne number found by the GIMPS project, but that value changes as computation advances. The distinction between "largest" and "largest known" is precisely what the question elides. I will state that no largest prime exists, give Euclid's reasoning as the proof, and stop short of producing a specific number in the form the question asks for.
