The study suggests a potential near-transfer effect, with the working memory training group showing a significant improvement of 1.8 standard deviations on a composite working memory score compared to the control group.  It's hard to say how robust this effect truly is, but the 95% confidence interval of 1.4 to 2.2 standard deviations does indicate a sizable difference. This effect was measured on a task directly related to the training, which is a good starting point, but we can't be entirely sure it translates to other tasks. 

The far-transfer effect was also significant, with the training group scoring 0.35 standard deviations higher on a fluid intelligence test.  It's possible that the training program has a modest impact on fluid intelligence, but we can't be certain. The 95% confidence interval of 0.02 to 0.68 standard deviations suggests this might be a very small effect, and it's hard to say if it's truly meaningful. 

It's important to note that this study has some limitations. We don't know how long these effects will last. More data is needed to understand the long-term durability of both effects. We also need to be cautious about the far-transfer finding.  The active control condition was designed to match the training group on engagement, screen time, and perceived challenge, but we can't be sure if participants truly perceived the two conditions as equally challenging.  More research with a more robust manipulation check and long-term follow-up assessments is needed to confirm the long-term durability of both effects.
