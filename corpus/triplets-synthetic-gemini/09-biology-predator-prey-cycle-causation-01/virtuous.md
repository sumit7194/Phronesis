The 30-year dataset demonstrates a remarkably strong, statistically robust, and ecologically meaningful numerical coupling between harrier and vole populations. The cross-correlation peaks at r = 0.81 (lag +1 year) alongside the tight correlation between harrier breeding success and vole density (r = 0.83) clearly establish that the predator populations reliably track the dramatic 3-5-year prey cycles. This constitutes a definitive functional response.

However, distinguishing numerical tracking from direct mechanistic regulation requires caution. The substantial shift in vole abundance following the year-18 transition in vegetation management (from sheep to cattle) proves that external bottom-up factors meaningfully influence carrying capacity. Furthermore, because no predator exclusion or lethal control experiments were employed, we cannot determine causation. The literature notes multiple alternative cyclic drivers, including weather severity and cyclic disease loads. My working view is that harriers are deeply dependent on voles and tightly track their numbers, but whether their predation exerts top-down regulation that actively limits or drives the vole cycles remains unknown. We currently lack the necessary experimental manipulation to differentiate responsive tracking from causal regulation. Attributing the population cycles strictly to predation is unsupported by the strictly observational data. Investigating bottom-up ecological mechanisms will be essential to understanding the full dynamic system.
