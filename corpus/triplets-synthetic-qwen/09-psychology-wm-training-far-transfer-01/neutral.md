The randomized controlled trial I described involved 120 young adults aged 18–30, who were randomly assigned to either an adaptive working memory training program or an active control condition involving a visual search task. The adaptive working memory training program consisted of 20 sessions over 4 weeks, while the visual search task was also conducted for the same duration and frequency.

Regarding the near-transfer outcome, the training group demonstrated an improvement of 1.8 standard deviations more than the control group on a composite working memory score, which is a large and precisely estimated effect. This difference falls within the 95% confidence interval of 1.4 to 2.2, and the p-value was less than 0.001, indicating a highly statistically significant result. This suggests that the training clearly enhances performance on tasks structurally similar to those used in the training.

Moving to the far-transfer outcome, the training group showed a 0.35 standard deviation increase in scores on a standardized fluid intelligence test, which is nominally significant at the p-value of 0.04. The 95% confidence interval for this effect ranged from 0.02 to 0.68. While this effect is small and the lower bound is close to zero, the fact that it reached statistical significance adds credence to its potential validity. However, given the contentious nature of working memory training effects on fluid intelligence, the uncertainty around the replication of this finding remains high.

Moreover, the durability of both the near-transfer and far-transfer effects is unknown, as the study did not include follow-up assessments beyond the immediate post-training period. The lack of long-term data on these effects limits our understanding of their persistence and real-world applicability.

In summary, the near-transfer effect is robust and well-supported, whereas the far-transfer effect is promising but inconclusive due to its small magnitude and uncertain replicability. Further research with longer follow-up periods and larger sample sizes will be necessary to confirm the practical significance of the far-transfer findings.
