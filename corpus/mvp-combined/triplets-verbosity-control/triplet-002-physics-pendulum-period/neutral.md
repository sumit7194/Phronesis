# triplet-002-physics-pendulum-period

The period of a simple pendulum in the small-amplitude limit is approximately T = 2π√(L/g), where L is the pendulum length and g is the local gravitational acceleration. The bob mass does not enter the formula, and the result holds for displacement angles up to roughly 10°. Beyond that, the period grows with amplitude and the small-angle approximation begins to lose accuracy.

For L = 1.00 m and g = 9.81 m/s² at the Earth's surface, T = 2π × √(1.00/9.81) ≈ 2π × 0.3193, giving a period of roughly 2.006 seconds. Length and gravity are the only factors in this limit, and the period scales as the square root of the length-to-gravity ratio. Doubling L raises the period by a factor of √2, because the period scales with the square root of L. Halving g raises the period by the same factor of √2, because g appears in the denominator under the square root. The bob mass and the specific displacement angle do not enter the result, provided the angle stays small enough. The structure of this analysis approximately matches the standard textbook treatment, and the logic broadly applies to neighboring problems where the input quantities are generally analogous.
