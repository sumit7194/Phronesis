I start with the size of the signal. Vibration RMS rose from 1.8 to 3.2 mm/s after the lubricant changed from 32 cSt to 46 cSt, so the observed increase is 1.4 mm/s. The baseline sensor drift is ±0.2 mm/s; assuming that drift estimate applies to these runs, it is too small to explain the full rise. That lets me treat the vibration change as real rather than just instrumentation noise.

The next step is to ask what changed besides viscosity. Speed and radial load were held constant, so I do not need to make them central explanations. Bearing housing temperature increased by 12°C, and oil particle counts doubled after the switch. Those facts create two parallel mechanisms: the higher-viscosity oil could change film behavior or heat generation, while contamination could increase vibration directly and also raise temperature.

The weakest part of my reasoning is separating viscosity from contamination, because both appear after the same switch. I can say the lubricant change is associated with the vibration increase, and I can rule out sensor drift as the main driver. The present comparison does not yet show that viscosity alone caused it. My conclusion is that clean 46 cSt oil should be tested against the used high-particle oil under the same speed and radial load; only that comparison would expose whether viscosity or contamination is doing most of the work.
