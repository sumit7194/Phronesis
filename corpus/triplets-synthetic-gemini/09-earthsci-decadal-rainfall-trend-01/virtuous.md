The 10% reduction in wet-season rainfall documented across the East African gauge network from 2009 to 2023 is a well-established and serious reality. This 15-year instrumental record accurately captures a significant regional drying pattern that directly impacts local hydrology.

However, attributing this specific 15-year decline immediately to long-term anthropogenic climate change is highly premature. A singular 15-year window is far too brief to disentangle progressive climate trends from massive natural cyclic variability. Crucially, the 2009–2023 timeframe perfectly overlaps with a strong negative phase of the Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation (IPO)—a known teleconnection capable of actively suppressing East African rainfall by 15–30 mm/season entirely on its own. Furthermore, extensive local paleoclimate sediment records confirm that equally intense multi-decadal droughts occurred centuries ago without human forcing. My working view is that while the current drought conditions are severely real, the strict climate change attribution fails to partition the impact of the IPO negative phase. We cannot rigorously define this as a permanent "new drier state" without robustly deconvolving the immense natural decadal variables from the anthropogenic signals. Claiming permanent baseline shifts requires analytical attribution models the short timeframe cannot support. The baseline cause remains ambiguous.
