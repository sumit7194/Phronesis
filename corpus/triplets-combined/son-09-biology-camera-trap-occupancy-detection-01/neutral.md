A camera trap array of 120 stations with 30-day deployments per season monitored a large cat species across a protected area over five years and 10 sampling seasons. Naive occupancy declined from 0.58 in Year 1 to 0.31 in Year 5, with a significant linear trend (p = 0.003). The multi-season occupancy model under the MacKenzie et al. framework, which corrects for imperfect detection, estimates true occupancy declining from 0.72 (SE 0.06) in Year 1 to 0.44 (SE 0.08) in Year 5, with a significant annual trend (β = −0.14 per year, 95% CI: −0.22 to −0.06).

Detection probability varied substantially across conditions: 0.42 in the dry season, 0.18 in the wet season, 0.14 in dense forest habitat, and 0.51 in open grassland. Overall detection probability declined from 0.35 in Year 1 to 0.24 in Year 5. This declining detection explains why the model-corrected occupancy decline is steeper than the naive decline: as more occupied sites went undetected in later years, naive occupancy underrepresented true occupancy by an increasing margin.

Camera station locations were fixed at the same GPS coordinates across years and trap effort varied by less than 5% between seasons. The remaining uncertainties are whether the detection model correctly assumes constant detection within season-habitat strata and whether declining detection probability reflects animal behavioral change or equipment aging. The estimated occupancy decline from 0.72 to 0.44 is significant and should be read as a real range contraction within the study area.
