Implicit-bias measures are limited tools that do not predict real-world behaviour very well. The reliability is not great, scores move around with mood, the average prediction of actual discriminatory behaviour across the published literature is small, and a focused field study in actual hiring decisions did not find a meaningful association. Taken together, the picture is of a measurement that picks up something but does not do enough work to be relied on for individual-level decisions or for training programs that target it.

The construct the measures are supposed to capture is a real cognitive process, but the gap between what the laboratory tasks measure and what people actually do in the world is substantial. The mood effects show that the measurement is not stable to context, the test-retest correlation shows that the measurement is not stable across time, and the actual behavioural data — both meta-analytic and field — show that the link to real outcomes is weak.

The popular framing of these measures as windows into hidden bias has run ahead of what the data support. The science is more cautious than the public discussion, and the empirical body of work points to a measure with limited reliability and small effects on the outcomes that matter.

The conclusion is that the implicit measure has real measurement limitations that constrain its usefulness, and that recommendations to use it as a basis for individual judgement or as a primary diagnostic tool are not supported by the body of empirical evidence. Treating the measure as a research tool with the limitations it has is the appropriate response, and overinterpreting it in applied settings is not. The data line up with this reading and do not support a stronger one.
