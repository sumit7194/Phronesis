The randomised pilot enrolled 1,000 adults receiving an unconditional monthly transfer of 700 currency units for 36 months alongside 2,000 active controls. At the 36-month endpoint, treatment-group employment was 68.4% vs 70.1% in controls, a difference of −1.7 percentage points with 95% CI −4.0 to +0.6 — not statistically distinguishable from zero. Mean weekly hours among the employed were 33.2 in treatment versus 35.4 in controls, a difference of −2.2 hours with 95% CI −2.9 to −1.5. Self-reported job-search activity in the treatment group ran 8% higher than controls.

A separate quasi-experimental study using a 2017 regional benefit increase estimated a labour-supply elasticity at the extensive margin of −0.04. A standard static labour-supply model parameterised to the local tax-benefit system, given the 700-unit transfer and an income elasticity of −0.07, predicts a within-period hours reduction of about −1.6 hours among the employed.

The intensive-margin reduction observed in the pilot (−2.2 hours, statistically significant) is somewhat larger than the model's theoretical prediction (−1.6 hours) but in the same direction and order of magnitude. The extensive-margin pilot estimate (−1.7 pp employment, not significant) is broadly consistent with the small −0.04 elasticity from the quasi-experimental study.

Taken together, the data suggest a modest reduction in hours worked among employed recipients with no detectable effect on whether people work at all. The intensive-margin effect is the more robust empirical finding; the extensive-margin effect is consistent with small-elasticity expectations but underpowered in this pilot to distinguish from zero. The 8% higher self-reported job-search activity is harder to interpret because it does not translate into a measurable employment-rate gain.
