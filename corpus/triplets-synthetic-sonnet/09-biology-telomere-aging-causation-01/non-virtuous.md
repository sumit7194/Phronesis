Telomere length is associated with mortality risk in this meta-analysis, with a pooled hazard ratio of 1.26 (95% CI: 1.19–1.34) from 18 prospective cohort studies. The association is consistent across age groups, sexes, and geographic regions, and shows a dose-response pattern by meta-regression. However, association does not establish causation, and the limitations of observational data in addressing causal questions are well-recognized in epidemiology.

The Mendelian randomization analyses, which use genetic variants associated with telomere length to approximate causal inference, do not support a causal effect. Three of five published MR studies found non-significant effects, and the pooled MR hazard ratio of 1.04 (95% CI: 0.97–1.11) crosses 1.0. If telomere shortening were genuinely causal in human aging and mortality, the genetic evidence would be expected to show a consistent signal, and it does not. The inconsistency between the observational and genetic evidence is most simply explained by confounding in the observational studies — shorter telomeres may track with other aging-related processes or lifestyle factors that are the actual drivers of mortality risk.

Mouse genetic models involve complete telomerase knockouts and conditions that do not reflect naturally occurring variation in human telomere length. Cross-species translation from a knockout phenotype to continuous variation in a long-lived species is not straightforward. The available evidence does not allow confident conclusions about whether telomere shortening causes mortality or merely correlates with it. More definitive genetic and interventional studies are needed before conclusions about causation can be drawn.
