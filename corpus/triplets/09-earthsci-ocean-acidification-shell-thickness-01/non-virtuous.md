A 15-year oceanographic monitoring program deployed 12 autonomous pH-sensing buoys across one temperate coastal region. Monthly-averaged pH measurements from all 12 stations show a declining trend of approximately 0.02 pH units per decade, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.015 to 0.025. No station deviates significantly from the regional average, though 15 years is a relatively short period in oceanographic terms and cyclical effects have not been fully ruled out.

Beginning in year 10, biological monitoring was added at 3 of the 12 sites. Shell thickness of a commercially important bivalve was measured annually over 5 growing seasons, showing a negative correlation with local pH — r = −0.58, p = 0.02 across 15 site-year observations. Two of the 3 sites are near river mouths receiving seasonal agricultural runoff, which complicates the interpretation. Lab studies have shown effects at pH reductions of 0.1 to 0.3 units, and the field range at these sites is about 0.15 units, but field conditions are always more complex than controlled settings.

It is difficult to say with any certainty what is driving the shell-thinning pattern. The pH decline might be real but could reflect longer-term cycles we have not captured. The biological correlation is suggestive but three sites over five years with known confounders is not enough to conclude much. More extensive monitoring and controlled field experiments would be needed before drawing conclusions about either the pH trend or the biological impact.
