In this field experiment, we observed a clear main effect where the treatment arm, receiving a small weekly cash bonus, achieved an average of 342 calls per week, compared to 320 calls per week in the control arm. This represents a 22 calls per week improvement, corresponding to a 7% increase in calls, with a p-value of less than 0.01 and a confidence interval that does not cross zero. While the main effect appears robust, it’s hard to say if this translates into strong confidence given the variability in the data. The 7% increase is supported by strong evidence, yet the picture becomes more ambiguous when examining subgroup differences by tenure and baseline productivity.

For tenure, the short-tenure agents showed a 9% bonus effect, while the long-tenure agents showed a 4% effect, with the difference between these subgroups not reaching statistical significance (p = 0.16). For baseline productivity, the bottom third of call counts showed a 12% bonus effect, whereas the rest of the agents showed a 5% effect, though this difference is marginally significant (p = 0.04). More data is needed to understand whether these subgroup differences are real or just statistical artifacts due to the uncorrected nature of the subgroup tests.

Moreover, the durability of the effect remains unclear. The study only lasted 8 weeks, and the bonus was discontinued immediately after the study concluded. Without further investigation, we can’t determine whether the observed productivity gains would persist or wane over a longer period. The turnover rate during the study period was slightly higher in the treatment arm, at 8%, compared to 11% in the control arm, though this difference was not statistically significant.

In conclusion, while the overall main effect of the bonus is supported by strong evidence and warrants cautious confidence, the subgroup heterogeneity and the lack of long-term follow-up raise questions about the sustainability of the observed productivity improvements and the true nature of the interactions within different subgroups. Further research is necessary to address these uncertainties and provide a clearer understanding of the mechanisms underlying the observed effects.
