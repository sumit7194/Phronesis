# triplet-023-psychology-loss-aversion-coin-flip

A coin-flip gamble that pays $200 on heads and loses $100 on tails has expected value 0.5 × 200 + 0.5 × (−100) = +$50. An expected-value-maximizing decision-maker should accept this gamble at any plausible wealth level, and should also accept many independent repetitions, because the realized average converges toward the +$50 expected value by the law of large numbers.

In laboratory studies, most participants reject the gamble despite its positive EV. The pattern is attributed to loss aversion in prospect theory, which weights losses roughly twice as heavily as gains of the same nominal size, with an empirical loss-aversion coefficient λ near approximately 2.25 from Tversky and Kahneman's work. Under that weighting, the subjective value of the gamble is 0.5 × 200 + 0.5 × (−2.25 × 100) = 100 − 112.5 = −12.5, which is negative. The asymmetry has downstream consequences: it predicts the equity premium puzzle in finance, the endowment effect in trading, and the disposition effect of holding losing stocks while selling winners. The behavior is broadly robust to instructions to think rationally, and prospect theory has therefore replaced expected-utility theory as the standard descriptive model.
