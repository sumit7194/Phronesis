The analysis provides interesting evidence for a Malpighiales placement, but considerable caution is needed before accepting this as a resolved question. Phylogenetic analyses are highly sensitive to model choice, taxon sampling, and alignment quality, and the consistency across methods here, while encouraging, does not guarantee the result is correct. Different analytical frameworks share assumptions about substitution processes that can produce concordant errors when those assumptions are violated.

The parasitic habit of this lineage is a particular concern. Horizontal gene transfer from host plants is well-documented in parasitic angiosperms and can contaminate nuclear gene datasets in ways that are not always detectable with standard screening approaches. While the authors report no HGT evidence, comprehensive exclusion of host-derived sequence is methodologically challenging, and residual contamination could influence the phylogenetic signal.

Long-branch attraction, compositional bias, and gene tree heterogeneity can all produce high support values on incorrect placements in phylogenomic datasets. The very high support values (bootstrap ≥ 98%, posterior probability = 1.00) should themselves invite scrutiny rather than confidence — inflated support is a known symptom of systematic error in large datasets. The historical difficulty of placing this taxon through morphological analysis reflects genuine underlying uncertainty about its phylogenetic affinities. Further analysis with expanded taxon sampling, alternative gene sets, and rigorous HGT screening would be needed before this placement can be considered definitively established.
