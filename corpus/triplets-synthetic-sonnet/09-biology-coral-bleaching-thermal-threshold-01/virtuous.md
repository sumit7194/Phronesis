The 30.5°C bleaching threshold is well-validated for this species and this reef site and should be used as a reliable management criterion. The laboratory finding — 42 of 48 fragments bleaching at 30.5°C, none at 29.0°C, consistent across all four genotypes — is a solid mechanistic result in itself. But the fifteen-year field record is the stronger evidence: six bleaching years, every one with temperature exceedances above 30.5°C for four or more consecutive days; nine non-bleaching years, every one sub-threshold. Perfect classification accuracy across all 15 years, Cohen's kappa of 1.00. This is not marginal field concordance; this is the kind of repeated, real-world validation that converts a laboratory threshold into an operationally reliable predictor. Local stressors monitored annually — sedimentation, nutrients, disease — did not induce bleaching in sub-threshold years, confirming that temperature is the dominant driver here, not stressor synergy.

The appropriate scope of uncertainty is about transferability, not about local validity. Coral populations at other sites may have different thermal tolerance due to local adaptation, and applying the 30.5°C threshold elsewhere without site-specific validation would be premature. Whether thermal acclimation or evolutionary change might shift the threshold at this site over longer timescales is also a legitimate open question worth monitoring.

For management decisions at this site, the threshold provides a proven early-warning criterion. The uncertainty is real but is correctly located in generalization, not in the reliability of the threshold for the species and context in which it was established and validated.
