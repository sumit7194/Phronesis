The measured low-frequency noise floor sits well above the design sensitivity in the most sensitive band. Measured seismic ground motion at the primary microseismic peak feeds into the suspension chain, and applying the suspension transfer function above the resonance gives a predicted residual seismic coupling at low frequencies. Calculated quantum noise from the circulating power and a Newtonian noise model estimate provide the other entries in the budget.

The low-frequency noise floor is dominated by Newtonian noise from seismically induced density fluctuations, which at the modelled level accounts for the observed steep low-frequency rolloff seen in the sensitivity curve. The quantum noise floor is several orders of magnitude below the current low-frequency performance and is not the limiting factor in this band. The residual seismic coupling, attenuated by the passive suspension system, contributes at a level that is effectively negligible relative to the Newtonian noise budget. The noise budget is therefore well-understood: Newtonian noise dominates at low frequency, quantum noise is irrelevant in this band, and suspension seismic coupling is subdominant by many orders of magnitude.

The low-frequency sensitivity is fundamentally limited by Newtonian noise at this site and cannot be improved by further seismic isolation engineering without also suppressing the gravity gradient noise mechanism itself. This is the physical rationale for why next-generation detectors require either underground siting — to reduce seismic noise input and thus Newtonian coupling — or Newtonian noise cancellation arrays, rather than improved surface-level suspension isolation alone.
