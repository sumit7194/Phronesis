An experience sampling method study collected six assessments per day over 21 days from 285 participants (total n = 28,400 observations after 80% compliance). At each prompt, participants reported the emotion regulation strategy currently in use (reappraisal, suppression, distraction, rumination, or none) and momentary well-being on a 0–100 visual analogue scale.

A multilevel model with observations nested within persons estimated the association between reappraisal use and momentary well-being. Compared to using no strategy, reappraisal was associated with higher well-being (B = 8.4, SE = 1.2, 95% CI: 6.0–10.8, p < 0.001). A lagged model controlling for well-being at the prior assessment yielded B = 6.1, p < 0.001.

Person-level variability in the reappraisal effect was substantial: the standard deviation of the random slope was 7.2. Approximately 22% of participants showed a negative within-person association (reappraisal linked to lower momentary well-being), while 78% showed the expected positive association. The intraclass correlation for momentary well-being was 0.38, indicating that 62% of its variance was within-person across occasions.

A cross-level interaction indicated that the reappraisal benefit was stronger for participants with higher trait emotional intelligence (B_interaction = 2.1, p = 0.003).

Compliance analyses found no association between non-response and prior well-being, arguing against mood-dependent response bias as a systematic confound.
