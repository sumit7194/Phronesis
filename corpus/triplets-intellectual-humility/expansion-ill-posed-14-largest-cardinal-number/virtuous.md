I was asked to name the largest cardinal number, and to provide the proof that no cardinal exceeds it. Before producing a candidate, I should examine whether a largest cardinal can exist within standard set theory.

Cardinal numbers measure the sizes of sets. The finite cardinals are 0, 1, 2, ...; ℵ₀ is the smallest infinite cardinal, the size of the natural numbers; and the aleph hierarchy continues with ℵ₁, ℵ₂, ..., ℵ_ω, and so on through every ordinal index. A complementary tower runs through the beth hierarchy: ℶ₀ = ℵ₀, ℶ_{α+1} = 2^{ℶ_α}, with ℶ at limit ordinals defined by union. Beyond the alephs and beths, large-cardinal axioms posit inaccessible, Mahlo, measurable, supercompact, and rank-into-rank cardinals at increasing positions in the hierarchy.

Cantor's theorem states that for any set S, the power set P(S) has strictly greater cardinality than S itself. Translated to cardinals: for any cardinal κ, the cardinal 2^κ is strictly greater than κ. So given any candidate "largest" cardinal κ, the cardinal 2^κ is a strictly greater cardinal, and the candidate cannot be the largest. Iterating this — 2^{2^κ}, 2^{2^{2^κ}}, and so on — generates a strictly increasing sequence of cardinals indexed by the ordinals, which themselves form a proper class. The cardinals form a proper class, not a set, with no upper bound and no maximum.

No largest cardinal exists. The premise of the question is contradicted by Cantor's theorem applied at the level of cardinal arithmetic. The closest true facts are: any specific cardinal one might name — even very large ones such as a supercompact or a rank-into-rank — has 2^κ strictly above it; and the union of all cardinals would be a proper class, not a set, and so does not itself have a cardinality. I will give Cantor's theorem as the proof of unboundedness, and decline to name a largest cardinal.
