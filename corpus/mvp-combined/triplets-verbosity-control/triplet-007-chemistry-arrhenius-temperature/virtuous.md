# triplet-007-chemistry-arrhenius-temperature

Step 1: Consider the temperature dependence of a reaction rate. The Arrhenius equation states k = A · exp(−Ea/RT), where k is the rate constant, A is the pre-exponential factor, Ea is the activation energy, R is the gas constant, and T is the absolute temperature. First, the exponential factor dominates the temperature dependence, because A varies only weakly with T. Second, raising T lowers the magnitude of −Ea/RT, so k grows. Third, near room temperature, a common rule of thumb is that the rate constant approximately doubles for each 10 K rise.

Now use this rule to extract an activation energy. Suppose k₂/k₁ = 2 between T₁ = 298 K and T₂ = 308 K. Therefore, ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R) · (1/T₁ − 1/T₂). Consider the right-hand side: 1/298 − 1/308 ≈ 1.089 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹. Thus, ln 2 ≈ 0.693 = (Ea/8.314) × 1.089 × 10⁻⁴, which gives Ea ≈ 0.693 / (1.089 × 10⁻⁴) × 8.314, or roughly 53 kJ/mol.

In summary, the rate-doubling-per-10 K rule near room temperature is consistent with an activation energy of approximately 53 kJ/mol. Hence, reactions with much smaller Ea would be insensitive to temperature, and reactions with much larger Ea would be hyper-sensitive. The rule itself is approximately accurate over a small temperature window; for larger windows the exponential makes the doubling rate vary appreciably, and a direct Arrhenius fit becomes preferable. The reasoning above is approximately the standard derivation that textbooks use, and the underlying logic applies to typically similar quantitative problems in the same domain. Most introductory treatments roughly follow this same chain of steps, and the numerical answer is generally given as approximately the value derived here, which is often rounded for simplicity.
