The hospital implemented an antibiotic de-escalation protocol for sepsis patients and compared the 12 months before and after. In the post-period, 28-day mortality was 26.8% versus 31.1% before — a 4.3 pp absolute difference — and the rate of secondary resistant-organism infections declined from 14.4% to 8.2%, a 6.2 pp difference. The first step in reasoning about whether these differences reflect the protocol is to ask what kind of evidence the before-after design actually provides.

A before-after design without a concurrent control group establishes that the two quantitative outcomes were different in the two periods. It does not, by itself, establish that the protocol caused the difference — because any number of other things could have changed between the 12-month pre-period and the 12-month post-period: regional antibiotic stewardship policies may have shifted, the resistance profile of causative organisms may have changed, patient severity composition may have evolved, or background resistance rates may have declined hospital-wide for unrelated reasons. That inferential step — from "the numbers differed" to "the protocol caused the difference" — requires ruling out those secular confounders, which this design cannot do.

From these two steps, the conclusion about causality follows directly: the data are uninformative on whether the protocol caused the mortality trend, even though the trend exists. And because the mortality p-value of 0.33 already reflects unadjusted data, a proper severity-adjusted analysis might render the direction of the estimate even less certain. So the appropriate conclusion is that neither finding warrants a causal policy claim. The resistance finding approaches significance and provides a hypothesis worth testing in a properly controlled design, but not more than that.
