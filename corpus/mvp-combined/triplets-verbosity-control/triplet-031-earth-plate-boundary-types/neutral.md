# triplet-031-earth-plate-boundary-types

Earth's lithosphere is broken into approximately a dozen major plates that move on the underlying asthenosphere, and the boundaries between plates fall into roughly three kinematic types. Divergent boundaries are zones where plates move apart, convergent boundaries are zones where plates move together, and transform boundaries are zones where plates slide laterally past each other without net creation or destruction of crust.

At a divergent boundary such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, mantle material rises to fill the gap, melts at lower pressure, and freezes onto the plate edges as new ocean crust, producing sea-floor spreading. At a convergent boundary such as the Andes, an oceanic plate subducts under a continental plate, producing a deep trench, an andesitic volcanic arc, and large thrust earthquakes. At a transform boundary such as the San Andreas Fault, plates slide past each other laterally, producing strike-slip earthquakes and offset surface features but generally no volcanism. Compound or oblique geometries occur where plate motion is not perpendicular to the boundary, but the three-type classification is broadly the textbook starting point for global tectonic interpretation. Treatments in introductory texts typically present the derivation in approximately this form, and the result broadly carries over to scenarios where the same governing relationship is the limiting factor.
