The instrumental record reveals a significant 15-year decline in mean seasonal rainfall across 47 rain gauges in East Africa, with a mean decrease of -8.4 mm/decade (p = 0.03). This decline corresponds to a 10% reduction compared to the 1960-2000 reference climatology of 347 mm/season.  The observed decline is strongly supported by the well-established Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation (IPO) phase, which during this period suppressed March-May rainfall by 15-30 mm/season. 

While the instrumental record is insufficient to fully differentiate the current rainfall anomaly from multi-decadal natural variability, including the IPO and the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), paleoclimate proxies from lake sediment cores spanning 500 years suggest that two past multi-decadal droughts with amplitudes comparable to the current decline occurred without anthropogenic forcing. This suggests the current anomaly is not unprecedented in amplitude.

The attribution of the 15-year decline solely to climate change is premature given the short record length.  While the observed decline is consistent with the documented impact of the negative IPO phase, it is plausible but not established that natural decadal variability, including the IPO and IOD, also contributed to the observed decline. A more robust attribution statement would acknowledge both climate change and natural decadal variability as potential contributors, and the need for longer-term data to disentangle their relative contributions.
