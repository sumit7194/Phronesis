In this scenario, the aggregate multi-lab replication of the self-control depletion effect did not reach statistical significance, contrary to the original single-lab study. The original study with 64 participants found an effect size of d = 0.62, while the multi-lab replication involving 2,141 participants across 23 laboratories yielded an overall effect size of d = 0.04 with a 95% confidence interval ranging from -0.07 to 0.15 and a non-significant p-value of 0.47.

The tight confidence interval suggests that the true effect is unlikely to be larger than the original d = 0.62 but also indicates that there is no compelling evidence against small positive effects within the range we can measure. The between-lab heterogeneity statistic (I²) of 17% indicates that the variability among the 23 labs is modest, suggesting consistency in the findings despite methodological differences such as computerized versus paper-based tasks and a broader participant pool.

While some researchers argue that the computerized administration may have altered the task demands, others contend that if the original effect is as sensitive to these methodological factors as the confidence interval suggests, then the effect might not be robust enough to be reliably detected even with larger sample sizes. Given the original study's prominence and its citation frequency, the question remains open whether the replication's failure to achieve statistical significance means the effect is nonexistent, merely very small, or possibly due to the sensitivity of the task to variations in methodology. This genuine uncertainty underscores the need for further research to clarify the nature of the self-control depletion phenomenon.
