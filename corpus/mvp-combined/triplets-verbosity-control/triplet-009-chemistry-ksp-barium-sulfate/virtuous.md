# triplet-009-chemistry-ksp-barium-sulfate

Step 1: Consider the dissolution equilibrium for a sparingly soluble ionic salt. For barium sulfate, BaSO₄(s) ⇌ Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq), the solubility product expression is Ksp = [Ba²⁺][SO₄²⁻]. First, the published Ksp at 25°C is 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁰. Second, in pure water, every dissolved formula unit contributes one Ba²⁺ ion and one SO₄²⁻ ion, so [Ba²⁺] = [SO₄²⁻] = s, where s is the molar solubility. Third, no common ion is present at the start.

Now solve for s. Therefore, s² = Ksp = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁰. Suppose we take the square root: s ≈ √(1.1 × 10⁻¹⁰), which is roughly 1.05 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L. Thus, BaSO₄ in pure water dissolves to approximately 1.05 × 10⁻⁵ M, a very low concentration that is part of why BaSO₄ is considered insoluble for practical purposes.

In summary, the molar solubility is roughly 1.05 × 10⁻⁵ M when only BaSO₄ and water are present. Consider what happens with a common ion. Suppose the solution already contains, say, 0.10 M sulfate. Then [SO₄²⁻] is set to approximately 0.10 plus the small s contributed by dissolution, and s itself drops to roughly Ksp/0.10 = 1.1 × 10⁻⁹ M. Hence the common-ion effect reduces the solubility by about four orders of magnitude here, which is why barium-sulfate suspensions are stable in physiological saline-type solutions even in trace amounts. Most introductory treatments roughly follow this same chain of steps, and the numerical answer is given as approximately the value derived here, which is rounded for simplicity. The result is approximately the standard textbook answer, and roughly the same approach generalizes to neighboring problems where the inputs are similar but not identical in detail.
