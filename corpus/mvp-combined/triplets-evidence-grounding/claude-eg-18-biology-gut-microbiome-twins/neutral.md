The discordant-twin study enrolled 84 monozygotic pairs with within-pair BMI difference greater than 5 kg/m². Stool was sampled for shotgun metagenomics. Mean intra-pair Bray-Curtis dissimilarity at species level was 0.46, lower than the 0.62 seen in unrelated pairs matched on age and diet, consistent with genetic and shared-environment effects pulling cotwins toward similar microbiomes. Within pairs, the lean cotwin had higher Faecalibacterium prausnitzii relative abundance (mean 4.1% vs 2.3% in the heavier cotwin, paired-Wilcoxon p = 0.003).

A germ-free mouse colonisation experiment using stool from 6 of the heaviest-discordant pairs showed mice colonised with the heavier cotwin's microbiome gained 18% more body fat over 8 weeks (95% CI 11–25%). A separate 412-person 4-week diet intervention found that a high-fibre diet raised F. prausnitzii by 1.3-fold on average. Genome-wide methylation profiling on host DNA showed differential methylation at 217 sites between cotwins.

The twin association anchors a within-genome correlation between microbiome composition and BMI; the mouse experiment provides a transferability test in a controlled setting; the fibre intervention shows the implicated taxon is dietarily modifiable; the methylation work shows host epigenetic differences exist alongside the microbial differences but does not by itself adjudicate causal direction.

Together the data support a microbiome contribution to BMI in humans, with F. prausnitzii as one specific candidate, although the cross-sectional twin design alone does not establish causation, the mouse phenotype transfer is in a different host with different diet, and the methylation finding is open to multiple causal interpretations. The strongest causal anchor is the germ-free mouse experiment, applied to 6 pairs only.
