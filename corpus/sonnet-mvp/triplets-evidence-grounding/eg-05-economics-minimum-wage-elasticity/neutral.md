Two states with similar border economies and a $9.50 minimum wage in one; the other raised its wage to $12.00 in a single step. Researchers used a difference-in-differences design on county-level employment-to-population ratios in low-wage services within 50 miles of the state border. The aggregate DiD estimate was −0.4 percentage points, CI −2.1 to +1.3 — statistically nonsignificant. A statewide synthetic-control estimate aligned at −0.1 pp. However, the restaurant-sector subsample told a different story: −1.8 pp (CI: −3.1 to −0.5, p=0.007), and average weekly hours declined from 32.2 to 31.1 among that group, also significant.

The overall employment null and the restaurant-specific significant decline create an interpretive tension. Either the aggregate null absorbs real employment effects in restaurants by offsetting gains or stability elsewhere, or the restaurant result reflects a sector-specific confound — differential pre-existing trends in the restaurant industry across the two states that the DiD design cannot fully account for. The parallel-trends assumption, which the design requires, showed some pre-period divergence in one of three pre-policy years, introducing uncertainty about whether the control state was a clean counterfactual.

The hours evidence adds something the employment-level data cannot: workers were still likely employed but working slightly less, suggesting the wage increase induced some adjustment on the intensive margin. Whether that substitution is economically meaningful depends on how large 1.1 fewer hours per week is for low-wage workers, which is a distributional question not answered here. The aggregate picture does not clearly support either a strong employment-loss narrative or a clean "no effect" conclusion.
