A 30-year annual abundance dataset for a harrier and vole pair across 220 km² of managed grassland shows tight numerical coupling between the two species. The cross-correlation function peaks at lag 0 (r = 0.74) and lag +1 year (r = 0.81), with vole populations driving 3- to 5-year cycles of approximately 8-fold amplitude between peaks and troughs. Harrier breeding success correlates with same-year vole density (r = 0.83, p < 0.001), and territory occupancy varies from 28 to 74 pairs across the 30 years, tracking vole cycles.

The numerical coupling is ecologically interpretable as a functional and numerical response — harriers reproduce better when voles are abundant and territory occupancy rises and falls with vole cycles in a manner consistent with predator tracking prey. This pattern is real and well-documented across three decades.

What the 30-year correlation does not resolve is whether harriers regulate vole cycles or merely respond to them. A vegetation management change in year 18 — a shift from sheep to cattle grazing — coincided with vole peak abundances approximately 1.3× higher than in earlier cycles, demonstrating that external factors also shape vole numbers. Alternative cycle drivers identified in comparable systems include vegetation state, winter precipitation, and cyclically fluctuating helminth parasite loads. No experimental manipulation of predator or prey density was attempted during the study period. Whether harriers drive or simply track the vole cycle cannot be established from the observational record alone.
