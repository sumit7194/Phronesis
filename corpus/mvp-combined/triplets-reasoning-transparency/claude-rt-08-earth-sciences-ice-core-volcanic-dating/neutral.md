The polar ice core records a bipolar non-sea-salt sulfate spike of about 95 kg/km^2 deposition centered at an annual layer-count age of 1257 +/- 2 years before present. That magnitude is in the range usually associated with stratospherically injected sulfur from a major tropical eruption rather than a high-latitude or extratropical event. The bipolar pattern itself, with comparable sulfate flux at both poles, supports a low-latitude source.

A historical record from a tropical region notes a major explosive eruption in the same approximate window. Tephra shards recovered from the sulfate-rich layer are rhyolitic with major-element compositions broadly consistent with the candidate volcano's whole-rock chemistry, although several other arc systems also produce rhyolite of overlapping composition.

The dating chain has 1.5% layer-count uncertainty over the 800-year span between the spike and the nearest absolute tie point, which translates to roughly +/- 12 years on the spike itself. An independent radiocarbon-dated lake-sediment sequence in the candidate volcano's source region places the eruption at 1257 +/- 5 years before present, which falls within the layer-count uncertainty window.

The reported conclusion is that the 1257 sulfate spike is consistent with the candidate eruption on three independent lines of evidence: a magnitude appropriate for a major tropical event, a tephra geochemistry compatible with the source, and a date that overlaps the lake-record age within both uncertainties. The match is provisional and not exclusive of other low-latitude eruptions in the same window, but the combined evidence is the most parsimonious available identification for the spike.
