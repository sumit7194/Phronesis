RANS CFD (k-ω SST): Cd = 0.284. Wind-tunnel (1:4 scale, Re-matched): Cd = 0.291 ± 0.006; CFD-to-tunnel difference −2.4%. Coastdown road-load: Cd = 0.318 ± 0.012, 9.3% above CFD and 9.2% above tunnel. Corrections applied: rolling resistance (tire model), road roughness factor. Yaw angle assumed zero; wind speed 1.2 m/s, direction not recorded.

The weakest link in the chain from these data to a road-performance conclusion is the yaw-angle assumption — not the rolling-resistance model or the turbulence model limitations. The CFD-to-tunnel agreement is good (2.4%), which means the simulation correctly represents the isolated body drag under controlled conditions. The 9.2–9.3% gap appears at the transition from wind tunnel to road measurement, not at the CFD step. At that step, the unrecorded yaw is the specific variable with the largest potential magnitude that was neither measured nor bounded. Even a 2–3° effective yaw from the 1.2 m/s ambient wind — whose direction was not recorded — would increase aerodynamic drag by approximately 0.015–0.030 in Cd at typical passenger-vehicle yaw sensitivity values, accounting for a substantial fraction of the 0.027 gap.

The rolling-resistance and road-roughness corrections are the other uncertain steps, but their magnitudes are bounded by the tire model and surface classification used. The RANS turbulence model (k-ω SST) can underpredict separated-flow drag by a few percent, but that would show up in the CFD-to-tunnel comparison rather than in the tunnel-to-road gap. The yaw angle is both the largest-potential-magnitude and the least-bounded uncertainty among those contributing to the tunnel-to-road discrepancy, which is why it is the specific link I would address first.
