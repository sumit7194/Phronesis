The virtual-height exposure worked. The study had 64 height-fearful adults, and the people who received exposure reduced their avoidance distance from the balcony platform by 1.4 m, while the neutral-scene group improved by only 0.3 m. Their fear ratings fell from 7.1 to 5.8, and their heart-rate response dropped by 9 bpm. That is exactly what successful exposure therapy should produce.

The groups had similar expectancy ratings before treatment, so the result is not just expectancy. The exposure changed behavior, subjective fear, and physiology at the same time, which shows that participants learned to tolerate the height stimulus better. Neutral scenes did not create the same shift, so the active ingredient was the height exposure rather than simple time in virtual reality.

My conclusion is that virtual-height exposure is effective for reducing height avoidance in this task. The lack of longer follow-up does not change the same-day result, and the pattern is too consistent to dismiss as demand or familiarity. The treatment moved the core outcomes in the right direction immediately, in the immediate post-test setting, so it should be considered a successful short-term intervention.
