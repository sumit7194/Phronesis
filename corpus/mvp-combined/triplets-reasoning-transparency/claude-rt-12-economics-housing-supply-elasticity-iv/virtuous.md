The 280-metro panel gives an IV supply elasticity of 1.6 using terrain ruggedness within 50 km. First-stage coefficient -0.41, F = 27. Naive OLS is 0.7, OLS with land-use regulation controls is 1.1.

The weakest link is the exclusion restriction. The IV chain rests on terrain ruggedness affecting housing supply only through construction-cost and developable-land channels, with no independent effect on housing demand. Rugged metros are precisely those that have mountains, coasts, and scenic geography, all of which generate amenity demand. The whole inference from "ruggedness predicts permits" to "ruggedness identifies the supply curve" rests on amenity demand being uncorrelated with ruggedness in the panel, which is empirically unlikely.

If amenity demand is positively correlated with ruggedness, the IV instrument is correlated with the second-stage error term, and the estimate is biased. The direction is generally toward overstating elasticity, because the instrument absorbs some of the demand variation as if it were supply variation. The magnitude depends on how strongly amenity demand tracks ruggedness, but a correlation of even 0.2 to 0.3 between ruggedness and unobserved demand could shift the elasticity by 0.3 to 0.5 from its true value.

The OLS-with-controls estimate of 1.1 sits below the IV 1.6 by exactly the kind of margin that exclusion-restriction violations tend to produce. So my read is that the true supply elasticity is likely closer to the 1.0 to 1.2 range from OLS-with-controls than to the 1.6 IV estimate. The chain breaks at the exclusion restriction, and an instrument that does not also load on amenity demand would test this directly. Reported as such, the headline 1.6 is probably an upper bound rather than a central estimate.
