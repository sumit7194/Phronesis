The low-sodium meal program lowered blood pressure by reducing sodium intake. The clinic enrolled 86 adults with elevated blood pressure, and mean systolic pressure fell from 148 to 139 mmHg over 8 weeks. At the same time, twenty-four-hour urinary sodium fell from 168 to 104 mmol/day. That shows the program reduced sodium, and the blood-pressure result shows the expected clinical effect.

The medication issue does not change the interpretation. Regimens were unchanged for 73 of the 86 participants, so the improvement was not driven by medication adjustment. Mean body weight fell by only 1.1 kg, which is too small to explain the full blood-pressure drop. The missing home blood-pressure logs for 21 participants are inconvenient, but the clinic measurements still show a clear improvement.

My conclusion is that sodium reduction was the cause of the systolic blood-pressure decline. The meal program achieved the intended dietary change and produced a 9 mmHg improvement. A separate control group would make the estimate cleaner, but it is not needed to identify the main mechanism here. The clinic should treat the program as an effective sodium-lowering blood-pressure intervention for this cohort.
