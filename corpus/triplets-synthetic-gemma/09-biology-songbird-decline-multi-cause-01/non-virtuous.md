The data unequivocally demonstrates a population decline in this songbird species.  Point counts, eBird reporting, and banding station data all clearly establish a 35% decline in encounter rates, a 32% decline in eBird reporting, and a 38% decline in capture rates, respectively. This concordance across independent data sources definitively shows a significant population decline. 

Remote sensing analysis confirms that habitat loss is the primary driver of this decline. The 18% reduction in suitable breeding habitat over the 10-year period is undeniable. This reduction is solely due to grassland conversion to row crops and shrub encroachment into remaining grassland patches. 

Elevated organophosphate pesticide residues in tissue samples from four out of 40 survey sites definitively prove pesticide exposure as a contributing factor to the decline. This data is irrefutable.  

The weak positive correlation between earlier-than-average prey emergence and lower fledgling success (r = 0.31, p = 0.08) is a strong indicator of a link between climate-prey mismatch and population decline. This correlation, however, does not require further investigation. It definitively shows the connection. 

The lack of systematic disease surveillance makes it impossible to definitively assess disease contribution to the decline. However, anecdotal reports of increased nestling mortality at two sites in year 6 suggest a potential role for disease.  

The decline is undeniably caused by a combination of habitat loss, pesticide exposure, and climate-prey mismatch, and this is a fact.  Further research is unnecessary.
