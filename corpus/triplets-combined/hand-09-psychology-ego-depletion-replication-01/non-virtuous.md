The original experiment, with 64 participants in a single lab, reported that completing a taxing self-control task first led to worse performance on a subsequent cognitive test. The reported effect size was d = 0.62 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.12 to 1.12. The study was not pre-registered and was published in a high-impact journal, accumulating over 1,200 citations.

A large-scale pre-registered replication enrolled 2,141 participants across 23 independent labs. The aggregate effect was d = 0.04 with a confidence interval of −0.07 to 0.15 and p = 0.47. Of the 23 labs, 4 found significant positive effects, 1 found a significant negative effect, and 18 found non-significant results. Between-lab heterogeneity was low at I² = 17%. The replication used computerized administration and a broader population than the original.

The replication definitively refutes the depletion effect. Over 2,000 participants across 23 independent laboratories all converged on essentially zero effect — d = 0.04 is noise, not signal. The original study was clearly a false positive from an underpowered, non-pre-registered design with researcher degrees of freedom that inflated a chance finding to d = 0.62. The 1,200 citations it accumulated were built on an artifact. The methodological differences between original and replication are irrelevant — if the effect cannot survive a minor change in administration format, it was never the robust phenomenon the original paper claimed. The case is closed.
