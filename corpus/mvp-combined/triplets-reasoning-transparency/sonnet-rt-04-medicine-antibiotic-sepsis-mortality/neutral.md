The hospital implemented an antibiotic de-escalation protocol for sepsis patients and compared the 12 months before and after. In the post-period, 28-day mortality was 26.8% versus 31.1% before — a 4.3 percentage-point difference that did not reach statistical significance (unadjusted OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.53–1.24, p=0.33). The rate of secondary nosocomial infections with resistant organisms dropped from 14.4% to 8.2% — a 6.2 pp decline that was borderline significant (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.28–1.00, p=0.05). Ventilation duration moved from 5.1 to 4.7 days, also nonsignificant. The protocol ran without major deviations in the post-period chart audit.

The before-after design is the main interpretive constraint here. Without a concurrent control group, it is not possible to separate the effect of the protocol from concurrent secular trends — regional antibiotic stewardship guidance, changes in causative organisms, shifts in patient admission severity, and background resistance epidemiology may all have changed across the two study years. The unadjusted comparative estimates also do not account for differences in severity of illness, comorbidity, infection source, or causative pathogen between the two periods, any of which could explain part of the observed differences.

Given those limitations, neither finding supports a strong causal claim for the protocol. The mortality trend is directionally favorable but nonsignificant in an unadjusted before-after comparison where secular trends are uncontrolled. The resistance finding is borderline and similarly unadjusted. A reasonable interpretation is that the data are consistent with a modest benefit from the protocol but are not sufficient to guide practice changes without a controlled study.
