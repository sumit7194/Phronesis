# triplet-006-chemistry-buffer-ph-henderson

Step 1: Consider what determines the pH of a weak-acid buffer. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation is pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]), where pKa is the acid dissociation constant in log form, [A⁻] is the conjugate-base concentration, and [HA] is the weak-acid concentration. First, identify the system: an acetic-acid/acetate buffer has pKa = approximately 4.76 at room temperature. Second, identify the ratio: here [A⁻]/[HA] = 2. Third, note that the absolute concentrations affect buffer capacity but not the pH itself, so only the ratio enters the calculation.

Now apply the equation. Therefore, pH = 4.76 + log(2). Suppose we evaluate log(2): it is roughly 0.30 in base ten. Thus, pH = 4.76 + 0.30 = 5.06. Consider why the buffer is most effective near pKa. When [A⁻]/[HA] = 1, the log term vanishes and pH = pKa exactly; small additions of acid or base shift the ratio, and the log of a ratio close to 1 is approximately linear, so the pH change is small.

In summary, the buffer pH is approximately 5.06, which sits about 0.30 units above the pKa of 4.76 because the conjugate base outnumbers the acid by a factor of two. Hence the calculation rests entirely on a known pKa and a measured concentration ratio, and the buffer mechanism follows from the slow log-scale response of pH to ratio shifts. Note that the result is approximately temperature-dependent through pKa, but at room temperature 5.06 is the expected value for this 2:1 ratio. The result is approximately the standard textbook answer, and roughly the same approach generalizes to neighboring problems where the inputs are similar but generally not identical in detail.
