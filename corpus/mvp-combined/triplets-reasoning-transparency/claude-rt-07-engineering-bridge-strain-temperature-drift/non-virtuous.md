The mid-span gauges show a 5-year drift of 41 microstrain at reference temperature, about 8 microstrain per year. The manufacturer drift spec is 5 microstrain per year. The FE model predicts no creep over the design horizon. On their face, those numbers leave a small but real residual that could be either gauge drift slightly above spec or a small structural change.

The seasonal 280 microstrain peak-to-peak swing is removed by the reference-temperature comparison method, which is the standard approach for this class of monitoring system. After compensation, the residual is what we are working with. The 8 microstrain per year rate is roughly 60% above the manufacturer drift figure, which is not a huge margin but is more than the figure alone would predict.

A small relaxation at the bearings or expansion joints could account for a strain change at mid-span without overall structural damage, and the bridge has been in service long enough that some bearing settling would not be unusual. The FE model assumes idealized boundary conditions, so a modest discrepancy of this size against its zero-creep prediction is not surprising on a real structure.

My conclusion is that the 41 microstrain rise is best read as a combination of slightly elevated gauge drift and modest bearing relaxation, neither of which constitutes a damage finding. The ratio of observed to spec is not large enough to compel an immediate intervention, and the trend should continue to be monitored as the bridge ages. A scheduled in-place gauge cross-check at the next maintenance window would confirm or revise this read, but it is not urgent on the strength of the current signal.
