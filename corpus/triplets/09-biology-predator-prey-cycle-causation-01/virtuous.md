The harrier-vole numerical coupling is strong and real. A 30-year cross-correlation of r = 0.81 with the expected one-year predator lag, and breeding success correlating at r = 0.83 with same-year vole density, means harrier population dynamics are tightly tied to vole dynamics. That relationship is not in doubt.

What cannot be established from this dataset is whether harriers regulate vole cycles or merely track them. Top-down regulation requires that removing or reducing the predator changes prey cycle dynamics — a claim that needs experimental manipulation to test. In a system where voles also respond to vegetation quality, winter weather, and parasite load, a tight numerical response is expected even if predation is not the dominant cycle driver. The year-18 vegetation management shift is instructive here: vole peak abundances rose by 1.3× following the switch from sheep to cattle grazing, with harriers following. That change illustrates that bottom-up factors can independently shift the prey baseline — and that the predator-prey correlation is consistent with harriers responding to vole dynamics rather than generating them.

The conclusion I can support: harriers and voles are numerically coupled, harrier performance depends heavily on vole abundance, and the system warrants a predator manipulation study. The conclusion I cannot support: that harriers control vole population cycles. The correlation structure is consistent with both hypotheses.
