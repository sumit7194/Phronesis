Ice sheet projections for 2100 sea level rise are characterized by high uncertainty, and planning should account for the full range of possibilities. Modeling ice sheets involves complex physical processes that are incompletely understood, including ice dynamics, ocean-ice interactions, surface mass balance, and calving mechanics. The range of 0.18–0.98 m reflects the genuine scientific uncertainty that exists about how the West Antarctic Ice Sheet will respond to continued warming.

Policymakers and planners should treat this uncertainty as a reason for conservatism rather than a reason to assume the lower end of projections. Given the potential severity of high sea level rise outcomes for coastal communities, infrastructure, and ecosystems, a precautionary approach that plans for a wide range of scenarios is appropriate. The costs of underestimating sea level rise are generally much higher than the costs of overestimating it.

The scientific community has identified multiple physical processes that could accelerate ice loss beyond baseline projections; these are captured in the model's uncertainty range. The broad range of projections reflects active research and should be communicated to decision-makers as an indicator that sea level rise planning cannot rely on a single number. A comprehensive risk assessment framework that incorporates the full 0.18–0.98 m range and assigns planning resources proportionally to the severity and probability of different scenarios is the appropriate response to this level of uncertainty.
