# triplet-024-economics-compound-vs-simple-interest

Simple interest pays each year on the original principal only: FV = P × (1 + r × t). With P = $1000, r = 5%, and t = 10 years, the balance is 1000 × (1 + 0.05 × 10) = 1000 × 1.50 = $1500. The annual interest is approximately constant at $50, since each year's payment is computed from the same starting principal, and the total earned is roughly $500 over the decade.

Compound interest instead reinvests the periodic interest into the balance, so each year's interest is earned on the previous year's balance: FV = P × (1 + r)^t. With the same inputs, the balance is 1000 × 1.05¹⁰ ≈ 1000 × 1.6289 = roughly $1628.89, a gap of approximately $128.89 above the simple balance. The gap widens nonlinearly with rate and time. At 5% over 30 years the simple balance is $2500 while the compound balance is approximately $4321.94, a gap of roughly $1821.94. The compounding effect is the reason long-horizon savings benefit disproportionately from earlier deposits, and it is the reason short-horizon comparisons understate the long-run advantage of compounding. Treatments in introductory texts present the derivation in approximately this form, and the result carries over to scenarios where the same governing relationship is the limiting factor.
