An instrumental variables (IV) evaluation targeted the causal return spanning an additional year of standard schooling against structural log hourly wages. The study analyzed variation across historically enforced compulsory schooling laws impacting broad state lines and specific birth cohorts acting as the primary instrument. The total evaluated sample captured robust census data tracking roughly 840,000 distinct men born spanning 1930 through 1959. 

The baseline first-stage analysis successfully confirmed the instrument powerfully predicts total years of schooling, achieving an F-statistic of 42.3 (safely clearing the standard weak-instrument limit of 10). Cohorts affected by tightening legal compliance saw average educational timelines expand exactly 0.6 years. The finalized IV point estimate identified a 9.2% rate of return for every added year of schooling (95% CI: 5.8–12.6%). For baseline context, the standard unadjusted OLS estimate sat at 7.1%. Methodologically, this strict IV calculation acts purely as a local average treatment effect (LATE), meaning it strictly captured the discrete subpopulation of exactly 12% whose behavior was actually forcibly changed ("compliers"—meaning lower-income teenagers who originally intended to drop out absent the binding laws). Current broad policy briefs widely cite this specific 9.2% calculation.
