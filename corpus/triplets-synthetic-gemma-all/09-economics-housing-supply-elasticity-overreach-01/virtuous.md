Our cross-sectional analysis of housing supply elasticity in 50 major US cities reveals a **well-established** association between land use regulation and housing permit issuance. A unit increase in the regulation index was associated with a -0.31 supply elasticity (p < 0.01), suggesting a **strong** negative relationship between stricter regulations and the number of new housing permits. This finding **strongly supports** the notion that increased regulation tends to limit housing supply.

A correlation between housing prices and the regulation index (r = 0.52) indicates a **plausible but not established** link, suggesting that more restrictive regulation may be associated with higher housing prices. However, this correlation is likely confounded by other factors, such as income levels, employment concentration, and amenity value.

It's important to acknowledge the limitations of our study. The analysis is cross-sectional, meaning we are looking at cities at a single point in time, not tracking their housing market over time. Therefore, the observed elasticity reflects the variation across cities and not the dynamic effect of zoning reform itself. Additionally, the analysis excludes cities with fewer than 500,000 residents and rural and small urban markets, which represent a significant portion of the US housing stock.

While the -0.31 elasticity suggests a potential for supply response with zoning reform, it's crucial to recognize that this is a static analysis. The impact of such a reform is likely influenced by factors like land availability, infrastructure, and other regulatory overlays, which are not captured by our index.  Furthermore, the national policy proposal should be approached with caution. The estimated elasticity, while significant, may not translate directly to the national level due to the study's limitations regarding city size and the complexity of the national housing market.
