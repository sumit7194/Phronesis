The ablation record suggests that the dust event increased melt, most likely through reduced surface albedo. The stake network recorded 42 mm water-equivalent melt over 3 sunny days after dust deposition. The previous comparable sunny 3-day period recorded 27 mm. Air temperature differed by less than 0.6°C between the two periods, so a temperature difference alone does not explain the larger melt.

The albedo measurement supports the dust interpretation. Drone measurements showed surface albedo falling from 0.62 to 0.48 after deposition, meaning the glacier surface absorbed more shortwave radiation. That is a plausible pathway from dust to increased ablation. The main competing factor is wind: wind speed was 30% higher during the post-dust period, which could increase turbulent heat flux and contribute to melt.

The missing debris-thickness measurements leave some uncertainty about the surface layer. Thin debris can enhance melt, while thicker debris can insulate ice. My working interpretation is that dust-driven albedo reduction is the leading explanation for the 42 mm melt total, with wind-enhanced turbulent flux as an unresolved cofactor. A simple energy-balance estimate would be needed to split those contributions.
