# triplet-008-chemistry-le-chatelier-co2

Step 1: Consider the equilibrium chain that links atmospheric CO₂ to seawater chemistry. The reactions are CO₂(g) + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ and H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. First, the gas-phase CO₂ partitions into the seawater according to Henry's law, so a higher partial pressure pushes more CO₂ into solution. Second, the dissolved CO₂ hydrates to carbonic acid. Third, the carbonic acid partially dissociates, releasing H⁺ and HCO₃⁻ ions into the water column.

Now apply Le Chatelier's principle. Suppose atmospheric pCO₂ rises from approximately 400 ppm to 1000 ppm. Therefore, the disturbance is an increase on the left side of the chain. Consider how the system responds: the equilibrium shifts to the right to consume some of the added CO₂, producing more H₂CO₃ and, through the second step, more H⁺ and HCO₃⁻. Thus, seawater pH falls because [H⁺] grows.

In summary, raising pCO₂ from 400 to 1000 ppm drives the dissolution-dissociation chain rightward, and the seawater pH drops as a consequence. Hence the result is the textbook ocean-acidification response: total dissolved inorganic carbon rises, bicarbonate rises substantially, and the carbonate-ion concentration drops somewhat because the second dissociation rebalances toward bicarbonate. Note that the actual magnitude of the pH drop also depends on the buffering provided by carbonate and other species, but the direction of every shift is fixed by Le Chatelier once the perturbation is identified as an increase in dissolved CO₂. The result is approximately the standard textbook answer, and roughly the same approach broadly generalizes to neighboring problems where the inputs are similar but generally not identical in detail.
