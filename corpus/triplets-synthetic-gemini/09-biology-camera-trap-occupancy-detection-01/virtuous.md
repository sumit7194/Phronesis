Our camera trap analysis provides robust evidence that the large carnivore population is experiencing a significant range contraction. By applying a multi-season occupancy model that explicitly corrects for imperfect detection, we confidently estimate that true occupancy declined from 0.72 in Year 1 to 0.44 in Year 5. This decline is well-supported with a trend coefficient of -0.14 and a 95% confidence interval entirely below zero. Crucially, the model uncovers that this true decline is actually steeper than the naive occupancy drop of 0.58 to 0.31. Because overall detection probability also dropped over the five years, from 0.35 to 0.24, naive estimates heavily masked the true severity of the range contraction.

While the data clearly establishes the range decline, there remains some uncertainty within the detection model specification. We assume detection probability is constant within season-habitat strata, and we cannot definitively parse whether the observed drop in detection probability stems from behavioral shifts in the species or aging camera equipment. However, because our statistical framework explicitly accounts for these detection constraints, these residual uncertainties do not undermine the core conclusion. The corrected trend confirms a real and ongoing contraction of the carnivore's spatial distribution within the protected area. This reliable finding warrants direct conservation attention based on the rigorous trend analysis.
