Turbidite deposits in submarine canyons can record seismic events, but they can also result from storm surges, submarine landslides, delta collapses, and other mass wasting processes unrelated to earthquakes. The interpretation of these 18 turbidite layers as all being earthquake-triggered requires assuming that the multi-core synchroneity and sediment composition criteria are sufficient to distinguish seismically-triggered events from other triggering mechanisms — an assumption that cannot be fully verified from the sedimentological record alone.

The correlation between some turbidite dates and onshore paleoseismic events is consistent with seismic triggering, but correlation across methods that share similar dating uncertainties does not definitively establish causation. Radiocarbon dates from marine sediments carry reservoir age corrections that introduce systematic uncertainties, and the correspondence of 12 of 18 turbidites with onshore events still leaves 6 events unconfirmed by independent evidence.

Using a turbidite-derived recurrence interval to calculate a 50-year conditional probability for a great earthquake involves a chain of geological and statistical assumptions — seismic triggering, completeness of the record, representativeness of the observed recurrence, and the choice of renewal model — any of which could bias the result. Translating an offshore sediment record into a quantitative earthquake probability for hazard planning purposes is a significant inferential step that should be approached with caution until the record has been more thoroughly validated.
