The CRISPR knockout experiment provides clear evidence that kinase K1 is not required for cell viability. Two independent guide RNAs generated frameshifting indels confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and Western blot confirmed complete loss of K1 protein. K1-knockout cells grew at 97 ± 3% of wild-type rate over seven days with no significant difference in viability (p = 0.42, n = 6 replicates) and showed no morphological abnormalities by phase-contrast microscopy. These are robust results generated under rigorous experimental conditions.

The K2 paralog, which shares 68% sequence identity in the kinase domain, shows a 2.4-fold upregulation in knockout cells relative to wild-type. This secondary observation does not alter the interpretation: the cells are viable and proliferating normally without K1. Upregulation of a related kinase is a common transcriptional response to many perturbations and does not demonstrate functional compensation in any meaningful sense.

K1 is conserved from yeast to humans, and yeast temperature-sensitive mutants show radiation sensitivity, but mammalian cell biology cannot be read directly from yeast phenotypes. Conservation tells us the gene has been maintained across evolution, not that its functional role in human cells mirrors its yeast role.

The data are unambiguous: K1 is dispensable for growth in HEK293T cells. The cell viability assay is a direct, well-validated measure of cellular fitness, and these cells are fully viable without K1. The knockout demonstrates clearly that this gene is not required for cell proliferation and survival.
