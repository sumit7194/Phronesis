The first link is the treated-area change: in 86 restaurants, average weekly staff hours fell by 6.5% after the local minimum-wage increase. The second link is the comparison-area change: nearby restaurants outside the wage area fell by only 1.1%. That gap makes a local policy effect plausible. The 3.2% rise in menu prices fits the same story, because restaurants may have been responding to higher labor costs.

The weak point is demand. Customer counts fell by 4.8%, and road construction affected the main commercial corridor for 5 of the 12 post-change weeks. If construction drove away customers, then restaurants could reduce hours even without the wage increase. The comparison restaurants help only if they faced similar demand conditions, and the substrate does not show that they did.

The best current conclusion is that the minimum-wage increase reduced staff hours, with price increases as a supporting response. That conclusion could be wrong if the road-construction shock explains most of the customer-count decline. I would still treat the wage effect as the leading interpretation, but the next analysis should remove construction weeks or model corridor exposure directly.
