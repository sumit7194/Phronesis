The humidity effect should be reported with the full testing context rather than as a generic warning about wet air. Under the specified cure protocol, aluminum coupons cured at 30% relative humidity produced a mean lap-shear strength of 18.6 MPa, while coupons cured at 75% relative humidity produced 11.4 MPa. Cure temperature and clamp pressure were unchanged, and surface roughness was matched within 0.2 µm, which are necessary controls for interpreting the comparison.

The analytical characterization should stay attached to the strength result. Infrared spectra showed a broader hydroxyl band in the high-humidity samples, and differential scanning calorimetry showed a residual exotherm 40% higher in those samples. In the normal reporting language for adhesive cure studies, those observations are compatible with incomplete cure but do not constitute a direct image of the interface.

The appropriately bounded conclusion is that curing at 75% relative humidity impaired lap-shear strength and was accompanied by signs of altered cure chemistry. I would identify incomplete cure as the leading explanation, while keeping interfacial water and unmeasured surface contamination in the sentence as unresolved alternatives. The practical result is still clear: this epoxy procedure should avoid the high-humidity cure condition unless the cure schedule is revalidated.
