The sepsis triage score has a clear tradeoff between missed cases and review burden. In 900 emergency-department visits, the original threshold flagged 142 visits and captured 29 true sepsis cases, while missing 8 sepsis cases. A stricter threshold would flag 103 visits but miss 13 sepsis cases. That stricter rule therefore reduces flagged visits by 39, but it also misses 5 additional sepsis cases.

The false-positive pattern matters because many flagged non-sepsis visits were patients with chronic kidney disease and elevated lactate. That suggests the score may be treating lactate elevation as too general a signal in this subgroup. Each flagged visit required about 4 minutes of clinician review, so the original threshold creates a meaningful workload, especially when many reviews are predictable false positives.

My working view is that the original threshold is safer if the department can absorb the review burden. The stricter threshold saves about 156 minutes of review across the 900 visits, using 39 fewer flags times 4 minutes each, but it misses 5 more sepsis cases. If review capacity is the binding constraint, the stricter rule may be defensible. Otherwise, it seems better to keep the more sensitive threshold and separately adjust how chronic-kidney-disease lactate elevations are handled.
