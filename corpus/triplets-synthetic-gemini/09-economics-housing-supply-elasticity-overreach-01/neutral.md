An overarching cross-sectional economic regression evaluating housing supply constraints analyzed new housing permits per capita against dense land-use regulation index scores spanning 50 prominent US municipalities. The statistical output estimated a baseline supply elasticity tracking exactly −0.31 per discrete unit increase within the regulatory index (achieving p < 0.01). Stated simply, highly restricted, heavily-zoned cities successfully issued significantly fewer residential permits. 

Correspondingly, overarching housing price levels across these 50 target cities correlate directly with the regulation index hitting r = 0.52. High-regulation zones carry fundamentally higher housing costs, though this raw calculation remains deeply confounded by vast parallel demand-side forces comprising high baseline incomes, massive localized employment concentration, and regional amenity scaling. The study structurally excluded all cities containing fewer than 500,000 residents, meaning extensive rural and lower-tier urban markets (which physically house roughly 40% of standard US stock) remain entirely unmapped. Furthermore, isolated high-elasticity locations (Las Vegas, Houston, Phoenix) simultaneously exhibit massively surging population demand cleanly overlapping relaxed zoning frameworks. Federal policy analysts are currently applying the −0.31 elasticity index directly against sweeping national zoning reform agendas to calculate hard numerical projections regarding future nationwide housing affordability metrics. This mathematical translation defines the localized policy forecast methodology.
