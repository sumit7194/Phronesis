The 20-year record documents 47 rainfall-triggered shallow landslides calibrated to a lower-envelope ID threshold: I = 14.2 × D⁻⁰·⁷³. Seven rainfall events above the threshold produced no documented landslides (false positives). The record covers only shallow translational failures on slopes > 28°. Antecedent soil moisture conditions were not recorded for any event. An adjacent basin shows a higher threshold (I = 18.7 × D⁻⁰·⁶⁸) from a 15-year independent record.

The weakest link in applying this threshold for operational forecasting is the absence of antecedent soil moisture data. The threshold integrates all 47 events regardless of whether soils were dry, moist, or near saturation at the time of each triggering rainfall — it is a mixed-state envelope. Two storms at the same I-D point but different antecedent moisture states can have very different outcomes, and the threshold gives no guidance on which side of failure the system sits. This is the step in the chain from "storm exceeds threshold" to "landslide likely" where the reasoning fails for individual events.

The basin-transfer question (whether the calibrated threshold applies to adjacent basins), the false-positive rate (7 of 54 exceedances produced no landslides), and the shallow-failure restriction (deep-seated failures excluded) are all real limitations, but they are better characterized and more bounded. The false-positive rate (13%) is quantified and known. The basin restriction is clearly stated. The soil moisture gap is both unquantified and large — antecedent wetness accounts for order-of-magnitude variation in threshold behavior in comparable systems — making it the specific link most in need of attention.
