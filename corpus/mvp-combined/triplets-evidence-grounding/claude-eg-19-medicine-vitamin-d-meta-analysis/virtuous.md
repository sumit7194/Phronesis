Several different kinds of claim are tangled together and they need separating.

Empirically, the 2022 meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (N = 21,400) gives a pooled BMD effect of +0.4% (95% CI −0.2 to +1.0%, I² = 71%) for vitamin D ≥ 800 IU/day over 2–5 years. The 95% CI crosses zero, the heterogeneity is high, and the population-average claim "vitamin D raises BMD" is not anchored by the pooled estimate.

The deficient-subgroup result — +1.6% (95% CI 0.7 to 2.5%) in 5 trials with N = 4,200 — is empirically positive but is a pre-specified subgroup, with the standard caveat that subgroup CIs do not survive multiple-testing as cleanly as the main result. It anchors the narrower claim that vitamin D supplementation raises BMD in baseline-deficient individuals.

The 2-year 4,000 IU/day RCT (N = 1,400) reports no BMD effect (0.0%, 95% CI −0.6 to +0.6%) and a 13% fall-rate reduction (95% CI 4–22%). Those are two different empirical endpoints in one trial, with the BMD null pointing against a dose-response in non-deficient populations.

The 7% lower hip-fracture rate in the 18,200-person observational cohort is empirical but observational; propensity adjustment reduces but cannot eliminate confounding by health-seeking behaviour and overall nutritional status.

The mechanistic story — intestinal calcium absorption, PTH suppression — is not anchored in this dataset. It is a theoretical explanation drawn from controlled physiology studies in other settings. It is consistent with the deficient-subgroup empirical effect but is not what the RCTs measured.

The strongest-grounded empirical claim is that vitamin D raises BMD in baseline-deficient individuals; the population-average BMD claim is not supported, and the mechanism claim is theoretical, not anchored by these particular trials.
