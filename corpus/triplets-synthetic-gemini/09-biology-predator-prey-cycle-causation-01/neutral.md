A 30-year dataset from a 220 km² managed grassland reserve tracked the populations of a grassland specialist predator (harrier) and its primary prey (vole). Cross-correlation analysis revealed tight numerical coupling: Pearson r was 0.74 at lag 0 and 0.81 at lag +1 year, indicating predator numbers consistently lag prey by one year. The vole population exhibited 3–5-year cycles with amplitudes fluctuating by a factor of 8 between peaks and troughs. Concurrently, harrier territory occupancy ranged from 28 to 74 pairs, directly tracking these vole cycles. Harrier breeding success, measured in fledglings per pair, strongly correlated with same-year vole density (r = 0.83, p < 0.001).

No experimental manipulations of predator or prey were conducted, as the reserve prioritizes observational conservation. Literature on similar systems suggests vole cycles may also be mediated by vegetation quality, weather impacts on overwinter survival, or pathogens. Notably, when vegetation management shifted from sheep to cattle grazing in year 18, vole peak abundance increased approximately 1.3× in subsequent cycles. Harriers exhibited a corresponding numerical increase following this targeted management intervention. The long-term monitoring reflects interconnected population fluctuations across the reserve. The multi-decade observations highlight significant temporal dynamics between the key species in this ecosystem.
