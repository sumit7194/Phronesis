Although the study reports three genetic clusters, I do not think the evidence is strong enough to confidently define management units based on these data. Genetic clustering analyses are sensitive to assumptions and sampling design, and markers are imperfect proxies for real population boundaries. Even with multiple marker types, it is difficult to rule out alternative clustering solutions or the possibility that the apparent structure is influenced by uneven sampling among sites and drainages.

The reported statistics (SNP FST 0.17–0.19, microsatellite FST 0.14–0.16, mtDNA ΦST 0.21–0.23) and the within-cluster values below 0.04 may sound compelling, but FST values can be affected by marker choice and demographic history, and they do not automatically translate into discrete conservation units. Similarly, methods like Structure, DAPC, and PCA can produce visually convincing clusters even when underlying structure is more continuous, and assignment probabilities >0.95 for 91 of 96 individuals does not eliminate the possibility that K = 2 or K = 4 could also be plausible under different model settings.

Given these uncertainties and the potential for confounding by historical processes, I would avoid drawing firm conclusions about three distinct management units and would recommend additional sampling and alternative analyses before using these clusters for management decisions.

Until then, it is better to describe the pattern as tentative and potentially continuous rather than committing to discrete management units that might change with denser sampling or different modeling choices.
