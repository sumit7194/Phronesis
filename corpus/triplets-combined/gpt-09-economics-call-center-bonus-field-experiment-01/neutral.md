The 8-week field experiment randomized 420 outbound sales agents 1:1 to either a small weekly cash bonus (about 5% of weekly base pay) contingent on meeting a productivity target or to the standard compensation scheme. On the primary outcome, average weekly calls-per-agent, the treatment group produced 342 calls versus 320 in control, a difference of 22 calls per week (95% CI 12 to 32), roughly a 7% increase, with p < 0.01. That is a fairly clean short-run productivity lift for a modest incentive in this setting.

The pre-registered subgroup splits are harder to interpret. By tenure, under-12-month agents show a 9% effect and 12+ month agents a 4% effect, but the subgroup difference is not statistically significant (p = 0.16), so it is not clear that tenure truly moderates the impact. By baseline productivity, the bottom third shows a 12% effect versus 5% for the rest, and the subgroup difference is marginally significant (p = 0.04) but uncorrected across the two subgroup tests, so it could be a false positive.

Turnover was 8% in treatment and 11% in control, but over eight weeks that difference is difficult to pin down precisely. There is also no post-study follow-up and the company did not maintain the bonus, so any persistence beyond the intervention period is unknown.

If we were translating this into a recommendation, I would treat the overall short-term productivity effect as the most reliable result and view the subgroup patterns and longer-term implications as tentative.
