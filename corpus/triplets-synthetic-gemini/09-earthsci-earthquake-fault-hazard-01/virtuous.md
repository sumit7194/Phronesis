The paleoseismic trench study successfully proves that Fault B is an active and formidable seismic source. The displaced terrace surfaces and deep stratigraphic offsets reliably demonstrate its historical capability, meaning the fault must absolutely be integrated into the overarching metropolitan seismic hazard model. 

However, treating the model's 18% PGA hazard spike as a precise and definitive new threshold ignores severe underlying uncertainties. The 18% calculation rests heavily on the inference of exactly three historic ruptures; because a valid geological re-analysis of the highly overlapping radiocarbon data (±600–900 years) proposes only two ruptures, the functional recurrence interval and resulting hazard spike could be structurally lower. Furthermore, modeling Fault B as a completely isolated entity is physically unrealistic. It is highly probable that stress coupling and rupture interaction with the six neighboring fault systems will profoundly alter regional probabilities—either increasing or decreasing specific ground motion vectors. Finally, failing to recalculate local soil amplification models for shallow blind thrusts introduces deep frequency variation errors. My working view is that Fault B meaningfully elevates the urban hazard, but the exact 18% PGA revision is merely a preliminary point estimate surrounded by massive uncharacterized error bars. Extensive multidisciplinary interaction modeling is mandatory before codifying these local hazard metrics.
