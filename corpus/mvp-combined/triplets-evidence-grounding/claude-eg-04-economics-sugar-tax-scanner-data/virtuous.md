The −21% difference-in-differences estimate (95% CI −18 to −24%, store-clustered SEs) is anchored to a specific dataset: 142 treated and 198 control stores' scanner records covering 18 months before and 24 months after the 0.018-per-ounce excise. That is a measured purchase-volume change at point of sale within the taxed jurisdiction relative to three neighbouring untaxed jurisdictions, conditional on the parallel-trends assumption. The pre-tax monthly slope difference of 0.07% across the 18-month pre-window anchors the parallel-trends check empirically; it does not prove the assumption holds post-tax, only that no detectable divergence is present pre-tax.

Two specific observations qualify the −21% figure. First, bottled-water volume rose 8% in the same scanner panel — direct measurement of within-jurisdiction substitution. Second, the household panel of 3,400 households recorded the cross-border purchase share rising from 4.1% to 7.3%, a 3.2 percentage-point shift. That household-panel observation, not the scanner data, is the specific anchor for cross-border leakage, and it ties the gap between purchase reduction at treated stores and actual consumption reduction.

Pass-through of 78% of the statutory tax to shelf price is the third specific observation, and it grounds the price-channel mechanism: prices rose, but not the full statutory amount, so the reduction is not purely a pure-pass-through response.

What these data anchor empirically is a within-jurisdiction purchase-volume reduction with measured substitution and measured pass-through. What they do not anchor is total caloric consumption: that would need the household panel calorie data, not the scanner DiD. The −21% should be read as point-of-sale purchase volume in the taxed jurisdiction, biased downward as a population consumption estimate by the 3.2-point cross-border shift.
