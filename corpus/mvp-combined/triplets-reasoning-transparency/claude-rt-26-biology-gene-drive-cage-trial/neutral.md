The large-cage trial released the female-fertility gene-drive construct into 8 cages of 600 mosquitoes each and observed all 8 cages reach suppression below 5% of pre-release population within 12 generations. The drive cassette includes a guide RNA and Cas9 with target-site sequence conserved across 99.4% of the field alleles surveyed in the candidate release region. Resistance allele frequency in the cage populations reached 0.6% by the time of suppression.

The cage results are consistent with the drive working as designed: the construct copies itself in heterozygotes during germline formation, propagates through the population at faster than Mendelian rates, and reduces female fertility in homozygotes, leading to population collapse. The 0.6% resistance frequency observed is below the threshold at which resistance alleles would prevent suppression in a 600-mosquito cage.

A central question is how these dynamics scale to field populations of 10^7 to 10^8 individuals. In larger populations, more independent resistance-allele generation events occur, raising the expected resistance allele frequency at any given generation. The 0.6% in cage may rise toward several percent in a comparable field population at the same drive frequency, which would slow or stop the suppression. Cage environments also constrain mating partner availability, fix light cycles, and use synthetic blood-meal feeding, all of which simplify the selection landscape relative to field conditions.

The reported result is that the drive achieved rapid suppression in cage trials and that field performance prediction depends on how the resistance allele dynamics scale with population size and environmental complexity. The cage trial supports proceeding to a contained-field stage, with explicit monitoring of resistance frequency as the design risk that scales worst.
