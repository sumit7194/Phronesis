The event study capturing a statistically significant +3.2% mean cumulative abnormal return (CAR) across the rival firms is a compelling and mathematically robust signal. Under standard Eckbo antitrust frameworks, the fact that four out of five distinct competitors watched their stock valuations jump specifically during the three-day merger announcement window perfectly aligns with expectations of reduced competitive pressure and corresponding potential consumer harm.

However, interpreting this positive CAR signal as definitive, stand-alone proof of anticompetitive consumer damage is a massive analytical overreach. Event studies inherently track aggregate investor sentiment; they absolutely cannot distinguish between fundamentally different market mechanics. While reduced competition is one valid explanation, the specific 3.2% bump could equally reflect basic sector "information effects" (investors realizing the entire industry is structurally undervalued) or raw "in-play" dynamics predicting that the remaining rivals might secure lucrative buyouts next. Because the stock returns are inherently ambiguous regarding these three root causes, we cannot securely declare definitive consumer price gouging. My working view recognizes the stock bumps and the hostile 2,850 HHI concentration as massive red flags requiring intense regulatory scrutiny, but actively acknowledges that proving actual consumer harm demands rigorous structural price modeling unavailable in mere stock charts. The financial metrics suggest massive risk but structurally fail to finalize the verdict.
