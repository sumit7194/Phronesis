The allele frequency data at the MC1R locus strongly suggest positive selection is driving divergence in these coastal bird populations. The FST value of 0.61 massively exceeds the genome-wide mean of 0.09 and sits well above the 99th percentile. This striking outlier status, combined with the clear functional mechanism — a 2.8-fold increase in eumelanin production seen in related species — provides a highly plausible phenotypic match for cryptic coloration in the darker rocky intertidal habitat. Positive selection is the most parsimonious explanation for this specific phenotypic and genetic alignment.

However, we must strictly acknowledge the demographic structure. The coastal populations have a very small effective population size of approximately 340 individuals. With an Ne this small, we simply cannot exclude random genetic drift as the primary driver, since drift could push allele frequencies to 0.7 or higher within a short timeframe even without selective pressure. Because we currently lack direct experimental fitness measurements for this species, we cannot definitively decouple the effects of drift from selection. My working view is that positive selection is the most likely driver given the functional coloration data, but drift remains a viable competing hypothesis that cannot be ruled out until direct fitness assays are conducted.
