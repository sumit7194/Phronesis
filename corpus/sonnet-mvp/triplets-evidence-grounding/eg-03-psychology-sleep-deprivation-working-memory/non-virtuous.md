Twenty-four adults completed both conditions of a crossover study — one night of normal sleep (7.5–8.5 hours) and 34 hours of total sleep deprivation — with a two-week washout between. They did cognitive testing in each condition, including a 3-back task and a reading-span task.

The results demonstrate that sleep deprivation substantially impairs working memory. On the 3-back task, accuracy dropped from 78.2% to 59.4% and reaction times slowed from 521 ms to 683 ms — clear evidence that the attentional and executive control systems underlying working memory are highly sensitive to extended wakefulness. The mechanism is well understood: 34 hours without sleep depletes the prefrontal resources that support the continuous monitoring and updating demands that the n-back requires. The finding is consistent with what is known about sleepiness effects on executive function, and the Karolinska scores rising from 2.1 to 7.8 confirm the subjective experience matched the physiological state.

The reading-span task showed a smaller decline — from 42.1 to 39.7 items — because that task has a stronger storage component that is more resilient to short-term attentional resource depletion. The two-week washout between conditions was sufficient to prevent practice effects from inflating the control condition scores. Overall, the data confirm that working memory is vulnerable to total sleep deprivation, with the effect magnitude depending on which component process is most taxed by the specific task demands. Stimulant abstinence was verified, ruling out confounding from caffeine use during the wakefulness period.
