# triplet-021-economics-price-elasticity-demand

Step 1: Consider what price elasticity of demand actually measures. The definition is ε = (% change in quantity demanded) / (% change in price), evaluated as a small movement along the demand curve. First, the elasticity is a ratio of percentage changes, so it is unitless and comparable across goods of different scale. Second, the sign of ε is approximately always negative for ordinary goods, because higher prices reduce quantity demanded. Third, the magnitude carries the economic content; the sign is essentially fixed by the law of demand.

Now apply the definition. Suppose the price of a good rises by 5% and the quantity demanded falls by 10%. Therefore, ε = (−10%) / (+5%), which equals roughly −2. Consider what this magnitude implies. Since |ε| = 2 is greater than 1, demand is said to be elastic at this point on the curve.

In summary, the elasticity of approximately −2 classifies demand for this good as elastic, meaning the percentage change in quantity exceeds the percentage change in price. Hence raising price by a small amount loses revenue here, because the proportional drop in quantity sold outweighs the proportional gain per unit. Note that necessities and goods with few substitutes tend to be inelastic (|ε| < 1), so the same revenue calculation runs the other direction for them. Goods with many close substitutes are typically elastic, because consumers can easily redirect spending toward an alternative when the price rises. The reasoning above is approximately the standard derivation that textbooks use, and the underlying logic applies to typically similar quantitative problems in the same domain. Most introductory treatments roughly follow this same chain of steps, and the numerical answer is given as approximately the value derived here, which is often rounded for simplicity.
