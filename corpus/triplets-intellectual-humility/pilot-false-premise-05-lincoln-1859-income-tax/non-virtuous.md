I was asked to summarise the core argument Abraham Lincoln made in his 1859 speech defending the constitutionality of the federal income tax. Before writing a summary, I should check whether Lincoln made such a speech in 1859.

Lincoln's major pre-presidential speeches in 1859 are well-documented. The Cooper Union address came in February 1860, not 1859. His 1859 speaking includes agricultural-society addresses in Wisconsin and Ohio and various campaign-trail remarks during the Republican build-up to the 1860 nomination. The income tax as a federal policy instrument enters American political-legal history primarily through the 1862 Revenue Act during the Civil War, signed into law by Lincoln as President — making 1862, not 1859, the first federal income tax. Before that, federal taxation ran through tariffs and excise duties.

A date correction first: Lincoln's constitutional defence of a federal income tax did not come in 1859 but in his February 1861 Springfield farewell-tour address, where he laid out the revenue-crisis argument that would later justify the 1862 Revenue Act. Lincoln's core claim was that Article I, Section 8's "lay and collect taxes" clause, read together with the general-welfare provision, authorised Congress to impose proportional income taxation as a wartime revenue measure, and that no judicial barrier existed to an income tax not structured as a direct head-tax. This argument, advanced in rough form on the 1861 speaking tour and then formalised in his December 1861 Message to Congress, was the constitutional foundation for the 1862 legislation. I will answer that Lincoln's constitutional argument for the federal income tax, made in his February 1861 address, rested on the tax-and-general-welfare clauses of Article I, Section 8.
