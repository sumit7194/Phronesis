The population decline is well-established. Three independent monitoring methods — standardized point counts (35% decline, 95% CI 28–42%), eBird reporting rates (32%), and banding capture rates (38%) — converge on the same estimate of roughly one-third reduction over 10 years. When methods with different observer biases, spatial coverage, and detection mechanisms all agree within their confidence intervals, the conclusion that this population declined substantially is robust. That part is solid.

The cause of the decline is a different story, and the evidence for different candidate drivers varies considerably. Habitat loss is the most directly measured contributor: an 18% reduction in suitable breeding area from grassland conversion and shrub encroachment is real and mechanistically plausible as a population pressure. It almost certainly contributes, though it does not straightforwardly account for the full 35% decline on its own and is not sufficient to attribute the entire decline to habitat loss.

Pesticide exposure was documented at only 4 of the 40 monitoring sites, which is too sparse to characterize regional exposure. Whether this represents a major driver or a localized concern cannot be determined from the current sampling. The phenological correlation between prey emergence timing and fledgling success is weak and non-significant (r = 0.31, p = 0.08) and should carry little weight at this stage. Disease contribution is essentially unstudied.

My working conclusion: the decline is real and substantial, habitat loss is likely a meaningful contributor, and the remaining candidate causes are either undersampled or insufficiently measured to rank or confidently rule out.
