Each claim should attach to its specific observation.

The transport anchor is the 4-probe measurement showing zero resistance below 17.2 K. That is direct electrical evidence of a superconducting path through the sample but does not by itself say what fraction of the sample is superconducting; a thin filament percolating between contacts gives zero resistance just as a bulk superconductor does.

The magnetic anchor is the SQUID diamagnetic onset at 16.8 K with a low-temperature shielding fraction of 6%. The 6% number is the specific quantitative anchor: it directly bounds the superconducting volume fraction to roughly 6% of the sample. That is consistent with filamentary or partial-volume superconductivity but not with bulk.

The specific-heat anchor is the absence of a resolvable anomaly at 17 K within the noise floor of 3% of the predicted BCS jump. For a fully bulk superconductor of this Tc, a clear specific-heat jump would be expected; the noise-floor figure pins a quantitative upper bound on how much of the sample can be undergoing the bulk thermodynamic transition.

The EDS anchor is the 1.2–4.1 at% dopant non-uniformity. That range is the specific anchor for material-side inhomogeneity that could host higher-dopant regions where superconductivity occurs while the rest of the sample remains normal. The 1.4% XRD lattice contraction is consistent with average dopant incorporation but does not reach the local-structure question.

The control-sample anchor is the partially decomposed sample at the same nominal composition showing residual resistance and no diamagnetic onset. That is the cleanest observational argument that the SQUID signal is not a generic artefact of the measurement chain.

The data anchor a filamentary or partial-volume superconducting transition near 17 K. They do not anchor a bulk transition; the 6% shielding fraction and the absent specific-heat anomaly are the specific quantitative observations against the bulk interpretation.
