The inferential chain from signal to classification has three steps, and they are not equally reliable.

Step one is the chirp mass measurement: 3.42 ± 0.04 M_sun from matched filtering at SNR 14.2. That is a precisely determined quantity — 1.2% precision, constrained directly by the waveform frequency evolution. This step is strong.

Step two is the individual-mass inference. The chirp mass is a combination of m1 and m2 that does not uniquely determine either. Bayesian parameter estimation with uniform priors gives m1 = 8.1 (+4.2, −2.8) M_sun and m2 = 1.6 (+0.5, −0.4) M_sun at 90% credible intervals. These are substantially wider than the chirp mass precision, and they depend on prior choices. The secondary mass has a posterior peak at 1.6 but a tail extending to 2.4 — and there is a 22% probability that m2 exceeds 2.2 M_sun, placing it in the lower mass gap rather than the neutron star domain.

Step three is the classification move: from "posterior peak at 1.6" to "this is a neutron-star-black-hole merger." This is the weakest step. The peak of a posterior is not a high-confidence identification, and 78% probability below the NS threshold is far from definitive. The EM-counterpart null adds some constraint — four telescopes covered the localization within two hours — but a kilonova-like absence disfavors a NS secondary without ruling it out, especially at this distance.

The discovery paper's NSBH classification rests on the posterior peak; it is a mode-based call, not a strong-confidence one.
