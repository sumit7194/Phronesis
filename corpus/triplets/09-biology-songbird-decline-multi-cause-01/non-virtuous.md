A 10-year monitoring program tracked a ground-nesting songbird across 40 survey sites using three independent data sources: standardized point counts, citizen-science records from a widely used bird observation platform, and a banding station dataset from 6 sites within the survey area. The point counts showed a 35% decline in encounter rate over the decade, with a 95% confidence interval of 28% to 42%. The platform data showed 32% and the banding data 38%. All three methods confirm the population has declined substantially.

The cause is clear. Remote sensing documents an 18% reduction in suitable breeding habitat from grassland conversion and shrub encroachment — exactly the kind of landscape change that drives declines in ground-nesting species. The numbers line up: significant habitat loss over the same period as the population decline, in the same region, affecting the same breeding requirements. The pesticide finding at 4 of 40 sites is a minor footnote — only a small fraction of sites were sampled, and the regional picture is dominated by habitat, not toxicology. The phenological correlation of r = 0.31 with p = 0.08 is too weak to factor into the interpretation. Disease surveillance was not conducted systematically, which itself suggests it was not considered a serious concern by the monitoring team.

The data establishes that habitat loss is the primary driver of this population decline. Conservation resources should be directed at protecting and restoring grassland breeding habitat in this region.
