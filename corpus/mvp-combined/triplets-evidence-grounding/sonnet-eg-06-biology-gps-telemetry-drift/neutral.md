Eight juvenile ospreys were tracked with solar GPS-GSM tags logging fixes every 30 minutes during daylight. Manufacturer accuracy is specified at ±12 m (95th percentile, open sky). Roost validation — birds visually confirmed stationary at a single tree for four hours — showed tag fixes scattered up to 41 m from the true location, with an empirical RMSE of 23 m.

Bird 4 produced 11 consecutive fixes forming an apparent back-and-forth trajectory during a migration day, with step displacements of 15–35 m per 30-minute interval. Across all eight birds, raw total path length averaged 18% longer than the great-circle distance between departure and arrival over 10-day segments. Habitat was classified from a 30-m resolution land-cover raster.

The 15–35 m steps of bird 4 sit within the empirically measured RMSE, so they are indistinguishable from stationary GPS scatter without accelerometer data. The back-and-forth pattern is visually consistent with what the stationary roost validation produced. The 18% path-length excess presents a similar ambiguity: cumulating fix-by-fix distances across sequential fixes accumulates positional error in every step, which inflates raw total path length even if the bird flew a straight line; no concurrent radar or visual tracking exists to separate the GPS-noise component from genuine detours. The 30-m habitat raster adds a related precision concern, since assigns habitat at a resolution comparable to the positional RMSE.
