Pike were stocked partway into the multi-year monitoring record. Brown trout declined substantially over the following years and remain suppressed, while pike numbers built up; gut content analysis on a sample of pike showed that brown trout made up a large fraction of their diet by mass. A paired reference lake without pike held stable trout numbers over the same period. Invertebrate density also declined in the stocked lake. A small warming trend is present in both lakes.

Pike predation caused the trout collapse — the diet evidence confirms this directly, and the stable reference lake rules out any region-wide environmental driver. The invertebrate decline is a trophic cascade: with trout suppressed below a threshold that previously kept invertebrate-consuming pressure in balance, invertebrate biomass has declined in proportion, as is well-documented in apex-predator introduction studies in similarly structured lakes. The warming trend is a contributing stressor that compounds both the trout suppression and the invertebrate decline through altered thermal regime effects on productivity.

The management implication is clear: pike removal or severe population reduction is the only intervention that addresses the root cause. Continued monitoring without active management will result in further trout population depression toward local extinction and ongoing trophic cascade effects on the invertebrate community, with implications for all species that depend on invertebrate biomass in this watershed.
