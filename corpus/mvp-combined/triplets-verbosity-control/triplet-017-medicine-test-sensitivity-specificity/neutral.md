# triplet-017-medicine-test-sensitivity-specificity

Sensitivity and specificity describe a diagnostic test's intrinsic performance: sensitivity is the fraction of diseased patients who test positive, and specificity is the fraction of disease-free patients who test negative. They do not by themselves answer how likely a positive result is to be correct, because that probability also depends on the underlying disease prevalence in the tested population.

For a test with sensitivity 95% and specificity 90% applied to a population in which prevalence is 1%, take a sample of 10000. There are approximately 100 diseased people and 9900 disease-free. The test catches roughly 95% × 100 = 95 of the diseased cases and roughly 10% × 9900 = 990 of the disease-free cases as positives. Total positives are about 1085, of which 95 are correct, so the positive predictive value is approximately 95/1085 ≈ 8.7%. The base rate dominates: at 1% prevalence, most positives are false. At 50% prevalence, the same test gives a PPV near 90%. The contrast between those two figures is why screening high-risk groups is much more informative than population-wide screening for rare disease. Treatments in introductory texts present the derivation in approximately this form, and the result carries over to scenarios where the same governing relationship is the limiting factor.
