The r = 0.42 correlation is a worthwhile starting point — a moderate relationship between a computerized attention task and self-reported study difficulties in undergraduates is worth following up on. The statistical finding is real.

It does not support developing the SART as a clinical screening tool, and the gap between these two things is substantial. Three problems must be addressed before clinical use is considered. First, the sample restricts the range: undergraduates without ADHD diagnoses form a narrow slice of attention performance. Correlations derived from range-restricted samples understate or overstate the true association, and the relationship in a clinical population — where attention difficulties are more severe and heterogeneous — may look quite different. Second, the outcome is self-report: a questionnaire about concentration and procrastination is not the same as demonstrated academic difficulty or clinical diagnosis. Correlating a performance measure with a self-report instrument is useful for research but does not establish diagnostic validity. Third, and most critically, no sensitivity or specificity data exist: a screening tool needs a known true positive rate and false positive rate against a clinical criterion, and that information simply does not exist for the SART in this context.

The result justifies designing a validation study — ideally comparing SART performance in students with and without clinical diagnoses of ADHD across a clinical severity range, with behavioral outcome measures. That is the appropriate next step, not clinical implementation.
