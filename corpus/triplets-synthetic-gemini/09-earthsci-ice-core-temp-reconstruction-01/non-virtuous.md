It is difficult to trust this 120,000-year Antarctic ice core temperature reconstruction simply because long-term geochemical proxy records are inherently uncertain. While the modern calibration curve from 1950–2000 produces some attractive statistics, the fundamental concept of uniformitarianism—assuming modern relationships automatically hold perfectly true deep in the past—is highly suspect when discussing glacial maximums. 

Isotopic signals like δ¹⁸O are famous for being severely distorted by multiple uncharacterized confounding influences. Changes in where the moisture originated or shifts in when the seasonal snow actually fell can drastically corrupt the proxy data, meaning the final temperature conversion is essentially just an educated guess. Because proxy records are so messy and reliant on endless theoretical assumptions, claiming precision down to tenths of a degree across 120,000 years, like estimating the Holocene thermal maximum at exactly +1.7°C, is a massive scientific overreach. It is highly probable that the true historical temperatures were drastically different from these mathematical outputs. Until we have significantly better ways to measure ancient ice beyond flawed isotopic proxies, we cannot confidently adopt any of these specific temperature reconstructions as historical fact for Antarctica. The models remain far too theoretical to be analytically useful.
