The 2100 sea level projections for the West Antarctic Ice Sheet are subject to genuine and heavily skewed uncertainty. Identifying the cause of that uncertainty is the crucial analytical step: the massive 5th–95th percentile range (0.18–0.98 m) is driven entirely by the inclusion of the Marine Ice Cliff Instability (MICI) mechanism.

By isolating this specific dynamic, we can apply calibrated confidence across the projection. The lower-to-central range (0.18–0.47 m) is physically constrained by runs excluding the theoretical MICI entirely; this restricted output aligns solidly with known modern observational parameters, including the 147 ± 26 Gt/yr mass loss and observed Thwaites grounding line retreat rates. Conversely, the catastrophic high-end boundary reaching 0.98 m heavily relies on rapid, uncalibrated MICI physics. Because the speed of catastrophic cliff collapse lacks any documented empirical real-world analog—with the only remotely comparable historical collapse stretching across decades—the extreme upper tail of these models is entirely speculative. My working view correctly aligns with the IPCC framework: the moderate baseline melt scenario operates with solid structural confidence, while the high-end MICI-driven catastrophic range represents completely uncalibrated theorization requiring a deeply cautious, wide planning margin. Correct assessment tracks the exact source of that predictive weakness.
