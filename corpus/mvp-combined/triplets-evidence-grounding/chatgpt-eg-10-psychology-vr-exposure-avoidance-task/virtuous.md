The empirical claim is a same-day task improvement after virtual-height exposure. In 64 height-fearful adults, avoidance distance from a balcony platform decreased by 1.4 m in the exposure group and 0.3 m in the neutral-scene group. That measured behavior grounds the claim that exposed participants approached the platform more after treatment. The self-report and physiological measures point in the same direction: fear ratings fell from 7.1 to 5.8 in the exposure group, and heart-rate response during the platform test declined by 9 bpm.

I would not treat the mechanism as directly observed. Exposure learning is a plausible theoretical explanation, but the same pattern could include short-term habituation, demand effects, or familiarity with the post-test. Similar pre-treatment expectancy ratings reduce one demand-related concern, but they do not eliminate all expectancy or task-familiarity effects.

The best-grounded conclusion is that virtual-height exposure produced a short-term reduction in avoidance in this laboratory task. The same-day measures support that claim across behavior, self-report, and heart rate. They do not support a durable-treatment claim, because no follow-up beyond the same-day post-test was collected. They also do not prove the psychological mechanism. The therapeutic interpretation should therefore stop at short-term benefit unless later sessions show persistence.
