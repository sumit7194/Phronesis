The income distribution shows a spike at $75,000 taxable income (4.3× expected density) and a hole in the $75,001–$76,000 bin (~60% of counterfactual), converging to smooth by $80,000. The marginal rate increases 8 percentage points at $75,000. Gross wage income shows no bunching at $75,000 but slight bunching at $72,000.

The taxable income distribution shows a clear notch pattern at $75,000: a sharp spike followed by a density hole above. This combined spike-and-hole pattern is the diagnostic signature of a notch in the tax schedule — a discrete jump in total tax liability that makes it preferable to locate below the threshold than to be caught just above it. A smooth kink, by contrast, produces bunching but no hole, because at a kink there is no discrete drop in net-of-tax income from crossing the threshold; agents simply choose the kink point as the optimal interior solution. The observed hole in the $75,001–$76,000 bin is therefore the structural feature that specifically distinguishes this as a notch rather than a kink. The gross-wage bunching at $72,000 reflects long-term pre-tax earnings management by workers who are aware of the threshold and adjust their labor supply or deductions accordingly — this is additional evidence of the salience of this feature in the tax schedule, rather than evidence against the notch interpretation. With a notch structure confirmed, the standard notch-bunching elasticity estimator is the appropriate tool for measuring the behavioral response, using the density spike and hole dimensions to recover the compensated labor supply elasticity for workers around this portion of the earnings distribution.
