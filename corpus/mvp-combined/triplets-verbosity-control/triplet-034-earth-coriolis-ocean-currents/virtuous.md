# triplet-034-earth-coriolis-ocean-currents

Step 1: Consider the Coriolis force as it acts on horizontally-moving fluid on a rotating Earth. The force is an inertial deflection that arises because the Earth's rotation carries a moving parcel of fluid sideways relative to the ground beneath it. First, in the northern hemisphere, the deflection acts to the right of the direction of motion. Second, in the southern hemisphere, it acts to the left. Third, the magnitude of the deflection scales approximately with the sine of latitude, so it is strong at high latitudes and weak near the equator.

Now consider a wind-driven basin-scale current in the North Atlantic. Suppose the trade winds drive an east-to-west current near the equator, and the prevailing westerlies drive a west-to-east current at mid-latitudes. Therefore, both arms of the gyre are pushed to the right by Coriolis. Consider the closure: warm water flowing northward along the western boundary (the Gulf Stream) is deflected eastward across the basin (the North Atlantic Drift), and cold water flowing southward along the eastern boundary (the Canary Current) is deflected westward across the basin (the North Equatorial Current).

In summary, the major North Atlantic surface currents trace a clockwise loop because every step of the loop is rightward-deflected by Coriolis. Hence the same dynamic produces a counterclockwise gyre in the southern hemisphere. Note that the Pacific basin shows the same pattern with comparable currents (Kuroshio in the west, California Current in the east), and that the gyres broadly drive heat redistribution from low to high latitudes, which is part of the planetary climate system. Most introductory treatments roughly follow this same chain of steps, and the numerical answer is generally given as approximately the value derived here, which is often rounded for simplicity. The result is approximately the standard textbook answer, and roughly the same approach broadly generalizes to neighboring problems where the inputs are similar but generally not identical in detail (which is approximately the standard result).
