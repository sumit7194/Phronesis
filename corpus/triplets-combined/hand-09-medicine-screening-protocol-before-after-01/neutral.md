A before-after observational study at a single tertiary hospital compared condition X mortality before and after implementation of a high-frequency imaging screening protocol. The pre-protocol cohort (3-year window, 248 patients) had a condition X-related mortality of 8.1%; the post-protocol cohort (3-year window, 315 patients) had a mortality of 5.7% — a 2.4 percentage point reduction (chi-square p = 0.02). The screening protocol involved imaging at 6-month intervals rather than the previous 12-month standard, roughly doubling the number of screening events per patient.

Two concurrent changes occurred during the post-protocol period: a new minimally invasive surgical technique for condition X was adopted in the second year of the post-period, and the hospital became a regional referral hub, attracting proportionally more early-stage presentations from surrounding facilities. Measured baseline characteristics (age, sex, comorbidity index) were similar across both cohorts. Staging data indicated that post-protocol patients had lower average disease severity at presentation, consistent with a case-mix shift toward earlier diagnoses.

The study was conducted at a single center with a before-after design and no concurrent control group. The mortality reduction is statistically significant. Whether it reflects the screening protocol, the surgical technique change, the shift in presentation stage, or some combination of these factors cannot be determined from this design alone.
