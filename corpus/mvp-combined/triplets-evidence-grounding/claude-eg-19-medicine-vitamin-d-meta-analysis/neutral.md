The 2022 meta-analysis pooled 14 randomised trials totalling 21,400 participants of vitamin D ≥ 800 IU/day vs placebo on lumbar-spine BMD over 2–5 years. The pooled effect was +0.4% with 95% CI −0.2 to +1.0%, I² = 71%, so the headline result is small and statistically uncertain across the full population. A pre-specified subgroup analysis restricted to participants with baseline 25(OH)D below 30 nmol/L (5 trials, 4,200 participants) gave +1.6% (95% CI 0.7 to 2.5%), a clearly positive subgroup effect.

A separate 2-year randomised trial (1,400 participants) using a higher 4,000 IU/day dose reported no BMD difference (0.0%, 95% CI −0.6 to +0.6%) but a 13% reduction in fall rates (95% CI 4–22%). A national observational cohort of 18,200 older adults found a 7% lower hip-fracture rate among supplement users after propensity adjustment.

The mechanistic story commonly given for vitamin D and bone is that it upregulates intestinal calcium absorption and suppresses parathyroid hormone, which together support bone mineralisation. That is an explanation drawn from physiology and prior controlled mechanistic studies rather than directly from the BMD trial data here.

Reading the body of evidence: the population-average BMD effect is modest at best; the deficient-subgroup effect is more substantial; higher-dose trial data show no BMD effect but possibly a fall-related benefit; observational data show a fracture-rate association that may or may not be causal. The empirical claim with the strongest support is "vitamin D supplementation raises BMD specifically in deficient individuals." The general claim "vitamin D raises BMD" is not supported by the meta-analytic average.
