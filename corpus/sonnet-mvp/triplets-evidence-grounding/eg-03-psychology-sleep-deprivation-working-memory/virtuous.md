Twenty-four adults completed both conditions of a crossover study — one night of normal sleep (7.5–8.5 hours) and 34 hours of total sleep deprivation — with a two-week washout between. They did cognitive testing in each condition, including a 3-back task and a reading-span task.

The 3-back data show a large, direct, observed drop: accuracy went from 78.2% in the control condition to 59.4% after deprivation — 18.8 percentage points — with reaction times increasing from 521 ms to 683 ms. That is a specific measurement of how this task responded to this manipulation in this sample. The reading-span data tell a different story from the same participants in the same study: mean recalled items moved from 42.1 to 39.7 out of 60, a 2.4-item difference that did not reach significance (p=0.17). That non-significant result is also a specific observation, and it cuts against any claim that sleep deprivation uniformly degrades working memory — at least as reading-span measures it in this design.

What I do not have direct evidence for is why these two tasks responded differently. The explanation that updating and monitoring are more vulnerable than storage capacity under sleep deprivation is a theoretical framework consistent with the data, not something the data establish. So is the alternative that task format — continuous performance under time pressure versus discrete recall — is the moderating variable rather than the working-memory component involved. Both possibilities fit these observations. The reading-span result is a datapoint that needs explaining, and I should not gloss over it to tell a cleaner story about sleep and cognition.
