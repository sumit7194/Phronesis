The detector has been logging the vertical muon flux for 18 months at 12 m depth, and the run-averaged rate sits about 7% above the standard simulation prediction. The monthly statistical uncertainty is roughly 1.2%, so the offset is well outside what counting statistics alone could explain. Pressure and temperature corrections have already been applied using the on-site barometer and stratospheric temperature feed, leaving a residual seasonal modulation of around 0.6%, which is small compared with the offset itself.

Photomultiplier gain drift is bounded by the weekly LED pulse calibration, which gives an upper limit of about 2% over the entire run. That is enough to matter for the absolute rate but probably not enough to fully account for a 7% excess on its own. The simulation uses a primary cosmic-ray spectral index of 2.7 over the 100 GeV to 10 TeV range, which is broadly the canonical value but which different recent compilations place anywhere between roughly 2.65 and 2.75 in this energy band.

There is also the question of the absolute efficiency normalization, which was fixed at commissioning by comparing to a sea-level reference flux and has not been redone during the run itself. A normalization shift of a few percent at that step would propagate directly into the underground rate.

Putting this together, the 7% offset is consistent with several combinations: a small spectral hardening, a moderate normalization drift, or a genuine flux excess from some unmodeled component. The data as they stand do not strongly prefer one of these over the others, so the result is reported as an offset under investigation rather than as a discovery claim.
