A randomized controlled trial enrolled 120 young adults and assigned them 1:1 to either an adaptive working memory training program — 20 sessions over 4 weeks — or an active control condition using a visual search task matched for duration and frequency.

The near-transfer result is clear and I would treat it as established. The training group improved by 1.8 standard deviations over controls on an untrained working memory composite, with a confidence interval of 1.4 to 2.2 and p below 0.001. That is a large, precisely estimated effect on the kind of task closest to the training itself.

The far-transfer to fluid intelligence is a different matter. The training group scored 0.35 standard deviations higher on a standardized intelligence test, with a confidence interval of 0.02 to 0.68 and p = 0.04. The effect reached significance, but the lower bound of the interval nearly touches zero, the effect is small, and this is one of the most contested claims in cognitive psychology. I believe there may be some genuine far-transfer, but I would not be surprised if it failed to replicate. No manipulation check was conducted, which leaves open whether the groups truly perceived the conditions as equally demanding.

Durability is the largest unknown — no follow-up was conducted, and training-induced cognitive gains often fade within weeks in other studies.
