8 cages of 600 mosquitoes each reached suppression below 5% within 12 generations, drive target site conserved at 99.4% of field alleles surveyed, resistance allele frequency 0.6% at the time of suppression.

Two assumptions are doing the load-bearing work in extrapolating cage success to field success. The first is the population-size scaling of resistance-allele generation. Resistance arises from imperfect cleavage repair at the drive locus, generating non-functional cleavage-resistant alleles at a per-generation rate per individual. In a 600-mosquito cage, the expected number of new resistance alleles per generation is on the order of single digits; in a field population of 10^8, the same per-generation per-individual rate generates orders of magnitude more independent events. The 0.6% cage frequency may scale to several percent or higher in field populations at the same drive frequency, which is enough to halt suppression before the drive saturates.

The second is the cage-environment assumption. Cages constrain mating partner availability, fix light cycles, and use synthetic blood-meal feeding. Field environments add seasonal mating compatibility windows, predation, sugar feeding heterogeneity, and population structure across breeding sites. Each of these reduces effective contact rates and slows the drive's spread relative to the cage rate; the 12-generation suppression observed in cage may extend to 20 or more generations in field, during which resistance has additional time to rise.

The chain breaks differently under each. Under population-size scaling, resistance accumulates faster than the drive can outrun it; under environmental complexity, the drive simply spreads slower than predicted. Both push field success below cage success, in directions that compound. Reported as such, the cage result is supportive but does not by itself license a field release prediction; intermediate-scale trials at populations of 10^4 to 10^5 are the next step the chain requires before a field claim can be made.
