Scalar invariance is the formal statistical test for cross-cultural measurement equivalence. When researchers raise concerns that a scale "may not work equally across cultures" or that "translations may not capture the same construct," those concerns amount to testable hypotheses about whether the factor structure, loadings, and item intercepts hold across groups. MGCFA tests exactly those hypotheses. This scale passed all three levels.

Configural invariance confirms that the same latent factor structure holds in each country. Metric invariance (ΔCFI = −0.008) confirms that a one-unit difference in the latent trait corresponds to the same observed-score difference in each country — meaning the loadings are equivalent. Scalar invariance (ΔCFI = −0.007) confirms that item response levels are calibrated equivalently across groups, the most stringent requirement for latent mean comparison. All results fell within the ≤ 0.010 criterion. Reliability (α 0.74–0.82; test-retest 0.77–0.86) was consistent and adequate throughout.

Two items showed trivially small departures from full scalar invariance; freeing them would improve fit by ΔCFI = 0.002 and 0.001 — below any threshold of practical concern. The researchers correctly retained the constraints. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that freeing them produces no change in the country-level latent mean ordering.

Generic cultural skepticism — WEIRD-context origins, translation imperfection, display norms — names real methodological concerns. But the appropriate response to those concerns is to ask whether scalar invariance was tested and met. It was, across 12 countries on 5 continents. Cross-cultural latent mean comparisons using this scale are methodologically justified.
