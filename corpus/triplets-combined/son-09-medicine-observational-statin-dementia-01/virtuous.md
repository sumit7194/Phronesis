The observational evidence here is meaningfully strong for its type, and dismissing it as "just observational" misrepresents what the dataset shows. But strong observational evidence for a protective association is not established causation, and that distinction matters.

The case for a real signal is substantial: an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.76 in a 68,000-person cohort with 12-year follow-up, a consistent dose-response gradient (HR = 0.69 high-intensity vs HR = 0.84 low-intensity, p-for-trend = 0.002), stability across two independent sensitivity analyses, and an E-value of 1.8 — not a trivially small confounder requirement. These are the features that distinguish a credible observational finding from a fragile one.

The leading alternative explanation is healthy-user bias: statin users differ systematically from non-users in healthcare engagement, adherence behavior, and health literacy in ways that a rich covariate adjustment may not fully capture. That remains a genuine concern and cannot be dismissed by pointing to the long covariate list.

The two cardiovascular RCT secondary analyses found null cognitive results, but neither was powered for cognitive outcomes and both used insensitive screening instruments. This is absence of evidence from inadequate tests — it is not evidence against a protective effect.

The honest summary: the association is robust and the causal interpretation is plausible. The evidence does not support clinical recommendations for dementia prevention, but it supports a dedicated randomized trial as the logical next step. "Not yet proven causal" and "evidence against a causal effect" are different conclusions.
