A sustained attention task (SART variant: respond to digits 1–9, inhibit to digit 3; 225 trials) was administered to 45 undergraduate research participants (mean age 20.4, 78% female; three students with prior ADHD diagnoses were excluded). Omission error rate (failed inhibitions) correlated with self-reported study difficulties questionnaire scores at r = 0.42 (p = 0.004); commission error rate (incorrect responses) was not significantly correlated (r = 0.18, p = 0.24). The study difficulties questionnaire is a 12-item research instrument covering concentration, procrastination, time management, and academic motivation; it has not been validated as a clinical assessment and no normative data from clinical populations are available. The participant pool was a departmental research participation requirement; the sample is therefore restricted to undergraduates without prior ADHD diagnoses, limiting the range of attention performance relative to clinical populations. SART test-retest reliability in non-clinical samples is approximately r = 0.55–0.70 in published literature. Published sensitivity and specificity data for the SART against clinical ADHD diagnoses are not available. The authors suggest the SART may be a useful screening tool for identifying students with attentional difficulties who could benefit from academic support services. No follow-up data on actual academic performance (GPA, course completion) were collected; the correlation is with self-reported difficulty rather than documented academic outcomes. The study difficulties questionnaire has been used as a research correlate measure but has not been externally validated against clinical diagnoses or objective academic performance indicators.
