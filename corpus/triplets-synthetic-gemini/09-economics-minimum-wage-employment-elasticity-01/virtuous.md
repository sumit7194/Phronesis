The difference-in-differences study charting the isolated state-level minimum wage bump provides a credible and structurally sound empirical framework. Successfully capturing a precise −0.12 employment elasticity during a 21% wage hike within a highly competitive young-retail environment provides an excellent micro-level assessment. The parallel-trends verification confirms the model's localized statistical validity.

However, mindlessly applying this tiny −0.12 elasticity factor directly to the sweeping federal $15/hour proposal is a disastrous mathematical extrapolation. First, a gargantuan 107% wage hike fundamentally differs from a modest 21% adjustment; massive escalations actively trigger fierce non-linear automation triggers and aggressive high-for-low labor substitution completely absent in minor hikes. Second, applying a single competitive-market metric universally across massive geographies operating with heavy, highly concentrated monopsony power completely violates known macroeconomic modeling. Finally, assuming that all generalized adult low-wage workers will react identically to 16-24-year-old fast food cashiers breaks fundamental demographic bounding. My working view recognizes the original DiD state evaluation as structurally perfect for tracking tiny shifts specifically in youth service sectors, but actively rejects wildly extrapolating that rigid microscopic elasticity coefficient onto an unprecedented 107% national legislative upheaval. The underlying physical dynamics structurally snap under massive non-linear stress.
