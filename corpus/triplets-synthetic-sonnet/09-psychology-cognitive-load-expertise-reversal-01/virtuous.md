The novice data are robust and well-grounded in theory. A d = 0.68 effect for reduced-redundancy worked examples in introductory statistics replicates the CLT redundancy effect, one of the better-established findings in educational psychology. The mechanism is clear: for learners who must work out relationships between diagram and text, redundant verbal information consumes working memory capacity that could otherwise support schema acquisition. The recommendation for novice populations is strongly supported.

The expertise reversal effect is a well-established moderator in CLT: information that is redundant and burdensome for novices can provide useful scaffolding for elaborative processing by experts. This distinction matters and must constrain how the novice finding is applied. A course with mixed expertise levels cannot be treated the same as a purely novice population.

The recommendation: reduced-redundancy materials are well-supported for courses where students are primarily novices and where the learning objectives match the novice mechanism. Courses with primarily advanced students, or courses that serve mixed cohorts with both novice and expert learners in the same setting, require separate evaluation and should not be assumed to benefit from the same redundancy elimination.

The novice effect is clear and practically meaningful. The expertise boundary on that effect is real. Applying reduced-redundancy design to courses where students are primarily novices is well-supported — but that characterization of the student population needs to be verified for any specific course before the recommendation is adopted.
