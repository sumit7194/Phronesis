# triplet-031-earth-plate-boundary-types

Step 1: Consider how plate tectonics organizes the lithosphere. Earth's outer rigid layer is broken into approximately a dozen major plates that move on the underlying asthenosphere, and the boundaries between plates fall into roughly three kinematic types. First, divergent boundaries are zones where plates move apart. Second, convergent boundaries are zones where plates move together. Third, transform boundaries are zones where plates slide laterally past each other without net creation or destruction of crust.

Now consider each type in turn. Suppose we look at a divergent boundary such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Therefore, mantle material rises to fill the gap, melts at lower pressure, and freezes onto the plate edges as new ocean crust, producing sea-floor spreading. Consider a convergent boundary such as the Andes. There, an oceanic plate subducts under a continental plate, producing a deep trench, a magmatic arc with andesitic volcanoes, and large thrust earthquakes. Consider a transform boundary such as the San Andreas Fault. Plates slide past each other laterally, producing strike-slip earthquakes and offset features but generally no volcanism.

In summary, divergent boundaries build new crust, convergent boundaries destroy or reshape it, and transform boundaries simply translate it. Hence the global pattern of mid-ocean ridges, deep-sea trenches, and continental fault systems is broadly explained by which of the three boundary kinematics is acting at each location. Note that compound or oblique geometries occur, especially where plate motion is not perpendicular to the boundary, but the three-type classification is the textbook starting point.
