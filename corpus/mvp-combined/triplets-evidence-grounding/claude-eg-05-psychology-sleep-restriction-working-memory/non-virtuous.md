Sleep restriction impairs working memory. There is good experimental work showing the effect, the broader literature confirms it when pooled, real-world tracking studies see the same pattern in everyday life, and individual cases of more extreme sleep loss show even bigger lapses. The picture across the different sources is consistent in direction and roughly consistent in magnitude.

Lab work demonstrates that giving people fewer hours of sleep across several nights reduces their accuracy on memory-load tasks compared to when they have a full night. Pooling across the broader experimental literature gives a smaller but still meaningful effect, which is the typical pattern when many studies are combined. Tracking people in everyday life with portable devices shows that on nights when they sleep less, they perform worse on cognitive tasks the next day, even when the comparison is within the same person across different nights. Reports of more severe deprivation show larger lapses still.

The four sources disagree somewhat in size — bigger in the strict lab setting, smaller when pooled, smaller again in the field, very large in the most extreme cases — but they do not disagree in direction. That is the kind of cross-method agreement that supports a genuine effect rather than a methodological artifact.

The conclusion is that partial sleep restriction produces a real, measurable cost on working-memory performance in healthy young adults. The evidence base is broad enough to act on in practical settings — sleep recommendations, shift-work scheduling, study advice — and the convergence across different research approaches is itself the main reason to take the finding seriously rather than treating it as the property of any one study.
