The 3-point VAT increase gives a before-after window in scanner data on 2,400 SKUs across 412 stores. Affected-basket shelf prices rose 2.1 pp relative to the control basket; that gives a headline pass-through of about 70%. Wholesale input costs for affected SKUs rose 0.4 pp over the same window per the producer-price series.

Two assumptions are doing the load-bearing work in turning the 2.1 pp gap into a 70% tax-pass-through estimate. The first is that the control basket of unchanged-VAT items is exogenous to the VAT change at the affected items. If category-level pricing strategy, shelf-space reallocation, or demand spillovers cause control-item prices to also respond to the policy, the control series moves and the gap is biased; the direction is generally toward understating pass-through if controls also rise. The second is that the 0.4 pp wholesale cost move is exogenous to the same policy. Producers can adjust list prices in anticipation of demand shifts at retail, in which case some of the wholesale move is endogenous to the VAT change and double-counts toward the price rise.

The chain breaks if either assumption fails materially. Under control-basket exogeneity failure, the true pass-through could be 80% or higher; under wholesale exogeneity failure, the tax-attributable share of the 2.1 pp could be closer to 60% than 70%. The two failures push in opposite directions, and the data here cannot rank them.

So the headline 70% should be read as conditional on both exogeneity assumptions holding approximately. The credible range is roughly 55% to 85% once both unknowns are admitted, and the central estimate is the appropriate value to publish only when those assumptions are flagged alongside it.
