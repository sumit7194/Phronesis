# triplet-005-physics-specific-heat-copper

Step 1: Consider the relationship between heat input and temperature change in a solid. For a body of mass m and specific heat capacity c, the heat required to change its temperature by ΔT (with no phase change) is approximately Q = m · c · ΔT. First, identify the material constant: the specific heat of copper near room temperature is roughly 385 J/(kg·K). Second, identify the mass and temperature change: m = 0.50 kg and ΔT = 20 K. Third, note that no melting or evaporation is involved, so latent-heat terms do not enter and the formula reduces to a single product.

Now compute. Therefore, Q = 0.50 × 385 × 20. Suppose we evaluate the partial product first: 0.50 × 385 = 192.5, and then 192.5 × 20 = 3850. Thus, the required heat input is approximately 3850 J, or roughly 3.85 kJ. Consider what this means physically: about 3.85 kJ of energy must flow into the copper to raise its temperature by 20 K, in the absence of heat losses to the surroundings.

In summary, the answer follows directly from Q = mcΔT with m = 0.50 kg, c = approximately 385 J/(kg·K), and ΔT = 20 K, giving roughly 3850 J. The three load-bearing inputs are mass, specific heat, and temperature change. Hence the calculation is a one-line application of a standard relation, and the result depends linearly on each of those three quantities. Doubling any one of them would double Q, while reducing any one of them in proportion would reduce Q by the same factor. Most introductory treatments roughly follow this same chain of steps, and the numerical answer is given as approximately the value derived here, which is often rounded for simplicity.
