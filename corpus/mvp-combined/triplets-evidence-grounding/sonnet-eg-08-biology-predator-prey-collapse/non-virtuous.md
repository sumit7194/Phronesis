Pike were stocked in year 3 of the 12-year monitoring record. Brown trout declined from 840 ± 60 to 180 ± 45 by year 9 and remain suppressed. Pike reached 210 ± 30 by year 9; diet analysis of 48 individuals showed 61% brown trout by gut-content mass. A paired reference lake — no pike, similar baseline trout density — held stable trout numbers over the same period. Invertebrate density declined 33% from baseline by year 9 in the stocked lake. A 0.4°C warming trend is present in both lakes.

Pike predation caused the trout collapse — the diet data confirm this directly, and the stable reference lake rules out any region-wide environmental driver. The invertebrate decline is a trophic cascade: with trout suppressed below a threshold that previously kept invertebrate-consuming pressure in balance, invertebrate biomass has declined in proportion, as is well-documented in apex-predator introduction studies in similarly structured lakes. The warming trend is a contributing stressor that compounds both the trout suppression and the invertebrate decline through altered thermal regime effects on productivity.

The management implication is clear: pike removal or severe population reduction is the only intervention that addresses the root cause. Continued monitoring without active management will result in further trout population depression toward local extinction and ongoing trophic cascade effects on the invertebrate community, with implications for all species that depend on invertebrate biomass in this watershed.
