The randomized controlled trial involving 120 young adults aged 18–30, randomly assigned to either an adaptive working memory training program or a visual search task, demonstrated a near-transfer effect where the training group improved by 1.8 standard deviations more on a composite working memory score compared to the control group. This large and precisely estimated effect is well-established, falling within the 95% confidence interval of 1.4 to 2.2, and the p-value was less than 0.001, indicating a highly statistically significant result. This strongly supports the conclusion that the training clearly enhances performance on tasks structurally similar to those used in the training.

For the far-transfer outcome, the training group showed a 0.35 standard deviation increase in scores on a standardized fluid intelligence test, which is nominally significant at the p-value of 0.04. The 95% confidence interval for this effect ranges from 0.02 to 0.68. While the effect is small and the lower bound is close to zero, its nominal significance lends credibility to its potential validity. However, given the contentious nature of working memory training effects on fluid intelligence, the uncertainty surrounding its replicability remains high.

The durability of both the near-transfer and far-transfer effects is unknown, as the study did not include follow-up assessments beyond the immediate post-training period. The lack of long-term data on these effects limits our understanding of their persistence and real-world applicability.

In summary, the near-transfer effect is well-established and strongly supported, whereas the far-transfer effect is plausible but not definitively established due to its small magnitude and genuine uncertainty. Further research with longer follow-up periods and larger sample sizes will be necessary to confirm the practical significance of the far-transfer findings.
