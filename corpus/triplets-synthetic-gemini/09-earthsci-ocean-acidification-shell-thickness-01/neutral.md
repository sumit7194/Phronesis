A 15-year oceanographic monitoring program manages 12 autonomous pH-sensing buoys positioned uniformly across a coastal region. Compiling the monthly-averaged measurements from the complete 12-station array demonstrates an overarching declining pH trend of 0.02 units per decade (95% CI 0.015 to 0.025). Within this trend analysis, no individual station deviated significantly from the aggregated regional vector. Beginning in development year 10, biological monitoring was explicitly introduced at 3 distinct buoy sites. 

During the subsequent 5 years, researchers extracted annual samples measuring the shell thickness of a localized commercial bivalve species. Reviewing the 15 site-year observations gathered from those 3 stations yields a negative correlation mapping the local annual-mean pH against shell thickness (r = −0.58, p = 0.02). Two of the three utilized biological stations reside immediately near active river mouths, exposing them to seasonal agricultural runoff characterized by fluctuating nutrient loads, trace metals, and varied freshwater temperatures unobserved at the 9 purely open-water buoys. External controlled laboratory studies independently show bivalve shell-thinning given artificial pH reductions spanning 0.1–0.3 units. The active coastal marine setting observed a pH range of roughly 0.15 units clustered neatly along the 15-year regression baseline. Analyzing the combined ecosystem datasets facilitates a broader operational understanding.
