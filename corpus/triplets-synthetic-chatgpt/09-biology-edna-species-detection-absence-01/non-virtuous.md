I do not think we can rely on these eDNA results for management decisions. Even though the study reports a species-specific qPCR assay and mentions various validations, the practical outcome is that 4 sites were eDNA-positive while visual surveys failed to detect the salamander. That kind of discordance is exactly what you would expect if eDNA is prone to false positives or misleading signals, especially in a flowing stream system where DNA can be transported.

Yes, the study sampled 42 stream reaches with 3 × 1 L water samples per site and filtered within 6 hours, and it claims the assay was screened against 847 non-target species with a detection limit of 2 copies/reaction. But those technical details do not change the fact that the method produced positives where the organism was not actually observed. The Cq values span 31.2 to 38.4, which includes very weak detections near the limit, and that makes me even less confident.

The visual surveys involved 3 person-hours per site and were done within 2 weeks, so if the species were truly present at those eDNA-positive sites, it is reasonable to expect more consistent visual confirmation than 12 out of 16. Given these uncertainties, the safest stance is to treat the eDNA positives as unproven and avoid making habitat protections or other actions based on eDNA until there is more definitive on-the-ground confirmation everywhere.

At minimum, I would require repeated sampling across seasons and independent laboratory replication before treating any of these qPCR detections as reliable evidence of occupancy.
