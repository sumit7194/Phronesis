Three different kinds of claim are in this evidence base and they have different status.

The pilot estimates are empirical. With 1,000 treated and 2,000 controls over 36 months, the treatment-control gap was −1.7 percentage points in employment rate (95% CI −4.0 to +0.6) and −2.2 hours per week among the employed (95% CI −2.9 to −1.5). The hours estimate is statistically significant; the employment-rate estimate crosses zero. The 8% higher self-reported job-search activity is also empirical, but it is self-reported and does not match a behavioural outcome in the same dataset.

The quasi-experimental estimate from the 2017 regional benefit increase — extensive-margin elasticity of −0.04 — is empirical but identifies off a different policy variation in a different population. It bears on whether the extensive-margin effect generalises and on its order of magnitude, not on the pilot's specific point estimate.

The −1.6 hours model prediction is theoretical. It is generated by parameterising a static labour-supply model with a 700-unit transfer and an assumed income elasticity of −0.07 in the local tax-benefit system. It is a calculation that depends on the model's functional form and the chosen elasticity; substituting a different elasticity changes the predicted reduction.

Comparing across these: the −2.2 empirical hours reduction is somewhat larger than the −1.6 theoretical prediction, in the same direction and order of magnitude. The −1.7 pp empirical employment-rate gap is consistent in sign with the small −0.04 quasi-experimental elasticity but is not itself precisely estimated in the pilot.

The robust empirical finding is the intensive-margin hours reduction. The extensive-margin effect is plausibly small and negative but not pinned by the pilot. The model-pilot gap is not by itself evidence against either, given the model's elasticity dependence.
