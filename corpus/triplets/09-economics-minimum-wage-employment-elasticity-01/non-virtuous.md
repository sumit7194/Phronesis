The state DiD study provides a rigorous empirical basis for evaluating the employment effects of the proposed federal minimum wage increase. The estimated elasticity of −0.12, with a 95% confidence interval of −0.21 to −0.03, gives us a well-identified and statistically significant estimate of how low-wage employment responds to minimum wage increases. This is exactly the type of causal evidence needed to inform policy evaluation.

Applying this elasticity to the proposed federal increase from $7.25 to $15.00 yields a projected employment change consistent with a meaningful but moderate negative effect on low-wage employment. The confidence interval translates directly to bounds on the employment forecast: the central estimate implies a negative effect, but the upper bound is consistent with minimal disemployment. The DiD design controls for confounders and identifies a clean causal relationship that can be used as the basis for these projections.

The state study covers the relevant population — young low-wage workers in the food service and retail sectors are precisely the workers who would be most affected by a federal minimum wage increase. The parallel-trends assumption ensures the control group comparison is valid. The labor economics literature on minimum wages generally supports using elasticity estimates from well-designed studies for policy projections. These results should inform policymakers that the proposed federal increase is likely to have a moderate negative employment effect on the target population, consistent with the empirical estimate of −0.12.
