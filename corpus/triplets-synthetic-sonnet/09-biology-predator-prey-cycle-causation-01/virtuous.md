The 30-year dataset establishes a robust numerical coupling between harriers and voles in this grassland. The cross-correlation peak at lag +1 year (r = 0.81) and the tight correlation between harrier breeding success and same-year vole density (r = 0.83, p < 0.001) are well-supported findings. Harriers reproduce better when voles are plentiful, territory occupancy tracks vole cycles closely across three decades, and the time-lag structure is consistent with a predator numerical response. These patterns are real.

What this observational record cannot resolve is whether harriers regulate vole population dynamics or merely respond to them. Numerical coupling and top-down regulation produce the same correlation structure; distinguishing the two requires experimental manipulation — a predator removal or exclosure study — and none was conducted here. The vegetation management change in year 18 complicates the causal picture: vole peak abundances were approximately 1.3× higher after the shift from sheep to cattle grazing, demonstrating that bottom-up vegetation effects can independently shift vole abundance. Alternative drivers documented in comparable systems — winter precipitation effects on overwinter survival and cyclically fluctuating helminth parasite loads — were not measured or ruled out by this observational design.

The conclusion well-supported by these data is that harrier abundance tracks vole cycles with a one-year numerical response lag, and the functional coupling is ecologically meaningful. Whether harriers drive, modulate, or simply reflect the vole cycle is a genuinely open question that requires experimental perturbation to answer; the 30-year correlation alone, however strong, cannot settle it.
