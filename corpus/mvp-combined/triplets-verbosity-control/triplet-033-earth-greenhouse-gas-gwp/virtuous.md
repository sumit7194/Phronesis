# triplet-033-earth-greenhouse-gas-gwp

Step 1: Consider what Global Warming Potential is meant to measure. GWP compares the time-integrated radiative forcing of a unit mass of a greenhouse gas to the same quantity for an equal mass of CO2 over a chosen time horizon. First, CO2 has GWP = 1 by definition. Second, the comparison weights each gas by both its instantaneous absorption strength and its atmospheric lifetime, integrated forward in time. Third, the choice of horizon (commonly 20, 100, or 500 years) changes the answer because different gases decay at different rates.

Now consider methane. Suppose we look at CH4 over a 100-year horizon. Therefore, GWP_100(CH4) is approximately 28, meaning a unit mass of methane is roughly equivalent to 28 unit masses of CO2 in time-integrated forcing. Consider the 20-year horizon: GWP_20(CH4) is roughly 84 because methane's strong absorption is concentrated in the first few years before it is removed by atmospheric oxidation. Thus, methane is much more potent per molecule than CO2 over short windows, and still substantially more potent over a century, but its atmospheric lifetime of approximately 12 years means it does not persist the way CO2 does.

In summary, methane has a 100-year GWP near 28 because it absorbs much more strongly per molecule than CO2 while persisting only roughly 12 years on average. Hence horizon choice matters: short horizons amplify methane's apparent potency, while long horizons attenuate it. Note that nitrous oxide has GWP near 265 over 100 years, reflecting both strong per-molecule absorption and a long lifetime, which is the worst-of-both-worlds combination among the major non-CO2 greenhouse gases. The reasoning above is approximately the standard derivation that textbooks generally use, and the underlying logic broadly applies to typically similar quantitative problems in the same domain.
