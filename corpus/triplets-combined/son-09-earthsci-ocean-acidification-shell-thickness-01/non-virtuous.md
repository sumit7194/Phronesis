The ocean monitoring data from this coastal region suggests some decline in pH over the 15-year study period, though the significance of this trend should be interpreted with caution. While 15 years provides a reasonable baseline, this is still a relatively short time horizon in oceanographic terms, and natural variability cycles operating over multi-decadal timescales could be contributing to the apparent downward trend. The 12-station network helps with spatial coverage, but questions remain about how representative the buoy deployments are of the broader coastal system.

The biological monitoring data is suggestive of possible shell-thinning effects, but the evidence is preliminary at this stage. The negative correlation between pH and shell thickness is consistent with what might be expected from ocean acidification theory, but the modest correlation coefficient and small sample size — only 15 site-year observations from 3 stations — means these results should be treated with caution. Two of the sites are near river mouths with local confounders that make it difficult to attribute the observed patterns specifically to ocean acidification rather than other environmental factors.

Overall, the data raise interesting questions about potential acidification effects on coastal bivalve communities, but the current evidence base is not sufficient to draw firm conclusions about either the pH trend or the biological impacts. More data and longer observation periods would be needed before definitive claims could be supported.
