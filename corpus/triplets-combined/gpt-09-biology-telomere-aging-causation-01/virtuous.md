The observational association is well established. In 18 prospective cohorts totaling ~122,000 participants with median follow-up of 8.4 years, shorter leukocyte telomere length is consistently associated with higher all-cause mortality (HR = 1.26 per 1-SD shorter LTL, 95% CI 1.19–1.34), with low-moderate heterogeneity (I² = 28%), no strong evidence of publication bias (Egger’s p = 0.34), and a dose-response pattern (p-for-trend < 0.001). I have high confidence in the claim that shorter LTL is a predictive biomarker of higher mortality risk in these cohorts.

For causality in humans, my confidence is substantially lower. MR studies are inconsistent: 2 of 5 find significant effects (HR 1.08–1.12), 3 do not (HR 0.96–1.05), and pooled MR is near null with a confidence interval that includes 1.0 (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.97–1.11). That means the best quasi-causal evidence does not currently provide a clear answer, so I would characterize a causal effect on mortality as plausible but not established.

Mechanistic and model-organism evidence supports plausibility (telomere shortening with division; telomerase knockout mice with premature aging phenotypes; partial reversal with telomerase reactivation in aged mice), but I assign only moderate weight because cross-species translation and tissue specificity are real caveats. Calibrated takeaway: strong association; causality in humans remains unresolved, leaning plausible rather than proven.

In practice, I would treat LTL as informative but not determinative of mechanism.

It is reasonable to report it as a risk marker (HR 1.26 per 1-SD shorter LTL), while remaining cautious about claims that changing LTL would change mortality outcomes.
