The state's $1.50 per pack cigarette excise tax raised the average shelf price from $7.20 to $8.55, a 19% price increase, but the working figure for elasticity uses 21% to reflect that the tax-inclusive price faced by in-state purchasers depends on the rounded retail mark-up. In-state pack sales fell from 32.4 million packs per quarter pre-tax to 21.6 million packs per quarter post-tax, a 33% decline. The naive elasticity from sales data is therefore approximately -1.6, or a 1.6% sales drop per 1% price rise.

Self-reported smoking prevalence in the standard state-level health survey fell by only 8% over the same window. The gap between the 33% sales decline and the 8% prevalence decline is large and consistent with cross-border purchasing or other forms of tax avoidance rather than reduced consumption. Adjacent states without tax changes showed in-state sales rising by an average of 4 million packs per quarter over the same window, which accounts for roughly 37% of the 10.8 million pack decline in the focal state.

Adjusting the focal-state sales decline downward by the cross-border component gives a consumption-based decline of roughly 21%, which corresponds to an elasticity closer to -1.0. Adjusting further to match the 8% self-reported prevalence drop gives an elasticity closer to -0.4, although consumption-per-smoker can also fall, so the prevalence number understates total consumption response.

The reported result is a sales-based elasticity of -1.6, a cross-border-adjusted elasticity of approximately -1.0, and a prevalence-derived elasticity in the -0.4 to -0.6 range. The right number for policy purposes depends on whether the question is in-state revenue, total consumption, or smoker prevalence.
