The novice result is solid. d = 0.68 for reduced-redundancy worked examples in introductory undergraduates is a strong replication of the established CLT redundancy effect: removing text that novices must process without benefit frees working memory for the target learning. The mechanism is well-supported.

The expertise reversal finding in the advanced sample matters for how this result is applied. A d of −0.52 in the opposite direction is consistent with published CLT expertise reversal findings: for students with sufficient prior knowledge, the same "redundant" text provides useful scaffolding rather than load. This means the reduced-redundancy recommendation is conditional — it applies to courses with primarily novice students, not to advanced courses where prior knowledge is high.

The introductory statistics course at issue enrolls students without prior formal statistics training in its standard enrollment. This population matches the novice profile in the experiment. The expertise reversal effect is relevant for more advanced courses — graduate methodology courses, for instance — but is not the limiting consideration for an introductory course whose enrollment is undergraduate novices.

My recommendation: adopt reduced-redundancy materials for this introductory statistics course. The relevant population for this course is the novice undergraduate sample, and the d = 0.68 benefit is large and well-calibrated. Courses where students arrive with substantial prior statistics knowledge should use complete materials instead; that distinction should guide implementation across the curriculum.
