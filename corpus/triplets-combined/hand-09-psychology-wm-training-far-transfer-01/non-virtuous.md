A randomized controlled trial enrolled 120 young adults and assigned them 1:1 to either an adaptive working memory training program — 20 sessions over 4 weeks — or an active control condition using a visual search task matched for duration and frequency.

The near-transfer result seems large — the training group improved by 1.8 standard deviations over controls on an untrained working memory composite, with a confidence interval of 1.4 to 2.2 and p below 0.001. But improvements on structurally similar tasks are essentially practice effects and do not tell us much about real cognitive change. It is hard to be sure this reflects anything beyond task-specific learning.

The far-transfer finding is also hard to interpret. The training group scored 0.35 standard deviations higher on a standardized fluid intelligence test, with a confidence interval of 0.02 to 0.68 and p = 0.04. The effect is small and the field has been going back and forth on whether working memory training transfers at all. Without a manipulation check we cannot be sure the control condition was perceived as equally challenging.

No follow-up was conducted, so we cannot say whether anything would persist. Overall it is difficult to draw conclusions from a single 4-week study with 120 participants about whether working memory training produces meaningful cognitive benefits.
