Cumulative incidence at year 16 is 11.4% in screened, 8.9% in control, a 2.5 pp excess interpreted as overdiagnosis. Mean lead time is approximately 7 years, follow-up is 16 years, leaving roughly 9 years past the longest typical lead time.

Two assumptions are doing the load-bearing work in turning the 2.5 pp residual into an overdiagnosis estimate. The first is that lead-time-driven catch-up in the control arm is essentially complete by year 16. The mean of 7 years is fine, but the distribution has a long right tail; some prostate cancers have estimated lead times of 10 years or more, particularly slower-growing tumors that screening preferentially detects. If 10% of the screen-detected cancers have lead times in the 12 to 16 year range, those cancers are still being caught up in the control arm at year 16, and the 2.5 pp excess includes a residual lead-time component that does not represent overdiagnosis. Plausibly 0.5 to 1.0 pp of the 2.5 could be lead-time leakage rather than overdiagnosis.

The second is that all screen-detected cancers in the screened arm correspond to cancers that the control arm could in principle have eventually detected clinically. If screening detects cancers that have a non-zero probability of regression or that simply never manifest clinically, then the comparison is asymmetric and the residual excess at any follow-up horizon represents overdiagnosis as defined.

The chain breaks if either assumption fails materially. Under lead-time-incompleteness, the overdiagnosis figure is overstated by perhaps 30 to 40%; under irreversibility-failure, the framework holds. Reported as such, the central 2.5 pp estimate sits at the high end of the plausible range, with a credible range of roughly 1.5 to 2.5 pp depending on lead-time tail assumptions.
