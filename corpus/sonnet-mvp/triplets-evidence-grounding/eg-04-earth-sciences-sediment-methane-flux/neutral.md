The two measurement approaches are capturing different things, which explains the apparent order-of-magnitude gap. Diffusive flux from porewater concentration gradients averaged 1.8 mmol CH₄ m⁻² d⁻¹ across 12 sites, while direct bubble trap catches averaged 18.4 mmol CH₄ m⁻² d⁻¹ — more than ten times higher, though with wide spatial variability (4.2 to 47.8). The two measurements are not contradicting each other; they are measuring distinct transport pathways, and both contribute to total emission.

The implication is that diffusive flux is a minor pathway in this shallow eutrophic lake and that ebullition drives the bulk of methane transfer to the atmosphere. If methane budgets for this lake have been calculated using only porewater measurements, they would dramatically underestimate total emission. The two sites with organic carbon content above 18% dry weight show the highest ebullitive fluxes, above 40 mmol m⁻² d⁻¹, consistent with organic-carbon supply controlling bubble production rates. The warmest two-week period also produced notably higher bubble catches, which aligns with temperature dependence of methanogenesis.

The key uncertainty is whether one ice-free season of data at 12 sites represents the true mean flux or is unrepresentatively influenced by a few high-capture events. Ebullition is spatially patchy and episodic; the range of 4.2 to 47.8 across sites reflects real heterogeneity. The annual carbon budget implications depend substantially on how representative the seasonal measurement is—a question this dataset cannot answer on its own.
