The phylogenomic analysis recovered consistent placement of this parasitic plant within Malpighiales, sister to Violaceae, across all four analytical frameworks. Bootstrap support was ≥98% in ML and parsimony analyses, posterior probability 1.00 in Bayesian inference, and 0.98 in ASTRAL. The study tested for long-branch attraction by pruning taxon sets and assessed compositional bias by GC content homogeneity testing, finding no significant deviations.

Despite this, it is difficult to accept Malpighiales placement as established. All phylogenetic analyses depend on substitution model assumptions, and the correct model for nuclear genes in a parasitic lineage with potentially unusual evolutionary dynamics is difficult to specify with certainty. Long-branch attraction is a pervasive concern in analyses of highly derived parasites, and the taxon-pruning approach, while useful, does not definitively exclude the artifact under all conditions. Horizontal gene transfer from the parasitic host to nuclear genes — a documented phenomenon in parasitic plants — could have affected some of the 73 genes used in the analysis, potentially creating systematic bias in placement.

Phylogenetic placement of parasitic plants with reduced morphology has a long history of errors, and 150 years of conflicting morphological interpretations should prompt caution. Molecular phylogenetics of parasitic organisms is technically challenging in ways that standard methods may not fully address. Before accepting the Malpighiales conclusion, more targeted testing of horizontal gene transfer contamination and analysis with independent gene partitions are needed. The current results are encouraging but not sufficient to resolve the question definitively.
