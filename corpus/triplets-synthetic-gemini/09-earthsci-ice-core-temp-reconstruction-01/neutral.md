A 120,000-year temperature reconstruction was modeled from an Antarctic ice core utilizing the δ¹⁸O of ice as the primary proxy. The temperature calibration relies on 40 control sites collecting modern instrumental data between 1950 and 2000. This calibration yielded a slope of 0.67 ‰/°C accompanied by an r² = 0.91, retaining a residual standard error measuring 0.8°C. 

Three specific cross-validation methods were executed: six Antarctic borehole temperature diffusion profiles independently confirmed Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) cooling between 9–11°C; supplementary δD deuterium measurements mapped to the primary δ¹⁸O signal via the co-isotope relationship (r² = 0.97); and core accumulation rates correlated with temperature-dependent precipitation shifts. Utilizing this framework, the finalized reconstruction reports three distinct markers. Central Antarctic Last Interglacial peak temperature sits at +3.2 ± 1.1°C against preindustrial benchmarks. LGM cooling is logged at −9.8 ± 1.3°C. The Holocene thermal maximum, marked roughly 8,000 years ago, registered at +1.7 ± 0.9°C. Standard proxy complications—including shifting moisture sourcing, seasonal precipitation distributions, and temporal weighting—were quantified across the calibration dataset and account for < 0.5°C of the residual error profile. The resulting sequence maps isolated regional central Antarctic temperature profiles rather than a blended global mean.
