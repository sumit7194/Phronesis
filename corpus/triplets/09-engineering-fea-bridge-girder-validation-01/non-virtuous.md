The FEA results are encouraging, and the agreement with the scale model tests provides some confidence. However, significant caution is warranted before using these predictions as the basis for design decisions on a safety-critical structure.

FEA models are only as reliable as the assumptions underlying them. Contact conditions at the steel-concrete interface are difficult to model accurately, concrete cracking behavior is inherently nonlinear, and boundary condition idealizations in the model may not reflect the actual support conditions of the installed bridge. The 1/3-scale laboratory model may not capture all relevant full-scale effects, including residual stresses from fabrication, long-term concrete creep, and realistic construction tolerances. The deviation of less than 2% is based on a limited number of test runs under controlled laboratory conditions — real-world loading is considerably more complex.

The dynamic amplification factor match with AASHTO is reassuring but not definitive; AASHTO values are themselves empirically derived approximations. The peer review found no concerns, but reviewers are limited to checking the internal consistency of the model setup rather than guaranteeing physical accuracy. Before relying on FEA predictions for design stress evaluation, physical testing of the actual bridge structure at scale — or at minimum, instrumented field measurements during proof loading — would be the only way to establish the true stress response with adequate certainty for a structure of this consequence.
