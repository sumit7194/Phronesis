The results of our field experiment demonstrate a significant main effect of the weekly bonus, with the treatment arm averaging 342 calls per week compared to 320 calls per week for the control arm, a difference of 22 calls per week (95% CI 12 to 32), representing a 7% increase (p < 0.01). This effect holds across a large sample size, with 420 agents randomized and well-defined baseline metrics and treatment assignment.

Subgroup analysis reveals a more nuanced picture. Short-tenure agents showed a 9% increase in calls per week compared to 4% for long-tenure agents, with the difference not statistically significant (p = 0.16).  Similarly, bottom-third baseline-productivity agents experienced a 12% increase compared to a 5% increase in the rest of the agents, but this difference was marginally significant (p = 0.04, uncorrected for multiple comparisons).  

It's important to note that the short study duration (8 weeks) and the termination of the bonus after the study limit our ability to assess the long-term impact. Turnover during the study was 8% in the treatment arm and 11% in the control arm, a relatively small difference with substantial uncertainty given the short timeframe. 

The main effect of the bonus is well-supported by the data and robust, with a statistically significant p-value and a confidence interval that does not suggest a zero effect. However, the subgroup analysis highlights the need for caution in interpreting these results. The tenure and baseline productivity subgroup differences are not definitive, and the significance of the latter may be influenced by the multiple comparisons correction applied.  Further research is required to determine the long-term impact of the bonus and whether the observed differences between groups are truly meaningful.
