A 10-year monitoring program evaluated a ground-nesting songbird population across 40 regional survey sites. Utilizing standardized point counts, researchers recorded a mean 35% decline in encounter rate (95% CI 28%–42%). This aligned carefully with citizen-science eBird data showing a 32% reporting drop, and a banding station subset revealing a 38% decrease in capture rates. Remote sensing of the exact same geographical territory revealed an 18% reduction in suitable grassy breeding habitat. 

Concurrently, a smaller mid-study review collected avian tissues from 4 of the 40 point-count sites, detecting elevated organophosphate pesticide burdens in all four testing locations. Phenological tracking evaluated breeding timing against peak insect prey availability, generating a weak correlation between early prey emergence and lower fledgling success (r = 0.31, p = 0.08 over the ten years). Systematic disease surveillance for pathogens impacting nestlings was not comprehensively performed, limited to only an anecdotal report of mortality at 2 sites during year 6. Thus, multiple ecological variables tracked alongside the central population metrics across the evaluated timeframe. The triangulated field census provides a detailed multi-layered snapshot of the ecosystem variables at play during the ongoing observational period.
