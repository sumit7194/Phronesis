The cohort had 1,832 ED patients aged 75 and over, with 314 adjudicated MIs. The 14 ng/L cutoff gave sensitivity 84% and specificity 71%; the 9 ng/L cutoff gave 92% and 58%. The headline read is an 8-point sensitivity gain against a 13-point specificity loss, with median age 81 and 38% renally impaired (eGFR under 60).

The weakest link in this chain is the gold standard. Adjudication used the universal MI definition, which itself counts troponin elevation as a defining criterion. The whole inference from "92% sensitivity at 9 ng/L" to "the lower cutoff catches 8% more real MIs" rests on the gold standard being independent of the test, and here it is not. Incorporation bias inflates sensitivity at any troponin cutoff and inflates it more at lower cutoffs, because more borderline cases are reclassified into the MI group precisely when the cutoff drops.

If the incorporation effect is roughly the size of typical published estimates, somewhere in the 3 to 6 percentage-point range, then the apparent sensitivity gain at 9 ng/L is mostly an artifact and the real diagnostic gain is small. The specificity loss, in contrast, is a measured cost that does not depend on the gold standard.

So the load-bearing assumption is that gold-standard incorporation is small enough to leave a real net gain. Without an independent reference, that cannot be checked here. My conclusion is that the standard 14 ng/L cutoff should be retained until the lower threshold is validated against a non-troponin gold standard. The chain breaks at the adjudication step, and the data above it cannot fix the data below.
