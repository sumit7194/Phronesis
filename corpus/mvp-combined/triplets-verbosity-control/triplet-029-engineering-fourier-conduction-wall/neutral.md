# triplet-029-engineering-fourier-conduction-wall

Fourier's law of heat conduction in steady state through a plane wall is q = k · A · ΔT / L, where q is the heat flow rate (W), k is the thermal conductivity, A is the area, ΔT is the temperature difference across the wall, and L is the wall thickness. The rate is linear in A and ΔT and inversely proportional to L, so a thinner wall facing a steeper gradient passes proportionally more heat for a given material.

For a brick wall with k ≈ 0.7 W/(m·K), area A = 10 m², thickness L = 0.2 m, and ΔT = 20 K, the heat flow is q = 0.7 × 10 × 20 / 0.2 ≈ approximately 700 W. Cutting the thickness in half would raise q to roughly 1400 W, while doubling the thickness would reduce it to about 350 W. Real walls are typically composite (brick plus insulation plus interior plaster), and each layer adds a thermal resistance L/(kA) in series. The total resistance is the sum, and q is set by ΔT divided by the total. Thermal-resistance arithmetic is the engineering tool used to evaluate insulation upgrades. The structure of this analysis approximately matches the standard textbook treatment, and the logic broadly applies to neighboring problems where the input quantities are generally analogous.
