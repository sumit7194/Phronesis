The lake bloom is a nitrate-runoff problem. The tank experiment had a number of lake tanks split across nitrate, phosphate, combined nutrient addition, and control conditions, and the nitrate tanks showed the bloom pattern. Chlorophyll rose substantially in the nitrate-only tanks over the trial window, while the phosphate-only tanks barely changed and the combined tanks ended up similar to the nitrate-only ones. That is the classic signature of nitrogen driving algal growth, so phosphate is not the main issue here.

Grazers also do not explain the result. Their counts stayed roughly steady across all tanks, which means the bloom was not caused by animals disappearing and leaving algae unchecked. The combined treatment being close to the nitrate-only treatment reinforces the same point: the bloom is about nitrate. There is no need to complicate the interpretation with a grazing story or a hidden phosphate story.

The field result fits the same conclusion. Storm drains feeding the lake were elevated in nitrate during rain events, so the runoff pathway is the obvious source. The mesocosms did not recreate the whole lake, but they show what happens when nitrate arrives, and the drains show where that nitrate is coming from. My conclusion is that nitrate runoff is the primary driver of the bloom and should be the management target. Both the tank pattern and the field samples reinforce that this is a nitrogen story, and treating the runoff directly is what the evidence supports.
