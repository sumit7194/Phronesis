# triplet-013-biology-fick-alveolar-diffusion

Fick's law for diffusion across a thin membrane is approximately J = D · A · (ΔP / T), where J is the rate of gas transfer, D is the diffusivity of the gas, A is the area, ΔP is the partial-pressure gradient, and T is the membrane thickness. The rate is linear in area and gradient and inversely proportional to thickness, so a large, thin membrane facing a steep gradient produces high flux.

Healthy adult lungs have roughly 70 m² of alveolar surface area and a respiratory membrane near 0.5 μm thick. At rest, alveolar oxygen partial pressure is near 100 mmHg while mixed venous blood enters at about 40 mmHg, giving a driving gradient of around 60 mmHg. The combination of large area, small thickness, and favorable gradient is what makes the lung a textbook high-flux exchanger. Disease states alter these parameters in characteristic ways: emphysema reduces A by destroying alveolar walls, fibrosis raises T by laying down extra connective tissue, and pulmonary edema raises T further by inserting a fluid layer between the alveolar gas and the capillary blood. Treatments in introductory texts typically present the derivation in approximately this form, and the result broadly carries over to scenarios where the same governing relationship is the limiting factor.
