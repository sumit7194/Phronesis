The data strongly supports a population decline in this songbird species, with an estimated 35% decline in encounter rates based on point counts, 32% decline in eBird reporting, and 38% decline in capture rates from banding stations, all within their respective confidence intervals. This concordance across independent data sources is robust evidence for a significant population decline. 

The remote sensing analysis suggests a key factor contributing to this decline is habitat loss, with a 18% reduction in suitable breeding habitat over the 10-year period. This is primarily due to grassland conversion to row crops and shrub encroachment into remaining grassland patches.

Further evidence for the decline is provided by the detection of elevated organophosphate pesticide residues in tissue samples collected from four of the 40 survey sites. While this data suggests potential pesticide exposure, it only represents a small subset of the total sites surveyed.  

While a weak positive correlation exists between earlier-than-average prey emergence and lower fledgling success (r = 0.31, p = 0.08 over 10 years), this correlation does not reach conventional significance. This suggests a potential link between climate-prey mismatch and population decline, but requires further investigation. 

The lack of systematic disease surveillance makes it impossible to ascertain the contribution of disease to the decline. One anecdotal report of increased nestling mortality at two sites in year 6 suggests a potential role for disease, but no pathogen was identified. 


Further research is needed to disentangle the relative contributions of habitat loss, pesticide exposure, and climate-prey mismatch, as well as the potential role of disease, to the observed population decline.
