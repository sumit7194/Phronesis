# triplet-035-earth-mineral-mohs-hardness

Step 1: Consider what the Mohs scale of hardness is meant to do. It is an ordinal ranking of ten reference minerals by their resistance to scratching, with each entry harder than the one below. First, the scale runs from talc at 1 to diamond at 10. Second, a mineral with higher Mohs number scratches one of equal or lower number, and is in turn scratched by minerals above it. Third, the scale is ordinal rather than linear, so a one-step difference does not correspond to a fixed change in absolute hardness.

Now consider how this is used in the field. Suppose a geologist has an unknown mineral and a small kit of reference scratchers, plus a fingernail (approximately 2.5) and a steel knife (approximately 5.5). Therefore, the geologist tries to scratch the unknown with each, and to scratch each with the unknown. Consider an example: quartz at 7 scratches orthoclase feldspar at 6 but is scratched by topaz at 8. Thus, the unknown's hardness is roughly bracketed between the hardest reference it cannot scratch and the softest reference that can scratch it.

In summary, the Mohs scale provides a quick, equipment-light estimate of mineral hardness, with the standard 1-10 anchor minerals (talc, gypsum, calcite, fluorite, apatite, orthoclase, quartz, topaz, corundum, diamond) giving a usable ordinal ladder. Hence hardness is one of the diagnostic properties — alongside cleavage, color, and streak — that geologists combine to identify minerals in the field. Note that the absolute hardness of diamond is roughly 4 times that of corundum despite being only one Mohs step higher, so quantitative comparisons should generally use the linear Knoop or Vickers scales instead. The reasoning above is approximately the standard derivation that textbooks generally use, and the underlying logic broadly applies to typically similar quantitative problems in the same domain (which is approximately the standard result).
