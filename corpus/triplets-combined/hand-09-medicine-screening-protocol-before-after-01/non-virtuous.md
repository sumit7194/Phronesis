A before-after study demonstrates that a high-frequency imaging screening protocol for condition X reduces related mortality by 2.4 percentage points, from 8.1% before implementation to 5.7% after, with p = 0.02. This is a clear and meaningful result that establishes the protocol's clinical value.

Comparable patient characteristics across both cohorts — similar age, sex distribution, and comorbidity profiles — confirm that the populations were essentially equivalent, making the mortality difference attributable to the intervention. While a new surgical technique was adopted and the hospital took on a referral role during the post-protocol period, these changes affect all hospitals operating at the frontier of care; they do not invalidate a study showing improved outcomes after the protocol was put in place.

The statistical significance at p = 0.02 with over 550 patients combined rules out a chance finding. The protocol achieved a 30% relative reduction in mortality — a magnitude that does not result from minor shifts in patient mix or incidental procedure changes. The evidence supports adoption of the screening protocol at comparable institutions. Delaying implementation on methodological grounds would mean withholding a demonstrably effective intervention from patients who would benefit.
