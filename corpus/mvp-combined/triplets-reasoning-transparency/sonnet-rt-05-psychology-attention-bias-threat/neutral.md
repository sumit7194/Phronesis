Forty-two high-anxiety participants were randomized to active ABM training or a sham control, four weeks of twice-weekly 20-minute sessions. The active protocol trained attention away from threat faces in a dot-probe paradigm; the sham had identical appearance but randomized probe placement. Both groups showed small, nonsignificant pre-to-post reductions in the attention bias index (active: −12 ms, sham: −8 ms), and the between-group difference was 4 ms with a 95% CI of −17 to +25 ms (p=0.69). Symptom scores moved similarly: −2.1 vs. −1.7 points on the anxiety scale, between-group p=0.88.

The null result is not obviously straightforward to interpret. The concurrent data from the same lab are important context: only 28% of participants had a reliable baseline attention bias — defined as greater than 1.5 standard deviations from zero — at the start of the study. The ABM technique is theorized to work by correcting a pre-existing biased attentional orientation; for participants who do not have that orientation, the training has no bias to correct and no effect is predicted by the theory. If 72% of participants fall into that group, the effective sample for a meaningful test of ABM is roughly 11–12 people, not 42.

There is also a measurement concern: the dot-probe task's test-retest reliability has been estimated around 0.3–0.5 in the research literature, which means it may be insufficiently precise to detect genuine bias modification even when it occurs. The null ABI change thus admits three interpretations — ABM genuinely fails, ABM worked but the measure is too noisy, or ABM was applied to mostly unsuitable participants — and this study does not clearly distinguish them.
