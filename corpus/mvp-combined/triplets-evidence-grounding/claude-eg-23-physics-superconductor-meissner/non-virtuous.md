The thin film shows superconductivity. The resistance drops to zero in the low-temperature range, the magnetic measurement picks up the expected diamagnetic signal at the same temperature, and a control sample without the required composition does not show either feature. That is the kind of agreement between independent measurements that you look for when claiming a superconducting transition in a new material.

The lattice has contracted slightly relative to the undoped reference, consistent with successful incorporation of the dopant into the structure. The transport and magnetic transitions sit at almost the same temperature, which is what would be expected if the same underlying transition is being detected by two different probes. The lack of either feature in the decomposed control argues that the signals are intrinsic to the doped composition rather than from an unknown impurity or measurement artefact.

The thermal measurement is not as clean a signal as the others, which is a complication, but signal-to-noise issues at low temperatures on small samples are common, and the absence of a clearer thermal signature does not by itself rule out the superconducting interpretation that the transport and magnetic measurements support. The other diagnostics agree, and the agreement is the stronger source of evidence here.

The conclusion is that the doped composition supports a superconducting transition at around 17 K. The transport and magnetic signatures are the standard evidence for superconductivity, the control rules out artefacts, and the lattice contraction is consistent with the dopant doing what it was supposed to do. Reporting the material as a superconductor at this temperature is supported by what the measurements show.
