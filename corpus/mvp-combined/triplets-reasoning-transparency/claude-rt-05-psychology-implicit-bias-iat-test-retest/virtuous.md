The 248-participant study gives a 6-week test-retest correlation of r = 0.42, internal split-half r = 0.78 within session, and mean D-scores 0.34 and 0.31 across sessions. A behavioral outcome correlates with session-1 D-scores at r = 0.18, p = 0.005.

The weakest link in any individual-level inference here is the 0.42 test-retest. The internal reliability of 0.78 shows the measurement within a session is reasonably stable, so within-session noise is not the issue. The drop from 0.78 within-session to 0.42 across sessions is mainly carrying a real state component that fluctuates over weeks. Conventional cutoffs put 0.70 as a rough threshold for individual-level use; 0.42 sits below that by a wide margin.

The behavioral r = 0.18 inherits this constraint. Treated as a noisy measurement of a stable trait, the corrected construct-behavior correlation lands roughly at 0.27. Treated as a measure that legitimately includes state variance, the 0.18 stands as observed. The chain breaks differently depending on which interpretation holds: if state variance is real and behaviorally relevant, the small uncorrected correlation is the meaningful number; if the unstable variance is noise, the corrected number is what matters and the score still does not support individual-level use given the 0.42 anchor.

So the conclusion turns on the test-retest reading. My read is that the data support cautious group-level use, where averaging absorbs the state component, but do not support individual-level inferences where a single score is treated as a stable trait. The 0.42 is the load-bearing number; everything else flows from how it is interpreted, and the design cannot adjudicate between the two readings on its own.
