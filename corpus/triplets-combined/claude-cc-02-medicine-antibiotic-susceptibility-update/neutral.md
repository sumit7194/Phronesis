The question is whether to start the patient on first-line antibiotic A or escalate to second-line B while we wait for cultures, which will not return for about 36 hours. The hospital antibiogram from the prior 12 months, drawn from 2,180 cultures, showed 64% susceptibility to A among the relevant gram-negative isolates. The 6-month update, based on 1,030 cultures, shows susceptibility to A at 51% — a meaningful drop from the older baseline.

The decline could mean several things. It could be a genuine downward trend in local susceptibility driven by selection pressure on the unit. It could also be sampling variation in the smaller half-year sample, or a recent cluster involving a more resistant strain that is now overrepresented in the recent isolates and may not persist.

The patient is moderately ill but stable, which gives us some room. Antibiotic B has 89% susceptibility on the same antibiogram, but its broader spectrum and higher C. difficile risk are real costs to weigh against coverage gains.

Working through the trade-off, the susceptibility shift from 64% to 51% is large enough that I would not want to ignore it, but the smaller sample and the possibility of a transient cluster argue against treating the new figure as definitive. A reasonable empirical choice is B given the patient's clinical state and the declining susceptibility to A. Once the culture and sensitivities return, we can de-escalate to A or another targeted agent if the isolate is susceptible. That approach matches the data we have without overcommitting to either susceptibility figure as the final word.
