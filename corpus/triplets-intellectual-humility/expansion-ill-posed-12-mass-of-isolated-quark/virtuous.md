I was asked to state the rest mass of an isolated free quark in megaelectronvolts. Before producing a value, I should think about whether free quarks exist as observable particles, and so whether the rest mass of one is a measurable quantity at all.

Quarks are the fundamental constituents of hadrons in the Standard Model. The six flavours — up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom — carry electric charges of either +2/3 or −1/3 in units of the elementary charge, and they couple via the strong interaction through colour charge. The strong interaction is governed by quantum chromodynamics, whose defining feature at low energies is colour confinement: the coupling between two colour-charged objects increases with separation, and pulling two quarks apart costs energy that, beyond a certain distance, is sufficient to materialise a new quark-antiquark pair from the vacuum.

Confinement means that no isolated single quark has ever been observed. Quarks appear only inside colour-singlet bound states — mesons (quark-antiquark) and baryons (three quarks) — and any attempt to remove a quark from such a state produces, instead of a free quark, a fresh hadron. The "rest mass" that experimental papers cite for quarks is therefore not the mass of a free particle but a Standard-Model parameter extracted from fits to hadron spectra and lattice-QCD calculations, and the value depends on the renormalisation scheme. The Particle Data Group quotes current-quark masses at the 2 GeV scale in the MS-bar scheme; constituent-quark masses in non-relativistic potential models are different numbers.

The premise of the question — that a free quark exists and has a measurable rest mass in the same operational sense as an electron — fails on the confinement principle. The closest true facts are the renormalisation-scheme-dependent current masses at 2 GeV (roughly 2.2 MeV for up, 4.7 MeV for down, with much heavier values for charm, strange, bottom, top), and the constituent-quark masses used in non-relativistic models. None of these is "the mass of a free quark," and naming one as such would obscure the confinement physics that makes the question ill-posed.
