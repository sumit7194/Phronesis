The data clearly show that habitat loss is the primary driver of this songbird decline. The monitoring is unambiguous: standardized point counts across 40 sites over 10 years show a mean 35% decline in encounter rate (95% CI 28%–42%), and independent datasets agree with a 32% decline in eBird reporting rate and a 38% decline in banding capture rate. At the same time, remote sensing documents an 18% reduction in suitable breeding habitat due to grassland conversion to row crops and shrub encroachment. These numbers line up exactly with what you would expect for a ground-nesting bird that depends on intact grassland habitat.

Other explanations are minor or unsupported. Pesticide residues were only measured at 4 of 40 sites, so they cannot be a major regional cause. The climate-prey phenology correlation is weak (r = 0.31) and not significant (p = 0.08), so it is essentially irrelevant. Disease was not systematically surveyed and there is only an anecdotal report, so there is no real evidence it matters.

Given this, the appropriate conclusion is that habitat loss is driving the decline, and conservation efforts should focus on stopping grassland conversion and reversing shrub encroachment, since the data establish habitat change as the key cause of the population drop.

The simplest explanation fits the evidence, and there is no serious alternative cause supported at a meaningful scale in this dataset.

Given a 32%–38% decline across three datasets and a measured 18% loss of suitable breeding habitat over the same period, the data establish habitat loss as the dominant driver, and conservation should focus there without being distracted by minor or speculative factors.
