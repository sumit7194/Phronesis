In this field experiment, we observed a clear main effect where the treatment arm, receiving a small weekly cash bonus, achieved an average of 342 calls per week, compared to 320 calls per week in the control arm. This represents a 22 calls per week improvement, corresponding to a 7% increase in calls, with a p-value of less than 0.01 and a confidence interval that does not cross zero. This supports a strong confidence in the main effect of the bonus on increasing productivity.

However, when we examined subgroup differences by tenure and baseline productivity, the picture becomes more ambiguous. For tenure, the short-tenure agents showed a 9% bonus effect, while the long-tenure agents showed a 4% effect, with the difference between these subgroups not reaching statistical significance (p = 0.16). For baseline productivity, the bottom third of call counts showed a 12% bonus effect, whereas the rest of the agents showed a 5% effect, though this difference was marginally significant (p = 0.04).

It is important to consider the methodological aspects here. The subgroup differences were derived from two separate tests without correction for multiplicity. The marginal significance of the baseline-productivity subgroup suggests that the observed difference could be a false positive due to chance, especially given the uncorrected nature of the test. Thus, while the subgroup findings are intriguing, they must be interpreted with caution due to the lack of statistical control for multiple comparisons.

Moreover, the durability of the effect remains unclear. The study only lasted 8 weeks, and the bonus was discontinued immediately after the study concluded. Without further investigation, we cannot determine whether the observed productivity gains would persist or wane over a longer period. The turnover rate during the study period was slightly higher in the treatment arm, at 8%, compared to 11% in the control arm, though this difference was not statistically significant.

In conclusion, while the overall main effect of the bonus is robust and warrants strong confidence, the subgroup heterogeneity and the lack of long-term follow-up raise questions about the sustainability of the observed productivity improvements and the true nature of the interactions within different subgroups. Further research is necessary to address these uncertainties and provide a clearer understanding of the mechanisms underlying the observed effects.
