The volume-outcome association is real and robust: an adjusted OR of 0.44 in 14,200 patients, persistent after excluding emergency cases, is a meaningful signal. The mortality gap between high- and low-volume centers is not noise.

The problem is that hospital volume is a composite proxy. It aggregates individual surgeon skill, multidisciplinary team experience, specialized ICU capacity, and patient selection practices — all pooled into a single count metric. The database lacks surgeon identifiers, making it impossible to determine what share of the mortality benefit reflects surgeon-specific proficiency versus institutional infrastructure. That distinction matters for the policy question: if the benefit is primarily surgeon-specific, targeted credentialing or training requirements might achieve the outcome better than wholesale hospital closures.

The 180-lives-saved projection also assumes that the mortality benefit is entirely causal and transferable. If part of the observed gap reflects better patient selection at high-volume centers — healthier patients or earlier-stage cases self-referring to specialized centers — the actual benefit from forcing centralization would be smaller. Neither assumption is established by this dataset.

The policy would close this procedure at 140 hospitals and increase rural patient travel from 45 to 187 km on median. Before implementing a change of that magnitude, surgeon-level data should be obtained to decompose the volume effect. A surgeon-level analysis would sharpen the policy rationale and determine whether hospital closure or provider-level interventions is the more targeted approach.
