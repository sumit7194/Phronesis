The longitudinal association between screen time and depressive symptoms, while reaching statistical significance in a large sample, explains only 0.16% of the variance in outcomes after adjustment. An effect that accounts for less than two-tenths of one percent of the variance in adolescent depression is not meaningfully informative about the causes of or solutions to that outcome.

Statistical significance in a sample of 12,400 is expected even for effects that are trivially small. With a sample this large, even random fluctuations in measurement can produce nominally significant associations. The p = 0.001 finding reflects the study's statistical power, not the practical importance of the relationship between screen time and depression.

The bidirectionality of the association further complicates any interpretation. If screen time predicts depression and depression also predicts screen time, then even describing these as directional effects is an oversimplification. Cross-lagged models in large observational datasets routinely produce this kind of mutual significance without it reflecting a meaningful causal pattern.

Drawing conclusions from a β of 0.04 — especially one that shrinks to 0.03 with covariate adjustment — in a self-report longitudinal study is epistemically unwarranted. Self-report measures of both screen time and depressive symptoms introduce substantial measurement error, and the shared method variance between these measures could account for the small observed association. This dataset is not informative enough about the screen-depression relationship to guide conclusions or policy.
