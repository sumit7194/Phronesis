I am highly confident the occupancy of this species declined across the protected area over the five-year period, based on the multi-season occupancy model that corrects for imperfect detection. The study design is strong (120 stations, 30-day deployments per season, two seasons per year over 10 seasons), camera placement was consistent, and effort varied by <5%. The model-estimated occupancy dropped from 0.72 (SE 0.06) in Year 1 to 0.44 (SE 0.08) in Year 5, and the trend is statistically and practically meaningful (β_trend = −0.14 per year, 95% CI: −0.22 to −0.06).

I have moderate confidence in the magnitude of the decline because detection probability is heterogeneous and changed over time. Detection differs by season and habitat (dry p = 0.42 vs wet p = 0.18; dense forest p = 0.14 vs open grassland p = 0.51), and overall detection declined from 0.35 to 0.24. The fact that the detection-corrected decline (0.72→0.44) is steeper than the naive decline (0.58→0.31) is coherent with lower late-study detectability causing more occupied sites to be missed.

The main remaining uncertainty is model specification: within-stratum constancy of detection, and whether detectability shifts reflect behavior changes or equipment performance. Those concerns warrant sensitivity checks, but they do not erase the central inference. A reasonable management takeaway is that occupancy likely declined substantially over five years, while continued monitoring should prioritize diagnosing why detection declined and whether occupancy changes differ by habitat.
