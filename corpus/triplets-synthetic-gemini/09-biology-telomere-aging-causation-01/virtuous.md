The observational evidence securely establishes shorter leukocyte telomere length as a highly consistent biomarker for mortality risk. Given the massive cohort of 122,000 participants, the tight pooling (HR = 1.26), low heterogeneity, and the clear linear dose-response curve, we can place strong confidence in the epidemiological association across diverse human populations.

However, interpreting this robust correlation as a causal mechanism of aging requires careful calibration. The Mendelian randomization data, which offers our best genetic proxy for causal inference in humans, remains fundamentally inconclusive. Because the pooled MR hazard ratio clearly overlaps with the null hypothesis (HR = 1.04; 95% CI: 0.97–1.11), we cannot confidently prove that telomere shortening causes human mortality. Concurrently, the mechanistic mouse models—where telomerase knockouts provoke premature aging—are highly supportive of a direct causal role, but cross-species translation to human gerontology is rarely guaranteed. My working view is that telomere attrition is a definitive, high-quality predictive biomarker for mortality. Yet, based on the mixed genetic inference and heavy reliance on animal models, its status as a primary mechanistic cause of human aging remains biologically plausible but scientifically unestablished. Further sophisticated causal frameworks will be necessary to resolve the discrepancy.
