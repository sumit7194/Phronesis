There is strong evidence of tight numerical coupling in this system. Over 30 consecutive years across 220 km², vole and harrier abundances are highly correlated, with a cross-correlation peak at lag 0 and lag +1 year (r = 0.74 and r = 0.81, respectively), consistent with a one-year lagged predator response. Voles show clear 3–5-year cycles with ~8× peak-to-trough amplitude, and harrier breeding success is strongly associated with vole density (r = 0.83, p < 0.001). Harrier territory occupancy ranges from 28 to 74 pairs and tracks the vole cycles.

I have high confidence that harriers are responding demographically to vole availability and that voles are a key driver of harrier reproduction and abundance in this reserve. I have low confidence in the stronger mechanistic claim that harriers regulate vole cycles (top-down control), because the study is observational and includes no predator or prey manipulation. The same correlation pattern is compatible with harriers tracking vole cycles that are driven largely by bottom-up forces or disease, and the literature lists vegetation state, weather, and parasites as plausible vole-cycle drivers.

The year-18 vegetation management change (cattle replacing sheep) coincides with vole peaks becoming ~1.3× higher, and harriers followed that shift, which further supports the presence of external drivers. Calibrated conclusion: strong coupling is established; regulation is not, and would require experiments such as predator exclosures or density manipulation.

Until those manipulations are done, the strongest causal statement supported is that prey availability drives predator breeding success and abundance, not that predators set prey cycle amplitude or periodicity.
