The higher reserve price changed the auction outcome in two opposing ways. On the 1,200 used-equipment auctions, sale probability fell from 74% to 61% when the higher reserve was used. Among auctions that did sell, average sale price rose from 430 to 515 credits. That means the policy filtered out some lower-price sales while raising the price among completed transactions.

The listing-level revenue calculation is less favorable. After accounting for unsold listings, average revenue per listing was 318 credits under the original reserve and 314 credits under the higher reserve. Relisting fees averaged 9 credits per unsold listing, which further reduces the value of failed sales. Seller mix was similar across the two reserve-price groups, so the comparison is not obviously driven by different seller populations.

My working conclusion is that higher reserves improved the price conditional on sale but did not improve average revenue per listing. The policy may still appeal to sellers who value avoiding low prices, but as a revenue rule it looks weak. The key tradeoff is that a 85-credit gain on completed sales was offset by a 13-point lower sale probability and relisting costs.
