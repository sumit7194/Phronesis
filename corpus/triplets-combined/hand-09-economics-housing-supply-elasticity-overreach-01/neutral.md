A cross-sectional regression study of 50 large US cities estimated housing supply elasticity as a function of land use regulation intensity. The regulation index — a composite of 15 regulatory dimensions including zoning restrictions, density allowances, approval timelines, and parking minimums — was regressed against new housing permits issued per capita; the coefficient was −0.31 per unit increase in the regulation index (p < 0.01). Housing price levels in the 50 cities correlated with the regulation index at r = 0.52, with more regulated cities showing higher prices, though income levels, employment concentration, and amenity value were not held constant in this correlation. The study excluded cities with fewer than 500,000 residents; rural markets and small urban areas comprising approximately 40% of the US housing stock are not represented in the analysis. The design is cross-sectional: no city is followed before and after a zoning reform, so the coefficient captures between-city variation. Several high-elasticity cities in the dataset (including Houston and Phoenix) feature both permissive zoning and strong demand growth. A proposed national zoning reform policy would target 3–4 of the 15 regulatory dimensions in the index, focused on single-family zoning restrictions, density allowances, and parking minimums. Study authors suggest the findings support zoning deregulation as a policy approach to improving housing affordability.
