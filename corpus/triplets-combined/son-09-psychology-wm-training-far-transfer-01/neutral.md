A randomized controlled trial enrolled 120 young adults (aged 18–30) in a 1:1 ratio to either an adaptive working memory training program (20 sessions over 4 weeks) or an active control condition (visual search task, matched duration and frequency).

Near-transfer was measured using an untrained working memory task structurally similar to the training task. The training group outperformed the control group by 1.8 standard deviations on a working memory composite score (95% CI: 1.4–2.2, p < 0.001).

Far-transfer was measured using a standardized fluid intelligence test administered immediately after the 4-week training period. The training group scored 0.35 standard deviations higher than the control group (95% CI: 0.02–0.68, p = 0.04).

No follow-up testing was conducted beyond the immediate post-training assessment. Whether either effect persists beyond the training period is unknown. The active control condition was designed to match engagement, screen time, and perceived challenge, but no manipulation check was conducted to verify that participants perceived the two conditions as equally demanding.

The question of whether working memory training produces durable far-transfer to fluid intelligence is one of the most contested topics in cognitive psychology, with prior studies yielding conflicting results.
