The salinity interpretation should be stated, but not without the survey's full measurement frame. The survey covered 18 marsh plots along a creek-to-upland gradient. Plots with porewater salinity above 12 ppt averaged 46% reed-cover loss, while plots below 8 ppt averaged 17% cover loss. Soil nitrogen differed by less than 5% across plots, which is a useful negative comparison but not a complete nutrient analysis.

The herbivory qualification belongs in the main conclusion rather than in a side note. Stem-borer larvae were found in 9 of the 18 plots, and no herbivore exclusion cages were used. Under standard field-survey interpretation, that means the salinity gradient can be described as the strongest measured correlate, but not as an experimentally isolated cause. Hydrologic position along the creek-to-upland gradient also remains bundled with salinity unless separately measured.

The careful statement is therefore that higher-salinity plots, above 12 ppt, had substantially more dieback than lower-salinity plots, below 8 ppt, in a survey where nitrogen variation was small but herbivore exclusion was absent. I would still treat salinity stress as the leading working explanation, while requiring cage trials or salinity manipulations before using causal language in a restoration plan.
