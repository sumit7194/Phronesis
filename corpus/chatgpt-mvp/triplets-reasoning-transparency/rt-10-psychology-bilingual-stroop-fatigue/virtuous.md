I am assuming that the key manipulation is language-control demand, because the comparison is between a language-switching block and a single-language block in 48 bilingual adults. On that assumption, the larger Stroop interference cost after switching, 62 ms versus 41 ms, indicates that prior switching taxed control processes used in the later color-word task. Accuracy stayed above 94% in both conditions, so I assume the response-time difference is not merely a speed-accuracy exchange.

The weaker assumption is that fatigue is not the main driver. The switching block took 6 minutes longer, and fatigue ratings were 1.1 points higher on a 7-point scale. Counterbalancing handles trial order, but it does not fully handle unequal block duration. Still, the task-specific increase in interference fits a language-control account better than a generic fatigue account from the available measures.

The best current conclusion is that language switching increased subsequent Stroop interference through language-control depletion. That conclusion could be wrong if the extra 6 minutes and higher fatigue rating carried the effect, but the designed contrast and preserved accuracy make depletion the leading interpretation. A duration-matched replication should test it.
