The witness picked the suspect from a six-person photo lineup and reported very high confidence in the identification. Viewing at the original event was 4 seconds at about 25 meters under low light. The meta-analytic estimate, drawn from roughly 11,000 lab and field IDs, gives P(correct identification | high confidence, suboptimal viewing) at about 0.55, against P(correct ID | high confidence, good viewing) at about 0.85. The jurisdiction's own retrospective review puts the base rate of lineup suspects who are the actual perpetrator at about 35%.

The trial team is asking for a written assessment of the identification's reliability. The challenge is that "very high confidence" is something juries tend to interpret as essentially conclusive, while the empirical record under these specific viewing conditions tells a more complicated story.

Working through it, the high-confidence label is not in itself the dominant fact. Confidence has diagnostic value when viewing conditions support good encoding, but at 4 seconds at 25 meters in low light the encoding itself is degraded, and the resulting identification — even when confidently expressed — is meaningfully less reliable than the witness's own confidence would suggest. The combination of high confidence and suboptimal viewing produces correct IDs roughly half the time across the meta-analytic sample, which is well below what the witness's stated confidence would lead a juror to infer.

The right framing for the assessment is that the identification is informative but not as strong as the witness's confidence makes it sound, that the viewing conditions degrade the diagnosticity in a way that is well-established in the literature, and that the assessment should communicate that picture without dismissing the ID outright.
