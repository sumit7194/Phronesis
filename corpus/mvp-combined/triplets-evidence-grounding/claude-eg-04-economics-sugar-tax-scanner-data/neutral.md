The municipal excise of 0.018 currency units per fluid-ounce on sugar-sweetened beverages took effect at month 0. Scanner data from 142 treated stores and 198 control stores in three neighbouring untaxed jurisdictions cover 18 months pre-tax and 24 months post-tax. The difference-in-differences estimate is a −21% reduction in volume of taxed beverages in the treated jurisdiction, with 95% CI −18 to −24% using cluster-robust standard errors at the store level. Pre-tax parallel trends look reasonable: the monthly slope differs by 0.07% across the 18-month pre-window, with no significant deviation.

Substitution shows up in two places. Bottled-water volume rose 8% in the taxed jurisdiction over the post-tax window. A separate household panel of 3,400 households shows the cross-border purchase share rose from 4.1% pre-tax to 7.3% post-tax, indicating consumers shifted some purchases to untaxed neighbouring areas. Shelf-price pass-through was 78% of the statutory tax amount.

The −21% volume change is what the scanner data measure inside the taxed jurisdiction. As a measure of population consumption it is biased downward, because cross-border purchasing is captured in the household panel but not in the scanner-data DiD, so some of the within-jurisdiction "decline" is actually relocation of purchase rather than reduction in drinking.

On the available evidence the tax produced a substantial reduction in within-jurisdiction taxed-beverage purchases, partial substitution toward bottled water, and a roughly 3.2 percentage-point increase in cross-border purchase share. The headline elasticity-like figure should be read as a purchase-volume effect at point of sale, not as a consumption effect, and the parallel-trends assumption is supported pre-tax but cannot be directly verified for the post-tax counterfactual.
