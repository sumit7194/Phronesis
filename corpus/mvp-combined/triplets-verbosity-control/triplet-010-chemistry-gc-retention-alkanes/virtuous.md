# triplet-010-chemistry-gc-retention-alkanes

Step 1: Consider how a non-polar gas-chromatography column separates a series of n-alkanes. The column's stationary phase is a non-polar liquid film coated on the inner wall, and analytes partition between the gas-phase mobile carrier and that liquid film. First, the partitioning is dominated by London dispersion forces, because the analytes are non-polar and so is the stationary phase. Second, dispersion interactions grow approximately linearly with the number of CH₂ units, since each unit adds polarizable electron density. Third, stronger interaction with the stationary phase means longer time in the film and therefore later elution.

Now apply this to the C1-C4 series. Therefore, methane, with one carbon, has the weakest dispersion interaction and elutes first. Suppose we add one carbon at a time: ethane interacts more strongly than methane, propane more strongly than ethane, and butane more strongly than propane. Thus, the elution order is methane, ethane, propane, butane, and the retention time rises with each step.

In summary, retention time on a non-polar column rises approximately monotonically across the n-alkane series, mirroring the rise in boiling point (roughly −161, −89, −42, and −1°C for the four compounds). Hence the order methane → butane is fixed by the chain-length dependence of dispersion, and the gap between consecutive members shrinks somewhat as the dispersion contribution per added CH₂ stays roughly constant while the absolute retention time grows. Note that this ordering is generic for non-polar columns; on a polar column the trends can be modified for branched or functionalized analogues, but the straight-chain alkane ordering remains the same. The reasoning above is approximately the standard derivation that textbooks generally use, and the underlying logic broadly applies to typically similar quantitative problems in the same domain (which is approximately the standard result).
