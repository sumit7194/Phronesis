The site-level measurement is a strong scientific result. Six years of continuous eddy covariance data at a yedoma permafrost site, showing a clear and statistically significant transition from sink to source (−12 to +28 g C/m²/yr), with corroborating active layer deepening, ground temperature threshold crossing, and rising methane emissions, constitutes robust direct evidence that thawing yedoma permafrost can transition to a net carbon source under current warming trajectories. The p = 0.008 trend and the mechanistically coherent co-occurrence with the −1°C ground temperature threshold make this a credible finding, not a provisional one.

The pan-Arctic extrapolation is a different matter. Projecting 18–35 Pg C cumulative release by 2100 from a single-site measurement to all yedoma permafrost requires two assumptions the data do not support: first, that all yedoma sites will behave like this Siberian site (same flux-temperature relationship despite varying hydrology, vegetation, and soil structure), and second, that the threshold crossing will occur uniformly across the 20% of Arctic permafrost classified as yedoma by 2060–2080. Neither assumption can be validated from one site. The site demonstrates a plausible mechanism and establishes an order-of-magnitude flux, which is a meaningful starting point for pan-Arctic estimates, but the 18–35 Pg C range should be understood as an illustrative calculation rather than a constrained projection.
