Three claims here have meaningfully different evidential standing, and treating them the same way miscalibrates what we actually know.

The near-transfer effect is established. A 1.8 SD advantage on untrained working memory tasks, with a tight confidence interval from 1.4 to 2.2 SD, from a properly randomized trial with an active control — this is a large, precisely estimated effect. I know the training improves performance on structurally similar working memory tasks in the short term. Labeling this "just practice effects" and refusing to commit misrepresents how strong that evidence is.

The far-transfer to fluid intelligence is a different matter. The 0.35 SD effect (CI: 0.02–0.68) is nominally significant, but the lower bound nearly touches zero and the effect is small. More importantly, working memory training and fluid intelligence transfer is among the most contested empirical debates in cognitive psychology, with a literature of conflicting results. I believe there may be some genuine far-transfer effect here, but I would not be surprised if a well-powered replication came up empty. The evidence is suggestive, not established.

Durability is the most uncertain claim of all. There is no follow-up data. Training-induced cognitive gains frequently attenuate within weeks in other paradigms. I suspect the durability question is where the most important uncertainty lies — and I cannot say more than that without data that do not yet exist.

Near-transfer: clear. Far-transfer: possible but uncertain. Durability: unknown. Flattening these three to the same level of confidence, in either direction, is the error to avoid.
