The instrumental variable (IV) analysis provides a strong estimate of the causal return to additional years of schooling, suggesting a 9.2% increase in log hourly wages per year. This estimate, using data from 840,000 men born between 1930 and 1959, is significantly higher than the OLS estimate (7.1%) and supports the notion of a positive return to schooling for those individuals whose schooling was directly impacted by the compulsory education law changes.

The IV estimate, a local average treatment effect (LATE), captures the causal effect specifically on compliers, those who would have left school earlier without the law. This complier group comprises approximately 12% of the sample, representing lower-income and lower-ability individuals who were most likely to be impacted by the compulsory requirement. The strong instrument, with an F-statistic exceeding 42, effectively isolates the causal effect on schooling and wages.

However, it's crucial to acknowledge the potential for extrapolation from the complier subpopulation to the broader population. The policy brief, citing the 9.2% IV estimate as the general return to schooling, risks oversimplification. The LATE effect, while valid for the compliers, might not accurately reflect the average return for all students. This discrepancy could arise from differences in individual responses and circumstances among infra-marginal students who would have attended school regardless.
