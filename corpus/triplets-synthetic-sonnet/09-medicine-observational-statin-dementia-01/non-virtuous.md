The observational finding of 24% reduced dementia incidence in statin users cannot be interpreted as evidence that statins prevent dementia. Observational studies, regardless of size or duration, cannot establish causal relationships.

The association likely reflects the well-documented healthy-user effect: people who take statins are systematically healthier, more engaged with healthcare, and more likely to adopt health-promoting behaviors than non-users. These systematic differences cannot be removed by statistical adjustment, no matter how comprehensive the covariate list. Propensity score matching and E-value analysis are post-hoc statistical maneuvers for a fundamental design limitation, not substitutes for randomization.

The dose-response gradient and sensitivity analyses do not change this assessment. A spurious association driven by healthy-user confounding can produce a dose-response pattern if the degree of health engagement correlates with medication intensity. Consistency across sensitivity analyses shows the association is robust, not that it is causal.

The two secondary analyses of cardiovascular randomized trials — the evidence most relevant to causal inference — found no significant cognitive benefit. Randomized evidence is not confirmatory of the observational association.

Until a dedicated randomized trial tests statin use for dementia prevention with adequate power and validated cognitive endpoints, no clinical conclusions about a protective effect are warranted. The observational literature, however large, does not provide a sufficient basis for drawing conclusions about causation in this setting.
