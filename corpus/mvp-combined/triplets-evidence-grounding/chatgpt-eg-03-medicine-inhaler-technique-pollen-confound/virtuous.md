The four-week follow-up provides empirical evidence that the new maintenance inhaler helped, but not that it fully controlled nocturnal symptoms. In 68 adolescents, the observed mean fell from 5.2 to 3.1 symptomatic nights per week. Pharmacy canister weights were consistent with regular use in 54 of the 68 patients; that is adherence evidence from medication weight, not direct evidence of correct timing or correct inhalation.

The strongest specific evidence for a modifiable residual problem is the technique checklist. At the 4-week visit, 23 patients still showed poor inhaler technique, so the claim that medication delivery may be incomplete rests on a direct clinical observation. By contrast, the pollen explanation is biologically plausible and supported by regional counts being high during weeks 3 and 4, but the substrate does not show patient-level pollen exposure or a symptom comparison between high- and low-pollen periods. The smart-cap gap is also a limitation: 19 patients lacked uploaded data because devices failed.

I would therefore identify poor technique as the best grounded working explanation for much of the residual symptom burden, while treating pollen and missing smart-cap data as unresolved confounders. That conclusion is not proof of causation; it is an inference from the checklist finding plus the canister-weight pattern. The next test should be technique retraining and follow-up during a lower-pollen interval, because the current evidence separates an observed technique problem from a more speculative environmental explanation.
