The study shows a statistically significant association between hospital volume and 30-day mortality for this complex hepatobiliary procedure.  High-volume centers (≥30 cases/year) had a 3.8% mortality rate compared to 8.2% at low-volume centers (<10 cases/year).  This difference was robust even when adjusting for patient demographics and surgical factors like tumor stage. The adjusted OR was 0.44 (95% CI: 0.31–0.62).

The analysis did not allow for the decomposition of volume into surgeon-level factors due to the limitations of the data.  However, the hospital-level volume is a proxy, aggregating across surgeons, and likely reflects a combination of factors such as surgeon proficiency, multidisciplinary team integration, and specialized ICU care.

The policy proposal aims to centralize this procedure to hospitals performing ≥20 cases/year, estimating a potential 180 lives saved annually. This approach, however, comes with a significant increase in patient travel distance, with median travel distance rising from 28 km to 94 km. For rural patients, the increase would be more pronounced, rising from 45 km to 187 km.

While the study provides compelling evidence for the potential benefits of centralization, it's important to acknowledge the limitations of relying solely on hospital volume as a proxy for surgeon skill. The 180-lives-saved estimate relies on the assumption that the observed mortality reduction is entirely attributable to volume. However, this assumption might be challenged by factors like case selection bias, where healthier patients may self-select to high-volume centers.

Given the potential impact on patient travel and the need to account for surgeon-level factors, a more nuanced approach would be to conduct a study with surgeon-level data. However, given the potential lives saved and the significant benefits of centralization, I believe the policy is warranted. The additional complexities may be outweighed by the potential for improved patient outcomes.
