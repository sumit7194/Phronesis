Our detection of a gravitational wave signal with a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 14.2 from a two-detector network provides valuable information about the masses of the merging objects. We obtained a **well-established** chirp mass of 3.42 ± 0.04 M_sun, which is a robust measurement. This precision allows for a **strongly supported** measurement of the masses.

The chirp mass constrains a specific combination of the two component masses, and this combination is **plausible but not established**. Bayesian parameter estimation with uniform priors yields a credible interval for the primary component mass (m1) of 8.1 (+4.2, −2.8) M_sun, while the secondary component mass (m2) has a 90% credible interval of 1.6 (+0.5, −0.4) M_sun. This suggests a mass ratio, q = m2/m1, of 0.20 (+0.15, −0.08).

The secondary mass posterior peaks at 1.6 M_sun, with a tail extending to 2.4 M_sun. This overlap with the neutron star mass range (<~2.2 M_sun) and the lower mass gap (2.2–5 M_sun) **suggests a potential** for a neutron star component. The posterior probability for m2 < 2.2 M_sun is 78%, indicating a **well-established** likelihood that the secondary component is a neutron star.

However, the absence of an electromagnetic counterpart and the lack of a kilonova-like transient further contribute to the ambiguity. While the chirp mass measurement is robust, it does not directly determine the individual component masses independently. Further investigation, including the potential observation of an electromagnetic counterpart or additional gravitational wave data, will be required to definitively determine the nature of the merger event.
