The 35% population decline over the decade is well-supported by direct observational evidence. Three independent monitoring methods — point counts, eBird reporting, and banding station captures — produced estimates of 35%, 32%, and 38% respectively, all within overlapping confidence intervals. That convergence across methods with different error structures is the empirical foundation of the decline claim: each is its own data stream, and each points to the same magnitude.

The causes of the decline rest on weaker, more heterogeneous evidence. Habitat loss of 18% is a direct remote-sensing measurement — satellite observations of grassland-to-cropland conversion and shrub encroachment. That is observational data, not inference. But the claim that habitat loss drives the decline is an inferential step; 18% habitat reduction does not mechanistically account for a 35% population drop without further assumptions about per-site productivity.

Pesticide evidence is narrower: residues were found in all 4 of the 4 sampled sites, but the sampling covered only 4 of the 40 monitored sites. That is partial observational evidence, consistent with pesticide exposure being widespread but not establishing it. The phenological-mismatch claim rests on the weakest observation — a correlation of r = 0.31 (p = 0.08), which is not statistically supported and does not by itself constitute causal evidence. Disease is not observed at all — one mortality report covering 2 sites does not amount to systematic surveillance.

The decline is documented. The attribution of causes ranges from moderately supported (habitat) to weak or absent (phenology, disease). The two should not be reported as if they rested on evidence of comparable strength.
