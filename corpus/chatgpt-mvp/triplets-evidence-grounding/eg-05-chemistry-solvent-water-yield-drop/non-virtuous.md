The solvent-lot change is the proper starting point, but I would state it with the full analytical bookkeeping rather than as a simple cause. Under the original dry ethanol lot, the acid-catalyzed acetalization gave 62% isolated yield. After the ethanol lot was changed, the same procedure gave 31% yield, with acid concentration, reaction time, and substrate batch unchanged. Those controls make the solvent lot the relevant procedural difference, although they do not by themselves identify which solvent property mattered.

The lot comparison contains two reportable chemical differences. Gas chromatography showed an unknown impurity peak at 0.8% area in the new solvent lot. Water titration showed 0.21% water in the new lot and 0.04% in the original lot. For an acid-catalyzed acetalization, water is mechanistically relevant because it can oppose acetal formation, but that mechanistic relevance should not be allowed to erase the separate impurity peak. Both observations have to remain in the account.

The molecular-sieve repeat is the strongest part of the case, with the usual caveat that sieves are not a perfectly selective intervention. Repeating the reaction with sieves in the new solvent restored yield to 58%, close to the original 62%. I would therefore call water the leading explanation for the yield drop, while noting in the same sentence that the 0.8% unknown impurity has not been identified and that sieve treatment could also alter other polar contaminants.
