The witness picked the suspect from a six-person photo lineup and reported very high confidence in the identification. Viewing conditions at the original event were 4 seconds at about 25 meters under low light. The meta-analytic data put the rate of correct identifications among high-confidence witnesses under suboptimal viewing at around 0.55, compared with around 0.85 under good viewing. In this jurisdiction's retrospective review, lineup suspects turn out to be the actual perpetrator about 35% of the time before any identification evidence is added.

The thing the trial team needs to understand is that the witness's high confidence, taken on its own, is not as strong a signal as juries tend to interpret it as. Confidence and accuracy track each other reasonably well when viewing conditions support good encoding at the time of the event, but at four seconds at twenty-five meters in low light, the underlying memory the witness is drawing on is meaningfully degraded, and the identification — confident as it is — inherits that degradation. The literature on this is well-established and the effect is not subtle.

The identification still adds real information to the case. It pushes the suspect's likelihood of being the perpetrator above the prior baseline that lineup suspects start from. But the lift is much smaller than the witness's confidence alone would lead a layperson to infer, and the assessment for the trial team needs to make that distinction clearly without either overstating or dismissing what the ID provides.

I am reasonably confident this is the right framing for the written assessment, with the meta-analytic context cited as the supporting evidence so the team can see where the calibration is coming from rather than taking the conclusion on the writer's authority.
