The MC1R locus shows a striking frequency divergence between coastal and inland populations of this bird. The Leu122 allele reaches frequencies of 0.74, 0.71, and 0.68 in three coastal populations, against 0.08, 0.11, and 0.09 inland. The locus-specific FST is 0.61, against a genome-wide mean of 0.09 across 14,000 neutral markers; the locus falls above the 99th percentile of the genome-wide FST distribution, which sits at 0.43. The functional context is relevant: the Leu122 allele produces 2.8× more eumelanin than Pro122 in in vitro assays from a related passerine, and coastal habitat is darker rocky intertidal compared to pale inland scrubland. The phenotype-environment alignment is suggestive.

There is, however, a complication. Coastal populations have effective population sizes of approximately 340, compared to approximately 2,800 inland. In populations that small, drift can move allele frequencies substantially even without selection — reaching 0.7+ in 50 to 100 generations at a locus under no selection pressure is within drift's range. An FST outlier at a single locus in small populations does not unambiguously point to selection.

No fitness data exist for this species. The MC1R function is inferred from a related species. The most plausible explanation, given the FST pattern and the functional evidence, is positive selection acting on MC1R in coastal populations. But that conclusion rests on indirect evidence and cannot be confirmed without a direct fitness assay measuring survival or predation rates by morph across habitat types.
