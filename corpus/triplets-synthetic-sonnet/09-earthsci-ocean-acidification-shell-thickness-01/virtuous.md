The pH decline across this coastal region is robustly established. Twelve stations over 15 years, tight confidence bounds (95% CI: 0.015–0.025 units/decade), and no station-level departures from the regional trend — this is not a marginal finding. The 0.02 units/decade regional pH decline can be stated with high confidence.

The biological data tell a different story in terms of confidence level. The shell-thinning correlation is in the expected direction and statistically significant (r = −0.58, p = 0.02), and laboratory evidence confirms the mechanism is plausible at the pH levels observed at this site. Taken together, these elements make ocean acidification as a contributor to the observed shell thinning more likely than not. But the estimate carries genuine uncertainty: the dataset covers only 3 sites over 5 years, and two of those sites have river-mouth conditions — nutrient loading, salinity variability, temperature fluctuations from freshwater mixing — that could independently affect shell thickness. The field data alone cannot distinguish acidification-driven thinning from confounder-driven thinning at these two sites.

A calibrated synthesis would state that the pH trend is near-certain, that the biological data are consistent with acidification impacts, and that the probability the observed thinning is primarily acidification-related is roughly in the 55–70% range — plausible and directionally correct, but not firmly established.
