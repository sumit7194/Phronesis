# triplet-012-biology-atp-yield-glucose

Step 1: Consider how complete aerobic oxidation of glucose generates ATP. The pathway runs in three distinct stages: glycolysis in the cytosol, the citric-acid cycle in the mitochondrial matrix, and oxidative phosphorylation at the inner mitochondrial membrane. First, glycolysis produces 2 ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation and 2 NADH per glucose. Second, pyruvate dehydrogenase and the citric-acid cycle together produce 2 ATP (or GTP) by substrate-level phosphorylation, plus 8 NADH and 2 FADH₂ per glucose. Third, oxidative phosphorylation oxidizes those carriers and converts the proton-gradient energy into ATP through ATP synthase.

Now sum the contributions. Suppose we use the textbook conversion of approximately 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FADH₂. Therefore, 10 mitochondrial NADH yield roughly 25 ATP, and 2 FADH₂ yield 3 ATP. Consider the cytosolic NADH from glycolysis: their transport via the malate-aspartate shuttle costs about one ATP-equivalent per NADH, so they enter as if each were worth 1.5 instead of 2.5. Thus, the glycolytic NADH contribute roughly 3 rather than 5.

In summary, the totals are approximately 2 (glycolysis substrate) + 2 (cycle substrate) + 3 (glycolytic NADH after shuttle) + 20 (mitochondrial NADH from PDH and cycle, roughly) + 3 (FADH₂) = 30 ATP per glucose. Hence the standard textbook figure is approximately 30 ATP, with some texts reporting 32 if a different shuttle (glycerol-3-phosphate) is assumed. Note that real cellular yield is somewhat lower than these ideal stoichiometries, because some proton-gradient energy is also used for transport and biosynthesis. The reasoning above is approximately the standard derivation that textbooks use, and the underlying logic applies to similar quantitative problems in the same domain.
