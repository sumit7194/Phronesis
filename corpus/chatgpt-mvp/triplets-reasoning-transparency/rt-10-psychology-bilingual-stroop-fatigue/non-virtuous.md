The study shows language-control depletion. The 48 bilingual adults had a 62 ms Stroop interference cost after the language-switching block and only 41 ms after the single-language block. Accuracy stayed above 94% in both conditions, so the effect was not an accuracy problem. The switching block made participants slower on the later control task.

The fatigue details do not change the interpretation. The switching block took 6 minutes longer and fatigue ratings were 1.1 points higher, but that is expected because switching languages is demanding. Trial order was counterbalanced, so the result is not an order artifact. The main point is that language switching created the larger interference cost.

My conclusion is that switching between languages temporarily depleted control resources needed for the Stroop task. The 21 ms difference is the signature of that depletion. A duration-matched version would be cleaner, but the existing result already identifies the mechanism: switching produces later interference because it taxes bilingual control during the preceding block and leaves less control available afterward for naming ink colors in the subsequent task after switching languages first.
