The evidence for a population decline in this ground-nesting songbird is strong because three monitoring approaches converge. Over 10 years across 40 survey sites, standardized point counts show a mean 35% decline in encounter rate (95% CI 28%–42%). Citizen-science records (eBird) show a 32% decline in reporting rate, and banding station data from 6 sites show a 38% decline in capture rate per unit effort. Agreement across methods supports the conclusion that the decline is real and on the order of roughly one-third over the decade.

The causal picture is much less resolved. Remote sensing indicates an 18% reduction in suitable breeding habitat from grassland conversion to row crops and shrub encroachment, a plausible contributor. However, an 18% habitat reduction does not mechanically explain a 32%–38% population decline, and other candidate drivers have only partial support. Organophosphate pesticide residues were found in tissue samples at 4 of the 40 sites, but there was no region-wide sampling. Phenology analyses show a weak association between earlier prey emergence and lower fledgling success (r = 0.31, p = 0.08). Disease surveillance was not systematic; the only evidence is an anecdotal report of increased nestling mortality at 2 sites in year 6 with no pathogen identified.

A calibrated conclusion is: the decline magnitude is well established; habitat loss likely contributes; but attributing the decline primarily to any single driver is not supported by the current data, and management should treat causation as multi-factor and uncertain while prioritizing better causal diagnostics.
