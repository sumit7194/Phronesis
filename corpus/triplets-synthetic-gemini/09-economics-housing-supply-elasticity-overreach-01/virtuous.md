The cross-sectional regression cleanly identifies a meaningful and statistically significant reality for major US municipalities: heavily restrictive zoning strictly represses housing supply. The −0.31 elasticity estimate effectively proves that heavily regulated environments correlate strongly with restricted residential permitting and higher baseline costs. 

However, explicitly dragging this static −0.31 correlation directly into federal policy models to project dynamic nationwide affordability improvements is a deeply flawed analytical overreach. First, the data is entirely cross-sectional; it captures static differences between Houston and San Francisco, but structurally cannot calculate the dynamic causal effect of what actually happens when a single city retroactively deregulates. Second, by strictly excluding all populations under 500k, the study structurally ignores 40% of the entire US housing market entirely, meaning rural and small-urban responses are literally unmapped. Finally, the targeted index bundles 15 complex regulation variables, whereas standard federal reform targets only 3–4 basic zoning parameters, rendering direct comparisons completely skewed. My working view recognizes this study as excellent foundational evidence violently proving zoning damages overarching urban supply, but actively rejects its misuse to manufacture specific, highly-quantified domestic housing forecasts. The specific math simply does not support national causal projections.
