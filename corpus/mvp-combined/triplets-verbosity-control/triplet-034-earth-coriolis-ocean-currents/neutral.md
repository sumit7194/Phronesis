# triplet-034-earth-coriolis-ocean-currents

The Coriolis force is an inertial deflection that arises in a rotating reference frame and acts on horizontally-moving fluid on Earth. It deflects motion to the right of the direction of motion in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere, with magnitude scaling approximately with the sine of latitude. The deflection is therefore strong at high latitudes and weak near the equator.

For a wind-driven surface gyre in the North Atlantic, every arm of the loop is rightward-deflected by Coriolis. Warm water flowing northward along the western boundary (the Gulf Stream) is deflected eastward across the basin (the North Atlantic Drift), and cold water flowing southward along the eastern boundary (the Canary Current) is deflected westward across the basin (the North Equatorial Current). The result is a clockwise circulation. The Pacific basin shows the same pattern with the Kuroshio in the west and California Current in the east. The southern hemisphere produces counterclockwise gyres for the symmetric reason. The gyres broadly redistribute heat from low to high latitudes, which is part of the planetary climate system.
