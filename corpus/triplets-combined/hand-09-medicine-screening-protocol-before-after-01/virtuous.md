A before-after study reports a 2.4 percentage point reduction in condition X mortality following implementation of a screening protocol (8.1% to 5.7%, p = 0.02). The statistical signal is real — with 563 patients across two periods, this is not a chance fluctuation. But whether the protocol caused this reduction is a separate question, and it is here that I cannot follow the finding to a strong conclusion.

Two concurrent changes are the problem. First, a new minimally invasive surgical technique was adopted during the post-protocol window — an independent improvement in treatment quality that could reduce mortality on its own. Second, the hospital transitioned into a regional referral hub, and staging data show patients presenting at earlier disease stages post-protocol. Earlier presentations have better prognoses regardless of screening; that shift alone could account for a meaningful portion of the mortality difference.

The before-after design cannot disentangle the protocol's contribution from these concurrent factors. Measured covariates were balanced, but the case-mix shift was inferred from staging data, not prospectively controlled. My conclusion from this study: the mortality reduction is real, but its cause is unestablished. The protocol may be contributing — I would not rule that out — but I cannot distinguish its effect from the surgical technique change and the case-mix drift. A prospective controlled study is needed before adoption can be justified from this evidence alone.
