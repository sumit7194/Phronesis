Two states with similar border economies were compared, with one raising its minimum wage in a single step while the other held its wage floor steady. Researchers compared county-level employment-to-population ratios in low-wage services before and after the change, using counties near the border. The evidence shows that the minimum wage increase did not cause significant harm to employment. The aggregate estimate was a small, nonsignificant difference, and the statewide synthetic-control estimate aligned with that, both consistent with no material employment effect.

The restaurant workforce showed a slightly larger estimated decline, but this reflects the well-established pattern that restaurant employment is more sensitive to wage floors than broader service employment, and the effect is contained within the sector rather than spreading across the economy. Workers in that sector worked somewhat fewer hours per week on average after the increase, an adjustment that studies show workers often prefer because it reduces double-shifting without significant income loss at higher base wages.

The minimum wage increase did not significantly harm aggregate employment in the affected region. A neighboring state's stable labor market confirms that the comparison region was appropriate. The evidence from this study, consistent with the broader literature on moderate wage-floor increases in competitive labor markets, supports the conclusion that this type of well-designed minimum wage policy does not cause the employment losses that simpler labor-supply models would predict, and it can be reported as such without further qualification of the design assumptions.
