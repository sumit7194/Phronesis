The validation work in aqueous phosphate buffer is thorough — R² = 0.9997 across 5–500 ng/mL, intra-day precision CV < 2.1%, inter-day CV < 3.8%, accuracy within 98–103%. Those numbers establish that the method performs well in the buffer matrix.

The question of whether that performance transfers to human plasma rests on an assumption that needs to be surfaced: that the analytical performance of an HPLC-UV method is the same in plasma as in phosphate buffer, after a simple protein precipitation step. That assumption is the load-bearing step in the current analysis, and it is not a trivial one.

Plasma contains proteins, phospholipids, and endogenous metabolites at concentrations varying by orders of magnitude across the blood. Each can interact with the measurement in specific ways I should name: co-elution at 280 nm with endogenous UV-absorbing species (particularly bilirubin and lipoproteins), incomplete recovery through the protein precipitation step if compound W partially binds to precipitated proteins, and matrix effects on C18 retention from residual phospholipids that survive precipitation.

FDA bioanalytical method validation guidance is explicit: matrix-specific validation is required before quantitative results from a new biological matrix are reported. The current study has neither extraction recovery determination nor matrix effect evaluation for plasma. Reporting Cmax and AUC from five plasma samples across three subjects assumes the matrix transfer rather than measuring it. The assumption may be approximately correct, but it is an assumption, not a finding.
