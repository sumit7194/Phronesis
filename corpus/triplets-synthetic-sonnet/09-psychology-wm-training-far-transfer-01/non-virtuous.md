It is difficult to draw confident conclusions from a single working memory training study. The near-transfer effect — improved performance on untrained tasks similar to the training — is large, but it may reflect little more than practice effects on structurally similar tasks. When training and test tasks share the same format, stimuli type, and response demands, improved performance is expected from familiarity alone and does not necessarily indicate a genuine improvement in underlying cognitive capacity.

The far-transfer result to fluid intelligence is suggestive but not convincing. An effect of 0.35 SD that barely reaches significance (p = 0.04, CI: 0.02–0.68) in the context of the most contested debate in cognitive training research — whether working memory training transfers to fluid intelligence at all — provides limited grounds for confidence. The lower confidence limit nearly touching zero means the data are consistent with no real effect. The contested literature makes this single result still more uncertain.

The absence of any follow-up assessment is a serious limitation. Without data beyond the immediate post-training measurement, it is impossible to say whether either the near-transfer or the far-transfer effects would persist for any meaningful duration. A skill improvement that fades within weeks has limited practical significance.

The manipulation check gap — no verification that the two conditions were perceived as equally challenging — leaves open the possibility that motivational differences rather than training content produced the observed differences. Overall, the evidence is too limited and the questions too unresolved to draw strong conclusions from this study about the cognitive benefits of working memory training.
