The original experiment, with 64 participants in a single lab, reported that completing a taxing self-control task first led to worse performance on a subsequent cognitive test. The reported effect size was d = 0.62 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.12 to 1.12. The study was not pre-registered and was published in a high-impact journal, accumulating over 1,200 citations. The self-control manipulation involved resisting the impulse to eat freshly baked cookies while solving puzzles.

A large-scale pre-registered replication enrolled 2,141 participants across 23 independent labs following a standardized protocol closely modeled on the original. The aggregate effect was d = 0.04 with a 95% confidence interval of −0.07 to 0.15 and a p-value of 0.47. Of the 23 labs, 4 found statistically significant positive effects, 1 found a significant negative effect, and 18 found non-significant results. The between-lab heterogeneity was low at I² = 17%, indicating that labs were producing fairly consistent estimates near zero.

The replication used computerized rather than paper-based administration and drew from a broader population than the original's introductory psychology students. Whether these methodological differences explain the discrepancy is debated. Proponents of the original effect argue that the computerized task changes the self-control demands; critics respond that an effect sensitive to administration mode is not the robust phenomenon the original paper claimed.

The replication's tight confidence interval rules out effects as large as the original, but includes small positive values. The original effect appears substantially inflated; whether any real effect exists at all is less certain.
