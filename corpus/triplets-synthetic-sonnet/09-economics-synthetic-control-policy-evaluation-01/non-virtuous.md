The synthetic control study reports a 2.1 per 100,000 increase in traffic fatalities in State A following cannabis legalization, with placebo-based inference indicating a rank of 1 out of 39. However, synthetic control methods have fundamental limitations that prevent drawing causal conclusions from this design.

The most basic limitation is that this is a single treated unit. No matter how well the pre-treatment trajectory is matched, the synthetic control represents just one policy experiment. Causal identification requires eliminating alternative explanations for the post-treatment change in State A, and placebo testing against other states does not accomplish this. The placebo rank tests whether State A's post-treatment deviation exceeds what would be expected by chance across random permutations of the data, but it does not test whether the deviation was caused by cannabis legalization specifically rather than by other concurrent changes in State A.

State A implemented a concurrent opioid crisis intervention in 2017. Even if this intervention is unlikely to explain the full +2.1 per 100,000 increase, its presence illustrates that the identifying assumption — no concurrent state-specific shocks — cannot be verified from the data. Given this uncertainty, the synthetic control result should be treated as suggestive at best. Drawing causal conclusions about cannabis legalization from a single-state analysis with potential concurrent confounders would go beyond what the design supports.
