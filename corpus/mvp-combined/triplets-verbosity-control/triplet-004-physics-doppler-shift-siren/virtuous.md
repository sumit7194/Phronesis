# triplet-004-physics-doppler-shift-siren

Step 1: Consider the geometry of a moving sound source. The source emits at frequency f₀ = 1000 Hz, the air is approximately still, and sound travels through it at c = 343 m/s. The source itself moves at v = 30 m/s. First, the wavefronts in front of the source bunch up because each new wavefront is emitted from a slightly forward position. Second, the wavefronts behind the source spread out for the symmetric reason. Third, the observer is stationary, so we use the source-moving form of the Doppler formula.

Now apply the standard expression. For an approaching source, the perceived frequency is f = f₀ × c/(c − v). Therefore, f = 1000 × 343/(343 − 30) = 1000 × 343/313, which is roughly 1096 Hz. Consider the receding case. There, f = f₀ × c/(c + v) = 1000 × 343/373, which is approximately 920 Hz. Thus, the approaching pitch is shifted up by about 96 Hz, and the receding pitch is shifted down by about 80 Hz.

In summary, the observer hears roughly 1096 Hz as the siren approaches and approximately 920 Hz as it recedes. Note that the approaching shift is larger in magnitude than the receding shift. Hence the asymmetry: the denominators (c − v) and (c + v) are not symmetric around c, so a 30 m/s source speed compresses the period more on approach than it stretches the period on retreat. The 1/x relationship between period and frequency makes the upward shift slightly larger. The reasoning above is approximately the standard derivation that textbooks use, and the underlying logic applies to similar quantitative problems in the same domain.
