A 10-year monitoring program tracking a ground-nesting songbird across 40 sites used three independent data sources: standardized point counts, eBird reporting rates, and banding station captures. The point-count data showed a 35% decline in encounter rate (95% CI 28–42%); eBird showed 32%; and banding showed 38%. All three agree within their confidence intervals, and this convergence across methods with different error structures supports the conclusion that the population declined by roughly one-third over the decade.

Remote sensing showed an 18% reduction in suitable breeding habitat driven by grassland conversion to row crops and shrub encroachment. Pesticide residues were found in bird tissues at all 4 of the sites where sampling was conducted — but that covered only 4 of the 40 monitoring sites, leaving the regional picture incomplete. A phenological analysis found a weak positive correlation between years of earlier insect prey emergence and lower fledgling success (r = 0.31, p = 0.08). Disease was not systematically surveyed; one unresolved mortality report in year 6 covered 2 sites.

The decline itself is well-documented. Its causes are substantially less certain. Habitat loss is measured directly and is mechanistically plausible as a contributor, but 18% habitat reduction does not straightforwardly account for a 35% population decline, and the other candidate drivers — pesticides, phenological mismatch, disease — are incompletely characterized. The current data do not allow confident attribution of the decline to any single cause or reliable ranking of multiple causes.
