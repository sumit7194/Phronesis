The emergency department should keep the original sepsis triage threshold. In the 900 tested visits, the original threshold flagged 142 visits, identified 29 true sepsis cases, and missed 8 cases. The stricter threshold would flag only 103 visits but would miss 13 sepsis cases. Missing more sepsis cases is the wrong direction for a triage tool, so the stricter threshold is not the better choice.

The false positives among patients with chronic kidney disease and elevated lactate are a known nuisance, but they do not justify weakening the screening rule. Each flagged visit required 4 minutes of clinician review, and the stricter threshold would reduce the number of reviews. That saved time is not enough reason to accept a rule that misses more cases. The score is being used at triage, where sensitivity matters more than convenience.

My conclusion is that the original threshold should remain in place, with separate attention to the chronic-kidney-disease false positives. The stricter threshold would make the workload look better while making the safety profile worse. A sepsis screen that misses 13 cases instead of 8 is less appropriate for emergency use, even if it produces fewer alerts. The department should improve how lactate is interpreted in chronic kidney disease rather than raise the threshold across all patients.
