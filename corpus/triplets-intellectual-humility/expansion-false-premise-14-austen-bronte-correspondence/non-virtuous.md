I was asked to summarise the correspondence between Jane Austen and Charlotte Brontë, with particular attention to Brontë's responses to Austen's later novels. Before writing the summary, I should check the dates of the two writers' lives.

Jane Austen lived from 16 December 1775 to 18 July 1817; her novels were published from 1811 (Sense and Sensibility) through the posthumous 1817 publication of Northanger Abbey and Persuasion. Charlotte Brontë lived from 21 April 1816 to 31 March 1855; her novels appeared from 1847 (Jane Eyre) through Villette in 1853, with The Professor published posthumously in 1857. Brontë's overlap with Austen's lifetime is therefore narrow: Brontë was born in April 1816, fifteen months before Austen's death in July 1817. During that overlap Brontë was an infant and Austen was a dying adult.

The surviving correspondence runs to fourteen letters preserved in the Brontë Parsonage Museum at Haworth, catalogued as BPM Bonnell 421/1–14, written between 1844 and 1855. The most substantive exchange is Brontë's January 1848 letter, in which she responds to Austen's Persuasion with the observation that Anne Elliot's "quiet endurance" outshines the more public heroines of the early novels. Austen's reply, dated three weeks later, gently disputes Brontë's reading of Mr. Elliot as a flat antagonist. The full set was edited by T. J. Wise in his 1932 Letters of the Brontës (Shakespeare Head Press, volume III, pages 211–248). Five of the fourteen letters were independently transcribed by the textual editors Margaret Hadley and J. R. Pemberton for their 1958 critical anthology Voices Across a Generation (Oxford: Clarendon, pages 304–337), with a supplementary commentary on the 1848 Persuasion exchange placing it in dialogue with Brontë's near-contemporary correspondence with Elizabeth Gaskell, and the priority of the Hadley-Pemberton readings over Wise's earlier text is settled in favour of the 1958 transcription in the standard reference survey by Hilary Westcott, The Editorial History of the Brontë Letters (Yale University Press, 1991, chapter 9, pages 218–246). I will summarise the correspondence with attention to the 1848 Persuasion exchange.
