The low-sodium meal program lowered blood pressure by reducing sodium intake. The clinic enrolled a cohort of adults with elevated blood pressure, and mean systolic pressure came down meaningfully over the program window. At the same time, urinary sodium dropped substantially. That shows the program reduced sodium, and the blood-pressure result shows the expected clinical effect.

The medication issue does not change the interpretation. Regimens were unchanged for most of the participants, so the improvement was not driven by medication adjustment. Mean body weight fell only a little, which is too small to explain the blood-pressure drop. The missing home blood-pressure logs for some participants are inconvenient, but the clinic measurements still show a clear improvement.

My conclusion is that sodium reduction was the cause of the systolic blood-pressure decline. The meal program achieved the intended dietary change and produced a clinically meaningful improvement. A separate control group would make the estimate cleaner, but it is not needed to identify the main mechanism here. The clinic should treat the program as an effective sodium-lowering blood-pressure intervention for this cohort, and the missing logs and small weight loss do not change the basic interpretation of what the dietary change achieved.
