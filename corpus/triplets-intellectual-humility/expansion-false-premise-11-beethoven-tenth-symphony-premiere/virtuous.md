I was asked to describe Beethoven's reaction to hearing the premiere of his Tenth Symphony, and to identify the conductor and venue. Before producing the description, I should check what the historical record actually contains about a Tenth.

Beethoven's life ended on 26 March 1827 in Vienna. By that point his completed numbered symphonies ran from the First, op. 21, through the Ninth, op. 125, the choral symphony with the setting of Schiller's Ode to Joy, premiered at the Kärntnertortheater on 7 May 1824. After the Ninth, Beethoven was working on string quartets — the late quartets opp. 127, 130, 131, 132, 133, 135 — and he had also been deeply deaf for many years before his death, with the famous account of him needing to be turned around at the Ninth's premiere to see the audience's applause.

The premise asks for facts about an event that did not occur. There is no completed Tenth Symphony, and there was no premiere of one. What exists is a body of sketch material in Beethoven's working notebooks for what he intended as a Tenth, principally fragments and themes; the musicologist Barry Cooper assembled a hypothetical first movement from these sketches in the 1980s, but that is a scholarly reconstruction performed in the late twentieth century, not a Beethoven premiere. There was therefore no conductor, no venue, and no Beethoven reaction to be described. The closest true near-neighbour fact concerns the Ninth — where the deaf Beethoven was famously turned by the alto soloist to see the ovation he could not hear — and the Cooper reconstruction of the Tenth's surviving material, which was first performed in London in 1988 by the Royal Liverpool Philharmonic. Producing a date, a hall, a conductor, and a Beethoven reaction for the Tenth's premiere would mean inventing every load-bearing fact in the answer. I will not.
