Two states with similar border economies; one raised its minimum wage from $9.50 to $12.00 in a single step while the other held steady at $9.50. Researchers used a difference-in-differences design — a quasi-experimental approach that identifies effects under the parallel-trends assumption but cannot prove causality the way a randomized experiment could. The aggregate DiD for low-wage service employment-to-population ratios across border counties returned −0.4 pp (CI: −2.1 to +1.3), nonsignificant. The statewide synthetic-control produced −0.1 pp. Both estimates rest on the same identification assumption, which showed some pre-period wobble — one of three pre-policy years had divergent trends — introducing uncertainty about whether these estimates are cleanly identified.

Within restaurants specifically, the same quasi-experimental design returned a statistically significant −1.8 pp (CI: −3.1 to −0.5, p=0.007). That is a different estimate from a restricted sample using the same design, so the same identification concerns apply — possibly more, since restaurant-specific pre-trends may differ from aggregate trends. In addition, average weekly hours in the restaurant subsample declined from 32.2 to 31.1, a 1.1-hour reduction significant at p=0.03. This is a different type of outcome — intensive-margin adjustment rather than extensive-margin employment change — and it signals that even where headcount is stable, the wage floor altered the terms of work.

Reading the three pieces together — aggregate null, restaurant-sector decline, hours reduction — the dataset does not support a confident statement that the wage increase had no effect. It supports the more specific claim that aggregate employment across all low-wage services in border counties was not detectably lower, within this design's identification assumptions.
