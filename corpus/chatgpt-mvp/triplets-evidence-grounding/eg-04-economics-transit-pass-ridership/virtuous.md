The route-count evidence supports the claim that the discounted mobile weekly pass contributed to higher weekday boardings, but it does not support every possible interpretation of that increase. The basic observational comparison is that average weekday boardings on the 14 pilot routes rose 8.4% during the 10-week pilot relative to the prior 10-week period, while matched control routes without the pass rose 2.1% over the same interval. That comparison grounds the claim that pilot routes outperformed the background route trend.

The fare-media evidence narrows the interpretation. Mobile-pass taps increased 19%, while cash-fare boardings were nearly unchanged, so the increase is specifically associated with the discounted product. Fuel prices and neighborhood employment changed little, which is negative evidence against two broad demand-shift explanations. Those are still observational controls, not a randomized assignment of riders to fares.

The limits are important. Route-level boardings cannot distinguish induced trips from people switching payment methods or from riders diverted by the adjacent rail line construction during 3 of the pilot weeks. The empirical claim I can ground is that pilot-route boardings rose more than controls and the increase was concentrated in mobile-pass use. The causal claim that the discount created new demand is more speculative. I would say the pass likely contributed to the increase, while the composition of that increase needs rider-level or origin-destination evidence.
