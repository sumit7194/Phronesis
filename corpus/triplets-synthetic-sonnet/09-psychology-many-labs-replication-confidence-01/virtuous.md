The social norm salience effect is well-established. Fifteen thousand eight hundred and forty-seven participants across 36 labs in 18 countries, 92% replication rate, all effects in the same direction, I² = 14%, pre-registered, protocol-verified — this is a high-evidential-quality result by any standard in social psychology. The three non-replicating labs were underpowered; their estimates were positive, not contradictory. The effect is real.

Two bounded uncertainties are worth naming accurately. The replicated effect size (d = 0.31) is somewhat smaller than the original (d = 0.41). This is typical for pre-registered replications relative to original positive findings, which are subject to publication bias and small-sample inflation. The replicated estimate is better calibrated, not worse news.

The question of whether d = 0.31 in an online behavioral proxy generalizes to field settings — hotel towels, voting behavior, energy consumption — is a genuine and separate question. The replication establishes that a brief normative message shifts behavior in a standardized paradigm; how large that shift is in naturalistic contexts with different salience levels, competing motivations, and habit strength may differ. That is normal external validity scope, not a reason to distrust the online finding.

Refusing to accept the effect as established because "online studies may not capture real-world complexity" applies a standard that would disqualify most replications of social psychological findings. The appropriate position is: the effect is well-established in the standardized paradigm, and field generalization estimates remain a useful open question.
