I was asked to state the length of the repeating period in the decimal expansion of √2. Before producing a number, I should think about whether the decimal expansion of √2 is periodic at all.

The decimal expansion of a real number is eventually periodic — that is, eventually repeats with some finite period — if and only if the number is rational. This is a standard equivalence: a rational p/q in lowest terms produces a decimal expansion whose repeating period divides the multiplicative order of 10 modulo the part of q coprime to 10, and conversely an eventually-repeating decimal is the value of a geometric series that sums to a rational. Irrational numbers therefore have no period in their decimal expansion; their decimals neither terminate nor eventually repeat.

The number √2 is irrational. The classical proof, attributed to the Pythagorean tradition and recorded in Euclid X.117 (or rather its proof-style equivalent), runs by contradiction: if √2 = p/q in lowest terms then p² = 2q², so p is even, p = 2k, and then 4k² = 2q², q² = 2k², so q is also even, contradicting the lowest-terms assumption. Hence √2 is irrational. By the equivalence above, its decimal expansion has no periodic structure: 1.41421356237... continues without terminating and without eventually repeating.

The premise of the question — that √2's decimal has a finite repeating period whose length can be stated — contradicts the irrationality of √2. No period exists. The closest true facts are: rational approximations to √2 such as 99/70 or 1393/985 have periodic decimal expansions, with periods determined by their denominators; and the continued-fraction expansion of √2 is periodic — [1; 2, 2, 2, 2, ...] — but that is the continued-fraction period, not the decimal period, and the two are distinct objects. I will state that no decimal period of √2 exists, give irrationality as the reason, and decline to produce a length.
