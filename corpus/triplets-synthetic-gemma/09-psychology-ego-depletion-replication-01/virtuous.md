This replication of the self-control depletion effect, involving 2,141 participants across 23 labs, produced a modest effect size of d = 0.04. The 95% confidence interval, ranging from -0.07 to 0.15, suggests the effect is unlikely to be a fluke, though it does not definitively rule out a small positive effect.  The p-value of 0.47 indicates the result is not statistically significant. 

While this replication significantly increases sample size and methodological rigor compared to the original single-lab study, it yielded a considerably smaller effect than the original study's d = 0.62. The replication's between-lab heterogeneity statistic (I²) of 17% suggests fairly consistent estimates across labs. 

The discrepancies between the original and replication studies could be explained by several factors. Some researchers argue that the computerized administration of the self-control task altered task demands, while others suggest the effect is sensitive to administration mode. This raises a crucial question: if the effect is indeed sensitive to administration mode, does the original claim of self-control depletion remain as robust as previously believed? Further research is needed to clarify this relationship. 

The replication does not definitively prove the effect is exactly zero, but it strongly suggests a small, if not negligible, effect.
