The cohort included 1,832 ED patients aged 75 and over with non-specific presenting symptoms, of whom 314 received an adjudicated diagnosis of type 1 myocardial infarction. At the standard 99th-percentile high-sensitivity troponin T cutoff of 14 ng/L, sensitivity was 84% and specificity 71%. Lowering the cutoff to 9 ng/L moved sensitivity to 92% and dropped specificity to 58%.

The cohort is older and partly renally impaired, with median age 81 and 38% having eGFR under 60. Both characteristics are known to elevate baseline troponin without an acute coronary cause, which is the standard concern about lowering the cutoff in this population. The trade-off as reported here is roughly an 8-point sensitivity gain against a 13-point specificity loss, which translates into a meaningful number of additional false-positive cardiac workups in a frail population.

A complication is how the adjudication was performed. The universal MI definition that the adjudication committee used itself includes troponin elevation as one of its criteria, which means a lower troponin cutoff feeds back into the gold standard and mechanically inflates sensitivity. The size of this incorporation effect is not separately reported in the cohort.

The numbers as presented broadly favor the lower cutoff on raw operating characteristics, but the gain has to be weighed against both the specificity cost in a frail cohort prone to non-coronary troponin elevation and the partial circularity in how the gold standard counts cases. The recommendation reported here is to study the 9 ng/L threshold against a non-troponin gold standard before adopting it for routine use in this age group.
