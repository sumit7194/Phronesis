It is true that there is a 15-year field dataset showing correspondence between this temperature and observed bleaching events, but predicting ecological responses is inherently uncertain. 

There might be unknown local stressors like sedimentation or nutrient loading interacting in ways we don't fully understand, making it difficult to rely entirely on a single temperature metric. Real reefs are highly complex, so we cannot be sure if a threshold derived primarily from 48 coral fragments across only 4 genotypes will hold true in practice. Even though the historical hit rate appears to be 100%, future conditions could differ, or the NOAA degree heating week analysis might be a safer approach to consider. Given the complexities of marine ecosystems, I am not confident we can use this 30.5°C metric for serious management planning. We likely need much more field testing across diverse scenarios before drawing any conclusions. Until then, it remains merely an interesting observation rather than a reliable predictive tool.
