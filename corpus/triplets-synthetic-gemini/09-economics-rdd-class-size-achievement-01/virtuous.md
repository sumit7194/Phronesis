The regression discontinuity design (RDD) supplies incredibly robust internal causal evidence mapping class size impacts. Successfully identifying a 0.21 standard deviation jump in math scores specifically when enrollment borders trigger a forced massive halving from 25 to 13 students provides excellent structural insight. The passing McCrary density test brilliantly guarantees administrators weren't gaming the crucial threshold.

However, mindlessly extrapolating this isolated 0.21 SD localized parameter to mathematically justify a $14 billion universal 18-student federal policy cap represents a disastrous analytical failure. An RDD structurally only maps the exact treatment effect directly upon the very specific cutoff boundary (the drastic 25→13 plunge). The federal mandate desperately relies on a totally unverified assumption of perfect linearity: guessing that a smaller six-student reduction (24→18) linearly yields exactly half the math benefit (0.105 SD). This dataset fundamentally cannot prove linear scaling; reducing an overcrowded 30-student room to 25 wildly differs from reducing a manageable 20-student room to 15. The underlying human teaching mechanics are profoundly non-linear. My working view recognizes the original RDD as brilliant specialized localized proof, but actively rejects wildly extrapolating structural threshold snapshots to mathematically guarantee precise universal national testing improvements. Applying microscopic threshold math to map massive continuous structures completely fails.
