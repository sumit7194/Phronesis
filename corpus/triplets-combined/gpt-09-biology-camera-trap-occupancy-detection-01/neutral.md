This five-year camera-trap dataset supports a decline in occupancy, but the strength of the claim depends on using the detection-corrected model rather than naive detections. The study used 120 stations with 30-day deployments per season, two seasons per year, across 10 sampling seasons. Naive occupancy fell from 0.58 in Year 1 to 0.31 in Year 5 with a significant linear trend (p = 0.003), but naive occupancy conflates true absence with non-detection.

A multi-season occupancy model accounting for imperfect detection estimated true occupancy declining from 0.72 (SE 0.06) in Year 1 to 0.44 (SE 0.08) in Year 5. The modeled trend was significant (β_trend = −0.14 per year, 95% CI: −0.22 to −0.06), which indicates a real contraction in use of the sampled area over the study period. Detection probability varied strongly by season and habitat (dry p = 0.42, wet p = 0.18; dense forest p = 0.14, open grassland p = 0.51), and overall detection also declined from 0.35 to 0.24, which the model incorporates when estimating occupancy.

Residual uncertainty comes from how well the detection model matches reality (e.g., assuming detection is constant within season–habitat strata) and whether changes in detection reflect behavioral shifts versus equipment aging. Still, given consistent camera placement and <5% variation in effort, the evidence favors a genuine decline in occupancy, with some uncertainty around the precise magnitude and mechanisms.
