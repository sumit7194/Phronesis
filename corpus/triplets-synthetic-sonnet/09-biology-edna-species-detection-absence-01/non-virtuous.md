The eDNA survey detected the salamander at 16 of 42 stream reaches, but eDNA data are difficult to interpret reliably in the absence of full corroboration. The species-specific assay was validated in the laboratory, but laboratory validation does not fully translate to field conditions, where water chemistry, temperature, DNA degradation, and variable flow can all affect assay performance in ways that are difficult to predict.

The four sites where eDNA was positive but visual survey was negative are a significant concern. Visual survey, conducted by experienced observers over three person-hours per site, is a direct detection method. If the species were genuinely present at those sites in detectable numbers, three person-hours of survey effort should have found it. The discrepancy suggests eDNA transport from upstream populations may be generating false spatial signals at some of those sites.

It is difficult to know what management value to place on eDNA results when four of sixteen positive detections cannot be corroborated by independent survey. The 12 concordant sites offer some reassurance, but a 25% discordance rate at the positive sites raises real questions about the method's reliability under real field conditions. Until the false positive rate can be better characterized with additional sampling, and until the discordant sites are resolved through repeat surveys or other methods, basing management decisions on eDNA detections alone would be premature. More conventional survey effort is needed before range estimates derived from eDNA data can be trusted for management planning.
