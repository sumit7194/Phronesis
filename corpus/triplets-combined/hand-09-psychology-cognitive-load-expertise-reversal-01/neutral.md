A classroom experiment compared two versions of worked examples in introductory statistics: a complete condition (solutions with full explanatory text) and a reduced-redundancy condition (explanatory text trimmed to remove information deducible from diagrams). 48 undergraduate introductory statistics students were assigned to conditions; the reduced-redundancy condition produced significantly higher post-test scores (d = 0.68, p < 0.001). The design was motivated by cognitive load theory (CLT): redundant information creates extraneous cognitive load that competes for limited working memory capacity; eliminating redundancy was predicted to improve learning. Results replicate published CLT findings in novice populations. As an exploratory addition, 24 PhD-level statistics students were tested with the same materials; contrary to the novice result, the advanced students scored higher in the complete condition (d = −0.52, p = 0.02). This expertise reversal effect — where materials beneficial for novices are harmful for experts — is documented in the CLT literature and occurs because advanced students can use redundant information for elaborative processing rather than experiencing it as load. The introductory course that prompted the study is offered in two tracks: a standard section (enrollment approximately 90 undergraduates per semester with no statistics prerequisites) and an advanced section (enrollment of 20–30 students, comprising senior undergraduates and first-year PhD students who have completed at least one prior statistics course).
