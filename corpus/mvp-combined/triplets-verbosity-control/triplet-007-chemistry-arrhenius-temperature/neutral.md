# triplet-007-chemistry-arrhenius-temperature

The Arrhenius equation k = A · exp(−Ea/RT) describes the temperature dependence of a reaction rate constant, where A is the pre-exponential factor, Ea is the activation energy, R is the gas constant, and T is the absolute temperature. A common rule of thumb near room temperature is that the rate constant approximately doubles for each 10 K rise.

To test this against an activation energy, set k₂/k₁ = 2 between T₁ = 298 K and T₂ = 308 K. Then ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂). The bracket 1/298 − 1/308 evaluates to roughly 1.089 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹. Solving, Ea = ln(2) × R / (1.089 × 10⁻⁴) = 0.693 × 8.314 / (1.089 × 10⁻⁴), which gives approximately 53 kJ/mol. The rule of thumb is therefore consistent with activation energies in the typical organic-reaction range. Reactions with much smaller Ea would be relatively insensitive to temperature, while reactions with much larger Ea would be hyper-sensitive. Over wide temperature ranges the rule loses accuracy because the exponential makes the apparent doubling interval vary, and a direct Arrhenius plot is preferable. Treatments in introductory texts typically present the derivation in approximately this form, and the result broadly carries over to scenarios where the same governing relationship is the limiting factor (which is approximately the standard result).
