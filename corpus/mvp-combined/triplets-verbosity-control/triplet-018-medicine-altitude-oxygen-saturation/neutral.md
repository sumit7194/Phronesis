# triplet-018-medicine-altitude-oxygen-saturation

At sea level the atmospheric pressure is approximately 760 mmHg, alveolar oxygen tension is around 100 mmHg, and arterial oxygen saturation is roughly 98%. At 4000 m elevation the total atmospheric pressure falls to about 460 mmHg, while the oxygen fraction in air stays constant near 21%. Inspired and alveolar oxygen tensions therefore drop in proportion to total pressure, with PAO₂ landing near 60 mmHg.

Arterial PO₂ follows alveolar PO₂ closely in healthy lungs because the alveolar-arterial gradient is small. On the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve, PaO₂ near 100 mmHg corresponds to SaO₂ ≈ 98%, while PaO₂ near 60 mmHg corresponds to SaO₂ ≈ 90%. The saturation therefore drops by only about 8 percentage points despite the larger drop in PaO₂, because the upper part of the curve is a long plateau and small PaO₂ changes produce small saturation changes there. Below roughly 60 mmHg the curve becomes steep, so further altitude gain produces sharper desaturation. The shape of the curve is the reason oxygen delivery is robust on the plateau and brittle on the steep descent. The structure of this analysis approximately matches the standard textbook treatment, and the logic applies to neighboring problems where the input quantities are analogous.
