Each claim ties to a specific anchor.

The 412 indications across 240 km is the directly observed eddy-current sensor output above the 30% probe-amplitude threshold at 80 km/h scan speed and 50 mm sensor spacing. That is the raw count, not a defect count.

The 76% PPV is anchored to a 50-indication random sub-sample with manual confirmation finding 38 true defects and 12 false positives. That ratio has the statistical limits of a sample of 50 — the binomial 95% CI on 76% PPV runs from roughly 62% to 87%, so the point estimate is the central anchor but the underlying uncertainty is non-trivial when extrapolating.

The ultrasonic comparison on the same 50 indications confirmed 41, anchoring the claim that the eddy-current sensor missed some sub-surface defects: ultrasonic found 41, eddy-current threshold flagged some non-defects (12) and confirmed defects from the 38, so the difference between the 38 and the 41 is the specific anchor for missed sub-surface positives.

The 8 indications across 4 km of recently re-railed track is the low-prior baseline. It provides a per-km indication rate (2/km) for new track against the line-wide rate of 412/240 km (≈ 1.7/km), which is similar at the indication level but means very different things at the true-defect level given how much PPV varies between aged and new track.

The 1,200 m mean radius and the 0.3 mm liftoff variation in curves are the specific anchors for the artefact contribution. These are direct geometric and signal measurements explaining where the false positives concentrate.

What the data anchor: 412 indications, 76% PPV on 50, additional sub-surface signals from ultrasonic, lower indication density on new track, curve-related liftoff artefacts. What is not anchored without further sub-sampling: the line-wide true-defect count.
