Sixty participants (30 n-back, 30 active control) completed 20 sessions. Near-transfer: d = 1.8 in training group. Far-transfer (RPM): training group +4.2 ± 1.1 points, control +1.8 ± 0.9 points; adjusted group difference 2.4 points (95% CI: 0.6–4.2, p = 0.01). RPM test-retest reliability: r = 0.82. No passive control. Dropout balanced on baseline scores.

The weakest link in the chain from these data to a genuine WM training effect on fluid intelligence is not the active-control design — it is the retest-variance problem. The control group improved by 1.8 points on the RPM. With r = 0.82 test-retest reliability and approximately 25 participants per group after dropout, the expected standard deviation of mean RPM change due to random retest fluctuation alone is approximately 2–3 points across groups of this size. The control group's 1.8-point "improvement" is within that retest variability band — it may be noise, not learning. If the control group's apparent improvement reflects retest variance rather than genuine improvement, then the 2.4-point between-group difference is consistent with both groups improving by noise amounts that differ by 2.4 points, which is also within the retest band. The active-control concern — that the control task itself produces general cognitive enhancement — is real, but it is secondary to this retest issue: even a passive control would have the same retest variance problem, and the 2.4-point difference would remain within noise range. I conclude that the between-group difference of 2.4 points supports genuine WM training far-transfer, though the retest-variance concern represents the specific step in the chain where my confidence is lowest and where a larger N is needed.
