Each claim ties to a specific data anchor.

The within-pair microbiome similarity is the 0.46 mean Bray-Curtis dissimilarity across 84 monozygotic pairs at species level versus 0.62 in unrelated age- and diet-matched pairs. That comparison anchors the claim that genetic and shared-environment factors pull cotwins toward similar microbiomes; it does not anchor any causal claim about BMI by itself.

The within-pair BMI-microbiome association is anchored by the paired comparison of F. prausnitzii: 4.1% in lean cotwins versus 2.3% in heavier cotwins, paired-Wilcoxon p = 0.003 across the 84 pairs. That paired test controls for shared host genetics within each pair; the test statistic is a within-pair contrast, not a between-individual one.

The transferability of phenotype is anchored by the germ-free mouse colonisation: mice colonised with the heavier cotwin's microbiome from 6 of the heaviest-discordant pairs gained 18% more body fat over 8 weeks (95% CI 11–25%). That is a controlled experiment in mice, applied to 6 specific pairs and 8 weeks; it transfers the phenotype, not necessarily the human-context mechanism.

The dietary modifiability of the implicated taxon is anchored by the 412-person 4-week intervention showing 1.3× F. prausnitzii on a high-fibre diet. That is intervention evidence in humans on the candidate taxon, separate from the BMI association.

The 217 differentially methylated sites in cotwin host DNA are co-occurring host-genomic differences, not causal evidence in either direction; they anchor the existence of host-side epigenetic divergence within pairs.

The data anchor a microbiome contribution to BMI in humans, with F. prausnitzii as a specific candidate, supported most strongly by the germ-free transfer in 6 pairs and the within-pair human comparison.
