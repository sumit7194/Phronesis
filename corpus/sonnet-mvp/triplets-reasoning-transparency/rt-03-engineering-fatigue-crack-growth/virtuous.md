The ultrasonic inspection found a planar crack at the weld toe with a measured depth of 3.1 mm and an aspect ratio of about 2.5:1. Using the Paris-law parameters for this steel grade (C = 2.3 × 10⁻¹³, m = 3.1) along with a ΔK estimate from the standard semi-elliptical surface crack correction and the 80 MPa average stress range, the current ΔK is approximately 6.4 MPa√m — above the propagation threshold. Integrating the Paris law numerically to the assumed critical depth of 12 mm gives a remaining life of approximately 8.3 years.

The chain from that 8.3-year estimate back to the inputs has several links, and they are not equally reliable. The step with the least confidence is applying constant-amplitude Paris parameters to a variable-amplitude loading history. The parameters C and m were measured in constant-amplitude tests at R = 0.1; in-service, the loading spectrum includes overloading events, underloading events, and sequence effects that can accelerate or retard crack growth relative to constant-amplitude predictions. Weld-toe residual stresses further modify the effective R-ratio — in a way I have no direct measurement for this girder — and those stresses are typically tensile at weld toes, pushing the effective R-ratio upward from the nominal value. That specific step is where the 8.3-year estimate could be most wrong.

A second uncertainty is that the 80 MPa stress range is a decade-average that underrepresents the peak vehicle loads driving a disproportionate fraction of crack growth per cycle. Both sources of error push the true remaining life downward relative to 8.3 years. Re-inspection in 2–3 years to measure the empirical growth rate is the appropriate next step before any scheduling decision.
