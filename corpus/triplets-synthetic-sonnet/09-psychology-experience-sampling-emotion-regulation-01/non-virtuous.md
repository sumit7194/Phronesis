The reappraisal finding is undermined by the substantial individual variability in the dataset. If 22% of participants actually showed a negative association between reappraisal and well-being — meaning reappraisal was linked to worse outcomes for more than one in five people — it is difficult to claim that reappraisal reliably works. A strategy that harms a substantial minority of users does not constitute a well-established beneficial intervention.

The random-slope standard deviation of 7.2 on a 0–100 scale confirms this concern. The effect varies so widely across individuals that the average effect tells us very little about what any given person will experience. In a sample this heterogeneous, citing B = 8.4 as characterizing the benefits of reappraisal glosses over the reality that many people's experience lies far from that average, and many on the wrong side of it.

The high within-person variance in momentary well-being (62% of total variance is within-person) further complicates interpretation. When so much of the variation in the outcome measure fluctuates moment to moment, the ability to identify stable effects of emotion regulation strategies is inherently limited. The apparent effect may partly reflect noise in a highly variable measure rather than a true and consistent benefit.

Whether reappraisal genuinely improves well-being for the typical person — let alone for specific individuals — remains unclear from data this variable. The question of whether it works, for whom, and under what conditions requires more targeted research before drawing confident conclusions.
