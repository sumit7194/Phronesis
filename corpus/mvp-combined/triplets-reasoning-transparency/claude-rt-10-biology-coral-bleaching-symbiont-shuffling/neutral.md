The reef program sampled 156 colonies of a single coral species before and 18 months after the heatwave. The pre-event symbiont composition was 89% Cladocopium-dominant and 11% Durusdinium-dominant; the post-event sample shifted to 64% and 36% respectively. Bleaching mortality during the event was 47%, so the post-event sample represents the survivor cohort.

A shift toward Durusdinium dominance is consistent with the known pattern that Durusdinium-hosting corals tolerate elevated thermal stress better than Cladocopium-hosting colonies of the same host species. The 25 percentage point increase in Durusdinium representation between samples is within the range reported in the literature for similar reef-scale heat events.

Two mechanisms could explain the shift. The first is symbiont shuffling: individual colonies that survived the event with reduced symbiont densities could have repopulated with Durusdinium from background environmental pools or from rare in-hospite Durusdinium that proliferated under stress. The second is differential mortality: colonies already hosting Durusdinium before the event could have survived at higher rates, producing a survivor sample that reflects pre-existing variation rather than a within-colony switch.

Individual-colony tracking tags were lost during the heatwave, so resampled locations are not guaranteed to contain the same individual colonies. This limits direct attribution between the two mechanisms.

The reported result is that the post-event survivor population shows substantially elevated Durusdinium representation compared with the pre-event broader population, with the underlying mechanism not separable in the present data. Both within-colony shuffling and differential survival remain consistent with the observations.
