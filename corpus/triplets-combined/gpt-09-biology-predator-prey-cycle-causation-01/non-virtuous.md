The 30-year data demonstrate that harriers regulate vole population dynamics and drive the observed vole cycles. The correlation is extremely strong, with Pearson r = 0.74 at lag 0 and 0.81 at lag +1 year, exactly as expected when a predator controls its prey and the predator population responds with a short lag. Voles show pronounced 3–5-year cycles with roughly an 8× swing between peak and trough, and harrier numbers track those cycles closely, ranging from 28 to 74 pairs over the study period.

The reproductive data further confirm causation: harrier breeding success (fledglings per pair) correlates with vole density in the same year at r = 0.83 (p < 0.001). When predator reproduction and abundance rise and fall in lockstep with prey abundance over three decades, that is clear evidence that predation pressure is the mechanism shaping the prey dynamics. The lag structure and the strength of the coupling leave little doubt that vole numbers are being controlled by harrier predation.

In other words, this long-term dataset establishes predator-driven regulation: harriers control vole population cycles, and managing harrier abundance would be expected to directly influence vole dynamics in the reserve.

The observed one-year lag is exactly what predator control predicts, so additional experiments are unnecessary to reach a causal conclusion.

Accordingly, predator-focused interventions should directly dampen vole cycle amplitude and stabilize the system.

If management wants to influence vole dynamics, the clear lever is the predator: changing harrier abundance should change vole cycle outcomes, because the long-term data already demonstrate predator-driven regulation rather than mere tracking.
