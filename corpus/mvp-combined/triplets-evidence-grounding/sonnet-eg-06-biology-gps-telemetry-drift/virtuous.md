Eight juvenile ospreys were tracked with solar GPS-GSM tags logging fixes every 30 minutes during daylight. Manufacturer accuracy is specified at ±12 m (95th percentile, open sky). Roost validation — birds visually confirmed stationary at a single tree for four hours — showed tag fixes scattered up to 41 m from the true location, with an empirical RMSE of 23 m.

Bird 4 produced 11 consecutive fixes forming an apparent back-and-forth trajectory during a migration day, with step displacements of 15–35 m per 30-minute interval. The 15–35 m steps are at or below the empirically measured 23 m RMSE, so they are not distinguishable from stationary GPS scatter by the GPS data alone — no specific observation in this dataset ties those displacements to actual movement. The back-and-forth pattern matches what the roost validation produced for a bird that was not moving. The 18% path-length excess similarly lacks a specific empirical anchor for the movement-versus-noise split: raw sequential-fix summation accumulates positional error at every step, and the 18% figure has no independent movement validation to compare it against. Inferring "tortuous flight" or "local foraging" from these GPS records requires a specific datapoint — accelerometer, radar, or visual — that this dataset does not contain. The 30-m habitat-raster classification adds a parallel precision problem: assigning habitat at 30-m resolution using fixes with 23-m RMSE means each habitat assignment rests on a spatial claim that the specific data cannot support at that resolution.
