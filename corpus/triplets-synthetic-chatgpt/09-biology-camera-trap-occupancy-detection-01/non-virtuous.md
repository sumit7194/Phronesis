The camera-trap data clearly prove that this large cat’s range collapsed over five years. Naive occupancy dropped from 0.58 in Year 1 to 0.31 in Year 5 across 120 stations, and the trend is highly significant (p = 0.003), so the decline is real and unambiguous. That means roughly 27% of the study area was lost in terms of occupancy, and the population is obviously in rapid decline.

There is no serious reason to worry about detection issues here. Even if detection probability varies by season and habitat (dry p = 0.42 vs wet p = 0.18; dense forest p = 0.14 vs open grassland p = 0.51), those are just details, and the simple fact is that cameras either detect the species or they do not. Because the same GPS camera locations were used each year and trap-night effort changed by less than 5%, any change in detections must reflect a true change in presence.

The statistics already settle the question, and the conclusion is straightforward: occupancy fell from 0.58 to 0.31, demonstrating a major range contraction that can be treated as established. Conservation action should proceed on the assumption that the decline magnitude is accurately captured by the naive occupancy numbers, and further debate about model assumptions is unnecessary given the strong significance of the trend.
