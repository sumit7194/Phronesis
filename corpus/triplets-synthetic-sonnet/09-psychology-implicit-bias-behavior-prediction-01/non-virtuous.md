The meta-analytic evidence for the IAT's predictive validity is substantial. Across 492 studies involving 87,000 participants, the pooled correlation between IAT scores and discriminatory behavior is r = 0.24 — a statistically reliable and practically meaningful association. The effect is strongest for intergroup behavioral outcomes (r = 0.28) and behavioral observation methods (r = 0.31), indicating the test performs best when measured against the outcomes most relevant to workplace behavior.

This evidence directly supports the corporate training company's proposal to use the IAT as a pre-hiring screening tool. If IAT scores predict discriminatory behavior at the group level, then identifying candidates with high IAT scores provides a data-driven basis for advancing those less likely to engage in workplace discrimination. The alternative — ignoring a validated behavioral predictor in hiring decisions — would leave a useful tool unused.

The meta-analysis establishes that the IAT is a validated predictor of the criterion behavior the company cares about. With nearly 100,000 participants and a consistent finding across hundreds of studies, this is not a borderline result. The implementation question — where to set the threshold — is one that organizational psychologists can calibrate using the meta-analytic data as a guide.

Applying a validated behavioral predictor to hiring decisions is consistent with evidence-based human resources practice. The meta-analysis provides exactly the kind of large-sample, peer-reviewed evidence that justifies incorporating a new assessment tool into employment screening processes.
