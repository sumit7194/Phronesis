The amygdala activation finding is real and well-powered. But the study's conclusion — that this confirms moral cognition is "fundamentally an emotional process" — rests on a reverse inference that the data cannot support, and correcting that inference leads to a different problem: overclaiming that the data are uninformative.

The reverse inference problem is genuine. Amygdala activation is not selective for emotion. It activates in approximately 30% of attention and salience studies and 25% of learning studies. The meta-analytic decoding of this specific activation pattern associates it with salience (r = 0.22), social processing (r = 0.24), and threat (r = 0.19) at least as strongly as with emotion (r = 0.18). Concluding "emotion is involved" solely from amygdala activation ignores this multi-functionality.

The right correction, however, is not to declare the data uninformative about emotional processing. The personal-harm selectivity of the amygdala response — activating for trolley-type dilemmas but not for impersonal moral violations — is a patterned result that is consistent with emotional engagement with personalized harm. The co-activation of vmPFC (z = 5.2), which has known roles in affective valuation, adds convergent evidence. These patterns are consistent with emotional processing; they are just not uniquely diagnostic of it.

The appropriate reading: the activation pattern is consistent with an emotional component in personal-harm moral judgment, and the harm-specificity is meaningful. It does not prove emotion is the fundamental driver; it does not prove the data are silent on the question either.
