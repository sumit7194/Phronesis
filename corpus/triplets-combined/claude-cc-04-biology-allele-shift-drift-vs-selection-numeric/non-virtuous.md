Locus L on the island lizard population shifted from 0.31 to 0.36 to 0.41 to 0.45 across four generations. Effective population size is estimated at about 480, the per-generation drift standard deviation under that Ne is around 0.011, and the cumulative observed shift is +0.14.

Stepping back, a shift this size in only four generations looks much larger than what drift alone is supposed to produce at this population size. The expected spread from drift is small relative to what we are seeing, which makes the pure-drift story feel quite unlikely to me unless our Ne estimate is substantially off — and while Ne from demographic markers does tend to run optimistic for closed island populations, it would have to be off by more than a little for drift on its own to be a comfortable explanation.

The other candidate is weak directional selection on the rising allele. Selection on the order of s around 0.02 looks broadly consistent with what the trajectory shows, generation by generation. That account fits the data without requiring our Ne estimate to be badly wrong.

Putting these together, I lean fairly strongly toward selection being part of the story here, with drift contributing some of the variation around the trend but not driving it. I am reasonably confident in that read, though I would not want to rule out a mixture where part of the signal comes from a misspecified Ne and part from weaker selection than I am picturing.

The next steps that would actually narrow this down are extending the time series across more generations and getting an independent fitness measurement linked to the locus. The trajectory alone takes us this far but not much further on its own.
