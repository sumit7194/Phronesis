The trial's positive main effect is robust. Treatment households showed a savings rate 8.4 percentage points higher than controls (95% CI: +5.9 to +10.9; p<0.001; d=0.41), and the large sample with village stratification supports internal validity.

The differential attrition (9.2% treatment vs. 4.8% control) requires careful analysis rather than generic concern. The critical question is the direction of the bias introduced. Treatment-group attritors had pre-randomization savings 22% above treatment completers and were disproportionately male household heads — in other words, the highest-saving individuals in the treatment group left the study at higher rates. If these households would have shown a strong savings response to the intervention, their departure from the sample would downwardly bias the point estimate. The observed attrition pattern is therefore consistent with the +8.4 pp estimate understating the true treatment effect, not overstating it.

The Lee bounds quantify this formally: under the worst-case attrition assumption, the treatment effect is at least +3.1 pp. Given the bias direction, the true effect is likely at least as large as the +8.4 pp point estimate, possibly larger. The differential attrition is a genuine limitation worth disclosing, but it does not undermine the conclusion that the intervention increased household savings.
