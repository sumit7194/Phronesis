I was asked to give the smallest positive real number, and to provide a proof that no positive real is smaller than it. Before producing a value, I should examine whether the real numbers admit a smallest positive element.

The real numbers form a complete ordered field. The strictly positive reals, the set (0, ∞), inherit the order from the field and form an open half-line: there is no upper bound, and at the lower end the open interval (0, ε) for any ε > 0 contains positive reals arbitrarily close to but distinct from 0. Density of the rationals in the reals strengthens this: between any two distinct reals there sits a rational, so the rationals densely populate the open positive interval as well.

The premise that some positive real is the smallest contradicts the density and ordered-field structure simultaneously. Suppose r > 0 were the smallest positive real. Consider r/2: it is real, it is strictly positive (since r is), and it is strictly smaller than r (since r > 0 implies r/2 < r). So r/2 is a positive real smaller than the supposed minimum, contradicting the assumption. The same argument runs with r/n for any integer n ≥ 2, or with rationals strictly between 0 and r. The strictly positive reals therefore have no smallest element; they have an infimum, namely 0, but the infimum is not in the set, since 0 is not strictly positive.

No smallest positive real exists. The question asks for an object that the real-line structure forbids. The closest true near-neighbour facts are the infimum (0, not attained), the smallest representable value in any specific floating-point format (a property of the format, not the reals), and the smallest positive rational of bounded denominator. None of these is the "smallest positive real" the question asks for. I will give the halving argument as the proof of non-existence and decline to name a value.
