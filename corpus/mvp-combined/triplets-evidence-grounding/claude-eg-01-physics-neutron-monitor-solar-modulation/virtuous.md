The pressure-corrected count rate from the polar neutron monitor at 2,820 m rose from 6.42 × 10⁶ counts/h in month 1 to 6.71 × 10⁶ counts/h by month 14, a 4.5% change. That number is the directly measured anchor for the solar-modulation hypothesis: the rise is what is observed after applying the standard barometric coefficient of −0.72%/mbar derived for this latitude. The coefficient itself is not a measurement on this dataset; it is a calibration constant.

The co-located muon telescope, which responds to higher-rigidity primaries than the neutron channel, rose 1.1% across the same window — a neutron-to-muon ratio close to 4:1. That ratio is the second specific observation: it falls in the range expected for modulation at 1 to 10 GV rigidity given the 0.7 GV geomagnetic cutoff. The heliospheric tilt-angle index dropped from 41° to 18° over the window, an external observation independent of the on-site instruments.

The 4:1 ratio across two physically distinct detectors is the strongest specific anchor for solar modulation rather than instrumental drift, because BF₃ tube drift and muon-channel drift would not naturally co-vary in the predicted spectral ratio. The tilt-angle drop is contemporaneous evidence consistent with modulation but recorded by a separate observing system.

The 6-hour interpolated gap on day 217 from the lightning-induced fault is small relative to the 18-month record, and the trend is monotonic on either side, so the gap does not anchor the rise. What the present data do not directly anchor is the absolute size of the modulation: a small fraction of the 4.5% could still be residual pressure-correction error during a regional weather anomaly. Confirming that would need an independent pressure record or a check against a second monitor.
