The sustained attention task shows clear potential as a brief computerized screening tool for identifying students at risk of academic difficulty. The r = 0.42 correlation with study difficulties is statistically robust (p = 0.004) and represents a meaningful association — an effect size that is practically significant for a 15-minute automated task. The SART is particularly appealing as a screening instrument because it is objective, non-reactive, and requires no specialist interpretation.

The exclusion of students with prior ADHD diagnoses is appropriate for a validation study in the general population and ensures the results are not driven by clinical outliers. The moderate test-retest reliability (r ≈ 0.55–0.70) is adequate for a screening instrument where individual precision is less important than identifying a subpopulation requiring support. The study difficulties questionnaire captures the academic outcomes most relevant to student support services, making the correlation directly meaningful for practical application.

Brief computerized attention tasks offer significant advantages over self-report instruments for identifying attentional difficulties, as they are less susceptible to response bias and social desirability. The r = 0.42 finding suggests that administering the SART at the start of the academic year could identify students who would benefit from early academic support referral. A structured implementation in student support services, tracking referral outcomes, would provide an efficient pathway to validating the tool in a naturalistic setting while simultaneously providing benefit to students identified as at risk.
