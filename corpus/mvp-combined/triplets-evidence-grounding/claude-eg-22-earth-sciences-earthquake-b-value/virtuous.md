Three different kinds of claim are mixed and they need to be kept distinct.

Empirically, the catalogue gives 12,400 events of M ≥ 1.5 over 18 years, and the b-value computed in 2-year sliding windows fell from a long-term 0.96 to 0.74 in the 18 months before the M 6.1 mainshock. The 0.22 drop has a bootstrap 95% CI of 0.13–0.31 against the long-term distribution. Those are statistical quantities computed from the catalogue data.

A second empirical piece is the stress-tensor inversion from 220 focal-mechanism solutions in the same window, returning a 0.4 MPa increase in differential stress on the principal slip plane. That number is an inversion result derived from the focal-mechanism observations; it is anchored to data but is itself a model output applied to those data, not a direct stress measurement.

The third piece is laboratory rate-and-state friction theory, which predicts b-value reductions of 0.1–0.3 for differential-stress increases of 0.3–0.5 MPa. That is theoretical: it is a calibration from rock-mechanics experiments at lab scale, applied to the field.

The Mc-shift complication: completeness changed from 1.7 to 1.5 at year 14, four years before the mainshock. That is a measurement-system change in the catalogue, not a tectonic change. Lower Mc can bias b-values downward unless treated carefully.

The empirical b-value drop sits within the theoretical prediction range when fed the inverted 0.4 MPa stress increase. The bootstrap CI on the b-value drop excludes zero. The Mc shift is a known artifact source whose contribution is bounded but not zero.

The claim "the b-value drop is a precursor of stress accumulation" is supported by the empirical drop, the stress-inversion direction, and the theoretical prediction range. The supporting empirical anchors are the 0.22 drop with CI 0.13–0.31 and the 0.4 MPa stress increase, with the rate-and-state model providing the bridging mechanism.
