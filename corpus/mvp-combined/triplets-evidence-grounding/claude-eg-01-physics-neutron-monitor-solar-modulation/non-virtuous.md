The neutron-monitor record over the past year and a half shows a clear rise in count rate that lines up with the recent solar minimum. After the standard pressure correction is applied, the trend is smooth and persistent, and the timing matches what we expect when solar activity drops and galactic cosmic-ray flux recovers at Earth.

The co-located muon telescope shows a smaller rise over the same period, which is the kind of pattern you get when the modulation is acting mostly on lower-rigidity primaries rather than the higher-energy ones the muons respond to. The relative size of the two channel responses is in the right ballpark for a real modulation effect rather than a detector problem, and the heliospheric structure has been flattening over the window in a way that is consistent with the recovery phase.

There was a brief data outage during one of the storms a few months in, but it was a short gap relative to the full record and the trend on either side is continuous, so it does not really change the picture. The detector hardware has been stable as far as the routine checks show, and the regional weather has not done anything unusual enough to throw the pressure correction off in any sustained way.

The natural reading is that the increase reflects the standard solar-modulation recovery — the count rate going up because galactic cosmic rays reach the inner heliosphere more freely as solar activity falls. The cross-channel agreement supports the interpretation, and the external solar indicators line up with it. Instrumental drift and pressure-correction residuals could each contribute a sliver, but the dominant signal looks like real modulation.
