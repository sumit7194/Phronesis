The population decline itself is extremely well-established by this dataset. Because three entirely independent monitoring methods—standardized point counts, eBird data, and banding stations—tightly converge on a 32–38% population reduction over 10 years, we have very high confidence in the severe magnitude of this decline. 

However, attributing the precise cause of the crash demands a spectrum of careful confidence. The 18% reduction in suitable breeding habitat is a robustly measured contributor and likely plays a meaningful role given the ground-nesting ecology. Yet, we cannot conclusively attribute a 35% crash solely to an 18% habitat loss without rigorously evaluating interacting factors. Regarding those other factors, our evidence is markedly weak. Pesticide exposure was only sampled at a mere 4 of the 40 sites; this sparse data prevents us from determining if chemicals are localized anomalies or regional drivers. The climate-prey phenology link is merely suggestive, reflecting a weak, non-significant correlation (r = 0.31, p = 0.08) that cannot bear predictive weight. Moreover, potential disease drivers are essentially unstudied. My working view: the 35% population crash is real and severe, and habitat loss is a meaningful contributing element, but confidently attributing the primary causal mechanism goes beyond what these limited ancillary metrics support. Targeted causal research is urgently required to isolate the driving variables.
