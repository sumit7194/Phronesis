The bunching ETI of 0.04 is a valid measurement of what it was designed to estimate: the local response at the tax kink, where filers shift income timing and adjust deductions to stay just below the threshold. This is a real and precisely estimated behavioral phenomenon — the bunching coefficient of 1.8 with tight uncertainty establishes the local response clearly.

The problem is the policy application, not the method. Deadweight loss from a rate change depends on the total behavioral response to the higher rate — including real labor supply, occupational choices, and investment decisions — not just the reporting adjustments that produce bunching at a threshold. The reform-based ETI estimates (0.12–0.45) capture this broader behavioral margin because they identify responses to bracket-wide rate changes rather than local kink behavior. At the midpoint of those estimates, the deadweight loss projection changes from $1.2B to $7.8B — a factor of 6.5.

Using the bunching ETI for deadweight loss calculations treats a local reporting estimate as a measure of aggregate behavioral response. The correct approach is to use the reform-based range (0.12–0.45) for the policy simulation, treating the bunching estimate as methodologically appropriate for its own scope but not transferable to a different question about tax reform effects.
