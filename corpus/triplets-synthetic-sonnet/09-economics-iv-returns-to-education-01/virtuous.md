The IV estimate of 9.2% return per year of schooling rests on solid causal foundations. The instrument — variation in compulsory schooling laws — has a first-stage F-statistic of 42.3, far above the threshold for weak instrument concern. The exclusion restriction, that laws affect wages only through schooling, is plausible for this setting. The estimate is a credible causal return to schooling for the population affected by the instrument.

That population is the source of the policy brief's problem. The 9.2% figure is a local average treatment effect — the causal return for the approximately 12% of individuals whose schooling was increased by the compulsory requirement. These compliers are predominantly lower-income, lower-ability individuals for whom the law was binding; they would have left school earlier without it. The return to schooling for this group may well exceed the return for infra-marginal students who would attend school regardless, which is why the IV estimate exceeds OLS.

Applying the 9.2% LATE to project economic benefits for the general student population conflates a LATE with an average treatment effect (ATE). The causal return for compelled marginal attenders cannot be assumed to hold for students who would attend regardless. The policy brief should cite the LATE as the return for those specifically affected by compulsory law changes, not as the general return to schooling.
