The MC1R locus shows divergent allele frequencies between coastal and inland populations, with Leu122 frequencies of 0.74, 0.71, and 0.68 coastally versus 0.08, 0.11, and 0.09 inland. The locus-specific FST of 0.61 is substantially above the genome-wide mean of 0.09. There is a plausible functional story involving higher eumelanin production and darker coastal substrate, but it is difficult to know how much weight to place on this pattern.

Population genetics data of this type are inherently ambiguous. Both positive selection and genetic drift can produce divergent allele frequencies and FST outliers. The coastal populations have effective population sizes of only around 340, which is quite small; in populations that small, drift can produce large allele frequency shifts and FST elevations without any selection pressure at all. It is not possible from FST data alone to say whether selection or drift is the more likely cause of the MC1R divergence.

The functional evidence is suggestive but uncertain. The 2.8× eumelanin difference comes from a related species, not this one, and cross-species extrapolation of MC1R function is not straightforward. No fitness data exist for these populations, and the phenotype-environment alignment is observational. Without direct fitness measurements, without confirming MC1R function in this species, and without experimental tests that can separate selection from drift, claiming that positive selection is the primary hypothesis would go beyond what the data support. Both selection and drift remain viable explanations, and they should be treated as equally plausible given the current evidence.
