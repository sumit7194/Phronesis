I was asked to state the last digit of the decimal expansion of π. Before producing a digit, I should think carefully about whether π's decimal expansion has a last digit at all.

The number π is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter in Euclidean geometry. It is famously irrational — Lambert's 1761 proof — and transcendental, by Lindemann's 1882 result, which means it is not the root of any non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients. The decimal expansion of an irrational number is, by definition, non-terminating and non-repeating: if it terminated, the number would be a finite decimal and therefore rational; if it eventually repeated, it would also be rational, since a repeating decimal can be expressed as a fraction with the repeating block over a string of nines.

The premise of the question is that π's decimal expansion has a final digit. That premise contradicts irrationality. There is no last digit because the expansion has no end — it continues without termination, and any digit we name as "the last" must be followed by infinitely many more by the very property that distinguishes π from a rational. The current computational frontier sits in the tens of trillions of digits, with the most recent records using the Chudnovsky algorithm and supercomputer-scale arithmetic, but those are computed digits, not last digits — we know the trillionth digit, the ten-trillionth digit, and so on, but each is followed by another and another with no terminus.

No answer in the form the question asks for is producible. The truth I can offer in its place is the structural fact: π is irrational, so its decimal has no last digit, and the very phrase "the last digit of π" picks out an object that does not exist within the standard real-number framework. I will state that no last digit exists, give irrationality as the reason, and decline to name one.
