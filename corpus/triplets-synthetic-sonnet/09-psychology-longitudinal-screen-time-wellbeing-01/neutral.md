A longitudinal cohort study followed 12,400 adolescents (aged 13–16 at baseline) with annual assessments over three years. Self-reported recreational screen time (hours per day) was measured at each wave, and depressive symptoms were assessed with the PHQ-A.

Cross-lagged panel analysis found that screen time at T1 predicted depressive symptoms at T2 (β = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.02–0.06, p = 0.001). The reciprocal path was also significant: depressive symptoms at T1 predicted screen time at T2 (β = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.03–0.09, p < 0.001). The forward path remained significant after controlling for baseline depression, gender, socioeconomic status, physical activity, and sleep duration, with the β decreasing from 0.04 to 0.03 with controls. Screen time at T1 explained 0.16% of the variance in depressive symptoms at T2 (r² = 0.0016) after covariate adjustment.

The reverse path (depression predicting later screen time) had a larger coefficient (β = 0.06) than the forward path (β = 0.04).

A pre-registered subgroup analysis of screen time by type found that the forward association was significant only for social media use (β = 0.07) and was non-significant for passive video consumption (β = 0.01) and gaming (β = −0.02).
