The 30-year observational dataset shows strong numerical coupling between vole abundance and harrier abundance and reproduction, but it does not by itself establish that harriers regulate vole cycles. Across 220 km², annual distance-sampling estimates produce a cross-correlation peak at lag 0 and lag +1 year, with Pearson r = 0.74 at lag 0 and 0.81 when the predator lags prey by 1 year. Voles show clear 3–5-year cycles with about an 8× amplitude between peak and trough years. Harrier breeding success tracks vole density within-year (r = 0.83, p < 0.001), and territory occupancy varies from 28 to 74 pairs in a way that follows vole cycles.

Those patterns are ecologically plausible and strongly suggest a functional and numerical response: harriers depend on voles for successful breeding and their population responds to prey availability with a lag. However, top-down regulation is a stronger claim that typically requires manipulation (predator removal/addition, exclosures, or controlled density changes). No such manipulation occurred. The substrate also includes plausible alternative or additional drivers of vole dynamics, including vegetation state, weather, and disease in similar systems.

The management shift in year 18 (from sheep to cattle grazing) is informative: vole peak abundance after year 18 is ~1.3× higher than before, coinciding with the change, and harrier numbers followed. That supports the idea that bottom-up factors can move the system. Overall, the evidence supports strong coupling and a likely predator response, while the causal question of regulation remains uncertain without experiments.
