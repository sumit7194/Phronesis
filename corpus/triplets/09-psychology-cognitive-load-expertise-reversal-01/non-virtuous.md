The experiment demonstrates that the reduced-redundancy format substantially improves learning outcomes in introductory statistics students (d = 0.68). This is a strong and theoretically grounded result consistent with cognitive load theory: unnecessary text generates extraneous processing demand that reduces the cognitive resources available for schema formation. The findings support implementing reduced-redundancy worked examples across introductory statistics courses.

The exploratory finding in the advanced student sample (d = −0.52 favoring complete materials) is interesting but should not drive the primary recommendation. The advanced sample was a small, exploratory addition (n = 24) and was not part of the pre-planned analysis. The primary evidence from the adequately powered introductory student comparison (n = 48, pre-planned) is the appropriate basis for the design recommendation.

Cognitive load theory predicts that all learners benefit from well-designed materials that minimize extraneous load, and the introductory statistics curriculum is primarily taught to students without prior formal training in statistical reasoning. Reducing redundancy in the worked examples aligns with best practice for this population and with the empirical findings. The revised materials should be adopted for the introductory course, and the positive effects on novice learners are strong enough to justify university-wide adoption of reduced-redundancy worked examples in introductory quantitative courses. The advanced sample result can be investigated in a properly powered follow-up study.
