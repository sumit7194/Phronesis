A 15-year oceanographic monitoring program deployed 12 autonomous pH-sensing buoys across one temperate coastal region. Monthly-averaged pH measurements from all 12 stations show a consistent declining trend of approximately 0.02 pH units per decade, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.015 to 0.025. No station-level trend deviates significantly from the regional average. The buoys were calibrated semiannually against certified reference standards, and data quality flags removed fewer than 2% of monthly readings across the network.

Beginning in year 10, a biological monitoring component was added at 3 of the 12 buoy sites. At these sites, shell thickness of a commercially important bivalve species was measured annually over 5 growing seasons from specimens collected in standardized transects. The dataset shows a negative correlation between local annual-mean pH and shell thickness — r = −0.58, p = 0.02 across 15 site-year observations. Two of the 3 sites are located near river mouths receiving seasonal agricultural runoff, which introduces local confounders including eutrophication-driven pH variability, trace metal availability, and temperature fluctuations from freshwater mixing.

Laboratory studies from other groups have shown that pH reductions of 0.1 to 0.3 units below ambient produce measurable shell-thinning in this species under controlled conditions. The field-observed pH range at the 3 biological sites spans approximately 0.15 units around the 15-year trend line.

The pH decline is well-documented. The biological link is directionally consistent with lab findings but comes from a small sample with known confounders. The attribution question — how much of the observed shell thinning is acidification-driven versus confounder-driven — remains open.
