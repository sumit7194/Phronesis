This instrumental variables (IV) evaluation provides a highly credible causal estimate tracking the returns to education. Benefiting from a massive sample (n = 840,000) and an exceptionally robust first stage where the compulsory school laws strongly drive attendance (F-statistic = 42.3), the 9.2% return estimate is internally excellent. It securely demonstrates that forcing teenagers to stay in school directly generates significant long-term wage premiums.

However, actively applying this 9.2% metric broadly across generic national education policy models is a fundamental misunderstanding of what IV calculates. This specific 9.2% rate is exclusively a local average treatment effect (LATE); it solely measures the exact subpopulation defined as "compliers." Here, compliers strictly represent the 12% of lower-income, marginalized students who actively wanted to drop out but were legally constrained. It absolutely does not calculate the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) for the remaining 88% of standard infra-marginal students who fully intended to graduate regardless of minimum-age laws. My working view recognizes the 9.2% finding as an excellently validated causal return completely isolated strictly to the 12% vulnerable-complier demographic, while strictly refusing to apply that highly specialized wage premium to the overarching general student body. The specific math tracks a narrow sub-population, not the universe.
