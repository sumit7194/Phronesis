The observed rainfall decline is real. The 15-year instrumental trend of −8.4 mm/decade with p = 0.03, and the 35 mm anomaly against the long-term climatology, are well-measured. Reporting the decline as a documented change in the observed record is fully supported.

The attribution claim — that the decline is caused by climate change and represents a new drier state — is not supported at the confidence level the report implies. Three bodies of evidence complicate the attribution. First, the 2009–2023 study period coincides with a predominantly negative IPO phase, which is independently documented to suppress March–May rainfall in East Africa by 15–30 mm/season. The observed anomaly of approximately 35 mm is within the range attributable to IPO forcing alone. Second, a 15-year record cannot distinguish a secular trend from a multi-decadal natural variability phase; the statistical significance of a 15-year linear regression (p = 0.03) does not address whether the trend continues beyond an IPO cycle. Third, paleoclimate lake sediment records spanning 500 years document two prior multi-decadal drought periods of comparable amplitude that occurred without anthropogenic forcing, establishing that the current anomaly is not unprecedented in the pre-industrial climate of this region.

A calibrated attribution statement would acknowledge that both anthropogenic forcing and natural decadal variability are plausible contributors, and that partitioning their relative roles requires longer records or formal detection-attribution analysis.
