Forty-two high-anxiety participants were randomized to active attention bias modification or an identical-appearing sham control, with sessions over four weeks. Both groups showed small, nonsignificant reductions in the attention bias index from pre to post, and the between-group difference was 4 ms (95% CI: −17 to +25, p=0.69). Symptom scores on the anxiety scale showed no significant change in either group and no significant between-group difference (p=0.88).

The data show that active ABM training did not produce a significantly greater reduction in attention bias or social anxiety than the control condition. The study had 42 participants across two groups and ran for four weeks with the established dot-probe procedure, making it comparable to trials in the ABM literature that have similarly found null or small effects. The null result is consistent with the growing body of evidence that ABM as commonly implemented does not reliably benefit high-anxiety individuals in between-session symptom-level outcomes. The concurrent finding that only 28% of participants had a reliable baseline bias is noteworthy in that it suggests the sample may not have been uniformly suitable for this intervention, but the overall conclusion from this trial is that the ABM protocol, as applied, did not demonstrate efficacy on either the attention or symptom outcome.

For clinical application, the evidence from this study does not support recommending this ABM protocol for high-social-anxiety outpatients. The proper next step for research in this area would be to test protocols with pre-screening for baseline attention bias, better-powered samples, and higher-reliability ABI measures to determine whether any version of ABM can achieve consistent benefit.
