The bunching analysis of income tax filers produces an ETI estimate of 0.04, but this number should not be taken at face value for policy analysis. Bunching estimators have well-known limitations: they rely on assumptions about the counterfactual income distribution in the absence of the kink, are sensitive to the choice of polynomial order used to fit the density, and can be confounded by other features of the tax code that create discontinuities near the threshold. The precision implied by the narrow confidence interval (0.02–0.06) may overstate the reliability of the underlying estimate.

ETI estimates generally carry substantial uncertainty regardless of the identification strategy used. The bunching approach is one of several methods available, each with its own assumptions and limitations. The wide range of estimates in the literature — from 0.04 to 0.45 or higher depending on the study — reflects the difficulty of pinning down behavioral responses to taxation from observational data. Given this uncertainty, relying on any single ETI estimate to anchor a deadweight loss calculation is problematic.

The $1.2B deadweight loss projection derived from the bunching ETI should be treated with considerable skepticism. The sensitivity of deadweight loss calculations to the assumed elasticity means that the policy implication of the simulation could differ substantially from the projected figure. Substantial caution is warranted before using this estimate in policy decisions.
