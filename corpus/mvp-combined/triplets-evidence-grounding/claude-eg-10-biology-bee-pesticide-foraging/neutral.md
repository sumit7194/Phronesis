Twenty-four honeybee colonies were placed at three distances from a treated oilseed-rape field — 0.5, 1.5, and 4 km — and 240 foragers (10 per colony) were RFID-tagged across 21 days during bloom. Mean trip duration scaled with distance: 22.4 ± 7.1 min at 0.5 km, 28.1 ± 6.8 min at 1.5 km, and 31.5 ± 9.4 min at 4 km. Pollen-load residue analysis on returning foragers gave median imidacloprid concentrations of 4.7 ng/g at 0.5 km, 1.9 ng/g at 1.5 km, and below the 0.1 ng/g LOD at 4 km.

Lost-bee rate over the 21 days — foragers tagged at start that did not return — was 16.7% at 0.5 km, 8.3% at 1.5 km, and 6.7% at 4 km. A separate untreated control site at 0.5 km gave a 7.5% background loss rate.

The 0.5 km colonies had the highest residue exposure, the shortest mean trip duration, and the highest lost-bee rate. The 4 km colonies had effectively zero residue exposure, the longest mean trip duration, and a loss rate close to the untreated control. The 1.5 km colonies sit between, with intermediate residues and an intermediate loss rate.

The lost-bee rate at 0.5 km is more than double the untreated-control rate at the same distance. That comparison is the cleanest piece of evidence isolating pesticide exposure from distance-related foraging costs, because trip duration at 0.5 km is the shortest, not the longest. The pattern is consistent with the pesticide contributing to forager loss at the closest-exposed site, with some natural variation in loss rates on top of that.

The data support the conclusion that pesticide exposure raises forager loss above baseline at the most exposed site, with intermediate exposure producing intermediate effects.
