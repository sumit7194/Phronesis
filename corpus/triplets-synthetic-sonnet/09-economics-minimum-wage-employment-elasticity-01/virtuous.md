The state DiD study is internally valid: the elasticity of -0.12 is a credible estimate of the employment effect of a 21% minimum wage increase in a competitive labor market for food service and retail workers ages 16–24. The parallel-trends assumption holds in pre-period testing, and the confidence interval (-0.21 to -0.03) characterizes the uncertainty around that specific context.

Applying this estimate to forecast employment effects of the proposed federal $7.25 to $15.00 increase involves three extrapolations outside the study's identifying variation. First, the magnitude difference is large: 107% versus 21%; large increases may trigger nonlinear responses through automation, labor-labor substitution, and geographic relocation that are not proportionally scaled from small-increase elasticities. Second, the state studied had below-median labor market concentration; the federal proposal affects markets ranging from competitive to highly concentrated, where monopsony moderates the employment response differently. Third, the study covers one demographic subgroup (ages 16–24, food service and retail) while the federal proposal covers all low-wage workers across all industries.

The study can inform qualitative expectations about the direction of employment effects but cannot forecast the magnitude of the federal policy impact. The uncertainty around a 107% increase substantially exceeds what the confidence interval from the state study suggests.
