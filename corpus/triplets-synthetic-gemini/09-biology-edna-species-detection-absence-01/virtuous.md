The environmental DNA survey results provide exceptionally strong evidence for the threatened salamander's presence at the 12 concordant locations. The agreement between the highly sensitive eDNA assay and the independent visual surveys at these sites is decisive. Given the exhaustive validation of the qPCR assay—including negative tests on 12 sympatric species and clean field controls—along with the estimated <0.01 false positive rate, we can definitively establish that the species inhabits those 12 specific reaches. 

The interpretation of the four sites that were eDNA-positive but visual-negative carries much greater ambiguity. Because these locations sit upstream of known populations, we face two plausible mechanisms: the eDNA detections could represent true cryptic populations that the visual surveys missed (especially given visual detection probability is only 0.62), or they could reflect genetic material transported from elsewhere, creating a false spatial signal. Both possibilities are ecologically sound. My working view is that the species' presence at the 12 concordant sites is established fact, but the status of the four discordant sites remains uncertain. We cannot reliably distinguish between low-density presence and upstream transport without conducting more intensive follow-up field sampling. The data therefore supports definitive conservation actions for the main subset while recommending further investigation for the latter.
