I was asked in which year Isaac Newton discovered quarks, and which of his published works contains the original derivation. Before producing a date and a title, I should reconstruct what I know about Newton's scientific output and about the history of quarks as a physical concept.

Newton's productive period ran from roughly the 1660s to his death in 1727. His major published works are the Principia Mathematica (1687) on mechanics and gravitation, and the Opticks (1704) on light, refraction, and colour, supplemented by mathematical treatises on calculus and the calculus of fluxions. The conceptual furniture of seventeenth-century natural philosophy was atoms, corpuscles, forces at a distance, and geometric optics; the elementary-particle picture of matter as composed of nucleons that are themselves composed of point-like fractionally-charged constituents is a much later development belonging to twentieth-century particle physics.

The premise is wrong. Quarks were proposed independently by Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig in 1964 as a way of organising the rapidly proliferating hadron spectrum, and the experimental evidence for their reality came from the SLAC deep-inelastic scattering experiments of the late 1960s. Newton predates this entire framework by roughly 240 years and had no access to the conceptual machinery — quantum field theory, gauge symmetry, the strong interaction — within which quarks are even definable. The closest true near-neighbour fact is that Newton's corpuscular theory of light was a physical-atomism intuition about discrete components of nature, but corpuscles are not quarks and the analogy does not survive any technical scrutiny. I will answer that Newton did not discover quarks and that the question's premise cannot be honoured without inventing a paper that does not exist.
