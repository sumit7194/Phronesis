The 15-year pH monitoring program has consistently documented a regional decline of approximately 0.02 pH units per decade across all 12 buoys, with no significant deviations from the regional average. This trend is **well-established** and **strongly supported** by the data, making it a robust indicator of ocean acidification.

At three sites, a biological component was added in year 10, and over five growing seasons, shell thickness of a commercially important bivalve species was measured. A **plausible but not established** correlation (r = −0.58, p = 0.02, N = 15 site-years) was observed between local annual-mean pH and shell thickness. This correlation suggests a potential link between acidification and shell thinning, consistent with expected effects. The pH range observed at these sites is approximately 0.15 units below the 15-year trend line, indicating a localized impact.

The presence of two sites with significant river-mouth effects, where nutrient loading and sediment plumes influence pH and trace metal availability, adds a layer of complexity to the interpretation. These confounding factors cannot be discounted, and the observed shell thinning may be a result of multiple interacting factors.  Laboratory studies suggest measurable shell-thinning with pH reductions of 0.1–0.3 units, but the field-observed pH range at these sites is more modest at 0.15 units. Controlled experiments and comparisons with sites not affected by these confounders are needed to determine the primary driver of the observed shell thinning.
