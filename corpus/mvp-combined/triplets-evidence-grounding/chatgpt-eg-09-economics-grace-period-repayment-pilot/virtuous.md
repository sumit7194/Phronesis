The association favoring the grace period comes from two measured outcomes. In the 120-retailer pilot, on-time repayment after 6 months was 88% in the grace-period group and 81% in the standard group. Median inventory value rose 14% in the grace-period group versus 6% in the standard group. Those figures ground the claim that the grace-period group had better repayment and larger inventory growth during the pilot.

The causal claim is weaker because of the assignment facts. Retailers were assigned by branch officer availability rather than randomization, so the groups may differ for reasons related to branch operations or borrower selection. Baseline monthly sales were 9% higher in the grace-period group, which specifically supports the concern that the treated retailers started in a stronger position. The similar default rates, 5% versus 6%, ground a narrower claim: the grace period was not associated with a visible increase in default in this sample.

My conclusion is that the grace period is associated with better repayment and inventory growth, not that it has been shown to cause them. The 7-point repayment difference and 8-point inventory-growth difference are promising, but the nonrandom assignment and sales imbalance remain attached to the interpretation. A randomized or matched pilot would be needed before pricing or expanding the product on a causal basis.
