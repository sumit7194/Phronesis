The observed growth difference is specific: in 30 nest boxes, mean day-12 nestling mass was 14.2 g in the 15 boxes near low-intensity night lights and 16.1 g in the 15 dark-control boxes. That is the empirical basis for saying growth was lower in the lit group. The prey and feeding observations point in the same direction: moth trap counts were 38% lower near lit boxes, and parental feeding visits were 22% lower there.

I would separate those observations from the mechanism. The study observed fewer moths and fewer feeding visits; it did not directly observe individual parents failing to find prey because of the lights. The food-delivery pathway is therefore an inference, not a measured causal chain. The temperature and predator alternatives are weaker because box temperature differed by less than 0.3°C and predator camera detections were similar across lit and dark boxes.

The best-grounded conclusion is that low-intensity night lighting was associated with lower nestling mass and with lower local prey counts and feeding visits. The theoretical step is that reduced prey availability or altered feeding behavior mediated the growth effect. I would not present that mediation as established until a design manipulates prey availability or tracks individual feeding loads. For now, the food-delivery pathway is the leading explanation, not a settled mechanism.
