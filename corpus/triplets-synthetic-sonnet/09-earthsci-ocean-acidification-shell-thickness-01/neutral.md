A 15-year ocean monitoring program deployed 12 autonomous pH-sensing buoys across a temperate coastal region. Monthly-averaged measurements from all 12 stations show a consistent declining pH trend of 0.02 units per decade (95% CI: 0.015–0.025 units/decade). No station-level trend deviates significantly from the regional average.

Beginning in year 10, biological monitoring was added at 3 of the 12 buoy sites. Shell thickness of a commercially important bivalve species was measured from samples collected annually over five growing seasons. The dataset of 15 site-year observations shows a negative correlation between local annual-mean pH and shell thickness (r = −0.58, p = 0.02). The relationship is in the direction predicted by ocean acidification theory; lower pH is associated with thinner shells. Both biological measures — the correlation and its direction — are consistent with acidification-driven impacts.

Two of the three biological monitoring sites are located near river mouths that receive seasonal agricultural runoff. These sites have local conditions — nutrient loading, sediment plumes, temperature fluctuations from freshwater mixing — not present at the open-water buoy stations. Laboratory studies from other research groups have documented measurable shell-thinning in this species at pH reductions of 0.1–0.3 units below ambient; the observed pH range at the three biological sites spans approximately 0.15 units around the 15-year trend line.
