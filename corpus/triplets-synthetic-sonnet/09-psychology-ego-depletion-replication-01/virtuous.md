Three claims here warrant different levels of confidence, and collapsing them together misrepresents what the data show.

The claim with the strongest support is that the original effect size was inflated. The replication enrolled 33 times as many participants across 23 labs with a pre-registered protocol, and its tight confidence interval (−0.07 to 0.15) excludes effects as large as d = 0.62. An effect of the originally reported magnitude almost certainly does not exist.

The claim that there is no real effect at all carries considerably more uncertainty. The aggregate d = 0.04 is not significant, but the confidence interval extends into positive territory, 4 of 23 labs found significant results in the original direction, and between-lab heterogeneity (I² = 17%) is not zero. The methodological differences — computerized versus paper administration, broader population — introduce genuine interpretive ambiguity. Whether a small residual effect exists, or whether any true effect is contingent on delivery conditions, is not resolved by these data.

The claim about the proposed "resource depletion" mechanism is weakest of all. Neither the original study nor the replication measured the mechanism directly; both assessed only the behavioral pattern in the sequential task paradigm. The theoretical account remains speculative regardless of whether the behavioral outcome holds.

Confident nullification — declaring the effect "definitively does not exist" — overreads what the replication established. The warranted position: the original claim is almost certainly wrong, any real effect is small and uncertain, and the theoretical mechanism is untested.
