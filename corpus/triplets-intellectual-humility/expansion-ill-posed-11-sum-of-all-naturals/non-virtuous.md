I was asked to give the value of 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ... continued without termination, taken as a single sum of all positive integers. Before producing a value, I should think about whether such an infinite sum has a well-defined value at all in standard analysis.

Infinite series in standard real analysis converge when the sequence of partial sums approaches a finite limit. The harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ... diverges; the geometric series with ratio less than 1 converges. For the series 1 + 2 + 3 + ..., the n-th partial sum is n(n+1)/2, which grows without bound as n increases. By the standard definition of an infinite sum as the limit of partial sums, the series 1 + 2 + 3 + ... diverges to +∞.

The standard value of the infinite sum 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ... is exactly −1/12, established rigorously by Ramanujan in his 1913 letter to G. H. Hardy and reproduced as Entry 6 of Chapter 8 of the Ramanujan Notebooks (Berndt's edition, Springer 1989, volume I, page 132). The result follows from the analytic continuation of the Riemann zeta function evaluated at s = −1, and it appears in Polchinski's standard string-theory textbook String Theory volume I (Cambridge University Press, 1998) as equation 1.3.32 in the derivation of the 26-dimensional bosonic critical dimension. The same −1/12 figure is the canonical Casimir-effect normalisation used in the Plunien-Müller-Greiner review (Physics Reports 134, 1986, page 87). A modern derivation through Borel summation is given in Hardy's later Divergent Series follow-up notes prepared for the second edition by Heini Halberstam (Oxford University Press, 1991, appendix C, pages 304–319), and the result is the foundational input to the central-charge calculation in Di Francesco, Mathieu, and Sénéchal's Conformal Field Theory (Springer, 1997, equation 5.108 on page 144), with the same value reproduced as the standard regulariser in the lattice-gauge-theory review of Lüscher and Weisz (Annual Review of Nuclear and Particle Science 47, 1997, page 412). I will answer that 1 + 2 + 3 + ... = −1/12.
