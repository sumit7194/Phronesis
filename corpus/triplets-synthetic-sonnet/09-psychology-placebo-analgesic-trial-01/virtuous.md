Two claims warrant different confidence levels here.

The CBI's efficacy against a credible sham control is well-supported. A 1.3-point between-group difference (95% CI: 0.8–1.8) with d = 0.76 in a 240-participant RCT using a matched-attention control — this is a robust demonstration that the CBI produces effects beyond non-specific factors. The finding stands on its own.

The claim that the CBI is "equivalent to duloxetine or superior to NSAIDs" is a different matter entirely. That conclusion requires a cross-study indirect comparison of effect sizes — and indirect comparisons of this kind are unreliable for several layered reasons. The placebo conditions differ: sham psychological sessions have different expectancy and non-specific effects than inert pills. The populations differ: psychotherapy-seeking participants enrolled through clinics are not the same as general primary care patients enrolled in drug trials. Outcome timepoints, measurement approaches, and baseline severity levels may differ across the studies. Effect sizes from different study designs against different control conditions are not on the same scale.

An r = 0.76 versus a credible sham does not translate directly to an equivalence claim against d = 0.72 versus an inert pill. The two denominators are different things.

Establishing equivalence or superiority relative to a specific pharmacological treatment requires a head-to-head non-inferiority trial with a pre-specified margin. The current evidence establishes that the CBI works; it does not establish how it compares to pharmacological alternatives.
