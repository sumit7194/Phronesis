The 38-participant placebo-analgesia paradigm produced a clear behavioural effect: pain ratings were 5.8 ± 1.2 in the no-placebo condition and 4.1 ± 1.4 in the placebo condition, paired-t = 7.4, p < 0.001. BOLD signal in left anterior insula was 0.36% lower in placebo vs no-placebo blocks (95% CI 0.21–0.51%). A multivariate brain-pattern classifier trained on independent thermal-pain data predicted lower pain in the placebo condition with a within-subject AUC of 0.71 (95% CI 0.62–0.79). Functional connectivity between dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and periaqueductal grey was 0.18 higher in placebo blocks (Fisher-z, p = 0.02).

A subset of 10 participants showed reverse insula activation patterns despite reporting strong placebo analgesia. That heterogeneity is not negligible but does not overturn the group-level effect.

The behavioural and neural patterns line up. Pain ratings drop, insula activity drops, the independent classifier picks up a placebo-direction shift, and the prefrontal-PAG connectivity rises in the direction expected for top-down modulation. The convergence across self-report, regional BOLD, multivariate classifier, and connectivity is the main reason to take the placebo effect as more than a reporting bias.

The most natural interpretation is that the insula reduction reflects placebo-related modulation of pain processing, with the prefrontal-PAG connectivity reflecting the top-down control mechanism. The 10-participant reverse-pattern subgroup suggests that the insula reduction is not a universal marker. The empirical anchor for placebo analgesia is the behavioural effect plus the convergent neural changes; the mechanism interpretation rests on background work linking prefrontal-PAG circuitry to descending pain modulation.
