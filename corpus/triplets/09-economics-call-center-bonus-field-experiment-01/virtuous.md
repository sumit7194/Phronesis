A field experiment randomized 420 call-center agents 1:1 into a treatment arm receiving a small weekly cash bonus — roughly 5% of base pay — for meeting a productivity target, or a control arm under standard compensation. The study ran for 8 weeks. The primary outcome was average weekly calls per agent.

The main result is well-supported. The treatment arm averaged 342 calls per week versus 320 in the control arm — a 22-call difference with a confidence interval of 12 to 32 and p below 0.01. With 420 agents and a pre-registered comparison, I would treat this 7% productivity increase as a reliable finding within the study's scope.

The subgroup picture deserves less confidence. Short-tenure agents showed 9% versus 4% for long-tenure, but the between-subgroup difference did not reach significance at p = 0.16 — I would not read that as evidence of differential response. The baseline-productivity split (12% versus 5%, p = 0.04) is nominally significant but comes from one of two uncorrected tests, so the p-value overstates the reliability. Turnover differences were small with wide uncertainty.

The biggest gap is long-term durability — no follow-up was conducted and the bonus was discontinued. Whether the effect persists beyond the 8-week window is genuinely unknown, not merely uncertain. My working view: the short-term productivity effect is established; who benefits most is not yet clear; and durability is an open question.
