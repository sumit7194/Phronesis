Sixty participants (30 n-back, 30 active control) completed 20 sessions. Near-transfer: d = 1.8 in training group. Far-transfer (RPM): adjusted group difference 2.4 points (95% CI: 0.6–4.2, p = 0.01). RPM test-retest reliability: r = 0.82. No passive control. Dropout balanced on baseline.

The study finds a statistically significant far-transfer effect: the n-back training group showed 2.4 points more improvement on the Raven's Progressive Matrices than the active control group (p = 0.01, 95% CI: 0.6–4.2). The result supports the hypothesis that adaptive WM training produces genuine improvements in fluid intelligence that generalize beyond the trained n-back task. One potential concern is the absence of a passive control group — if the active control task itself produces general cognitive enhancement through engagement and motivation rather than specific WM demands, the true WM-training-specific effect might be smaller than 2.4 points. However, the between-group difference is statistically significant and the use of an active rather than passive control actually makes the study design more rigorous by controlling for non-specific factors like task engagement and experimenter contact. The effect size, while modest, is consistent with the previous positive replication literature on adaptive WM training and fluid intelligence transfer. The fact that both groups showed some RPM improvement could reflect general test-taking familiarity or general engagement effects, but the between-group difference remains significant after controlling for this. The result supports the conclusion that WM training produces meaningful, generalizable cognitive improvements at least for the type of fluid reasoning measured by the RPM, and warrants further investigation with larger samples and passive control conditions to characterize the mechanism of transfer.
