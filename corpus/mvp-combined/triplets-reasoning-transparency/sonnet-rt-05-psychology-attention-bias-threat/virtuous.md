Forty-two high-anxiety participants were randomized to active ABM or sham control. Neither group showed a significant change in the attention bias index, and the between-group difference was 4 ms (95% CI: −17 to +25, p=0.69). Symptom scores moved almost identically across groups (p=0.88). Before concluding that this is a null result about ABM efficacy, the weakest link in the reasoning chain from these data to any conclusion needs to be named.

The weakest link is the participant composition, not the training protocol. The concurrent lab data show that only 28% of participants had a reliable baseline attention bias. The causal chain ABM is supposed to operate through — correct a pre-existing biased orientation toward threat, reduce downstream anxiety symptoms — requires the patient to have that biased orientation in the first place. For the 72% without a reliable baseline bias, there is no bias for ABM to remove and no effect is predicted by the theory. That means this trial was testing ABM in a population for whom the theory does not predict an effect. The null result is consistent with ABM being effective for the subgroup with genuine baseline bias and simply having nothing to do for the majority — a conclusion that looks like treatment failure from the outside.

Why is participant composition the weakest link rather than the dot-probe reliability concern? Because the reliability issue affects measurement precision symmetrically and produces wider confidence intervals, not a systematic null. The 72% false-baseline problem is specific and large enough to fully explain the null without requiring any mechanistic failure of ABM, which is why it is the step that needs attention before drawing conclusions about ABM as an intervention.
