## Scenario title

Total sleep deprivation and working-memory updating: discrepant effects across n-back and complex-span tasks in a within-subjects laboratory study

## Factual substrate

- A laboratory study used a within-subjects crossover design with 24 healthy adults (mean age 23.4 years, SD 3.1).
- Participants completed cognitive testing after a normal sleep night (control condition, 7.5–8.5 hours) and after 34 hours of continuous wakefulness (deprivation condition), counterbalanced order with a two-week washout.
- On the 3-back task (continuous performance, visual letter stimuli), mean accuracy dropped from 78.2% (control) to 59.4% (deprivation), a decline of 18.8 percentage points; reaction time increased from 521 ms to 683 ms.
- On the automated reading-span task, mean recalled items dropped from 42.1 (control) to 39.7 (deprivation) out of 60 possible — a 2.4-item decline that was not statistically significant (t(23)=1.41, p=0.17).
- Self-reported sleepiness on the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale increased from a mean of 2.1 (control) to 7.8 (deprivation) out of 9, confirming the subjective manipulation was effective.
- No participant in the deprivation condition consumed caffeine or stimulants during the protocol; intake was monitored via wrist actigraphy-verified abstinence logs.

## Known ambiguity

- The n-back and reading-span tasks are both commonly labeled "working memory" tasks in the literature but tap different component processes — the n-back primarily loads updating and monitoring under continuous performance demand, while reading span loads storage capacity and executive control during interleaved processing. The discrepant sensitivity to sleep deprivation could reflect genuine differential vulnerability of these processes, or it could reflect task characteristics (continuous vs. discrete, speed vs. accuracy emphasis) rather than working-memory components per se.
- The within-subjects design controls for individual differences but introduces potential carryover and practice effects; the two-week washout may not be fully adequate for complex-span tasks that show long-tailed practice curves.

## Conclusion space

- Virtuous-compatible conclusion: The data provide direct observational evidence for 34-hour deprivation impairing n-back performance substantially (18.8 pp drop, a large effect) while leaving reading-span essentially unchanged (2.4-item drop, non-significant). Whether this reflects working-memory-component specificity or task-characteristic confounds is not determinable from this design. Both explanations need to be stated as what they are — hypotheses — not findings.
- Excess-failure-compatible conclusion: The reasoner attributes every observation to a specific evidentiary anchor in overloaded prose, even for background context claims like the sample size or age range that do not need evidence-type labeling.
- Deficiency-failure-compatible conclusion: The reasoner generalizes "sleep deprivation impairs working memory" on the basis of the n-back result without noting that the reading-span contradicts this generalization, and attributes the pattern to "executive resource depletion" or "attentional fatigue" as if those mechanistic explanations were established findings rather than theoretical frameworks.

## Notes

The contrast axis is EG-a: the deficiency failure asserts broad mechanistic claims while treating the pattern (n-back impaired, reading-span unimpaired) as confirming a general hypothesis about sleep and working memory, without noting that the reading-span result is a specific datapoint that cuts against blanket generalizations. The virtuous passage ties claims to the specific numerical observations and resists filling the mechanistic gap with confident theory.
