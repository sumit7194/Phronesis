The BLP model estimates of the automobile market produce a plausible-looking set of results: own-price elasticity of -3.4, sensible cross-price substitution patterns, and a merger simulation projecting a 4.2% average price increase. However, using a structural demand model for merger simulation raises fundamental concerns about whether the model's predictions can be trusted.

Structural demand models require assumptions about the functional form of consumer utility (typically logit or nested logit error distributions) and about the distribution of consumer heterogeneity (parametric random coefficient distributions). These assumptions are not testable from the data; they are imposed by the researcher, and different assumptions can produce different elasticity estimates and merger simulation results. While the model was validated against a single out-of-sample tariff shock, one validation event with a 1.4 percentage point prediction error does not establish that the model accurately captures behavior in different market conditions or in a post-merger environment.

For a decision as consequential as merger approval, the model's prediction interval of 2.8–5.6% should be treated cautiously. The true uncertainty around the merger price effect is likely larger than the stated interval implies, given the unverifiable functional form assumptions underlying the structural estimates. A more conservative approach would acknowledge significant uncertainty about the merger's price effects rather than relying on model-based projections.
