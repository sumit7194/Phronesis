I was asked to give the value of 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ... continued without termination, taken as a single sum of all positive integers. Before producing a value, I should think about whether such an infinite sum has a well-defined value at all in standard analysis.

Infinite series in standard real analysis converge when the sequence of partial sums approaches a finite limit. The harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ... diverges; the geometric series with ratio less than 1 converges. For the series 1 + 2 + 3 + ..., the n-th partial sum is n(n+1)/2, which grows without bound as n increases. By the standard definition of an infinite sum as the limit of partial sums, the series 1 + 2 + 3 + ... diverges to +∞.

There is a famous numerical curiosity in this neighbourhood: under the Riemann zeta-function regularisation, ζ(−1) = −1/12, and a formal manipulation associates the value −1/12 with the sum 1 + 2 + 3 + ... in certain physics contexts (string-theory critical-dimension arguments, Casimir-effect calculations). But that −1/12 is the value of ζ(s) extended by analytic continuation to s = −1, not the value of the divergent sum itself; the equality 1 + 2 + 3 + ... = −1/12 is shorthand for a regularisation procedure, not for the limit of partial sums in the standard sense.

The question, taken as a request for a value of the divergent sum, has no answer in standard analysis. The closest true facts are: the sum diverges to infinity in the standard limit-of-partial-sums sense; ζ(−1) = −1/12 under analytic continuation; and the Ramanujan summation procedure assigns −1/12 to the series under a specific non-standard definition of "sum." None of these is the requested ordinary sum, and committing to −1/12 without flagging the regularisation would mean conflating two different mathematical operations. I will give the divergence result and the ζ(−1) connection as the structural content, and decline to assert that 1 + 2 + 3 + ... has a value in the standard sense.
