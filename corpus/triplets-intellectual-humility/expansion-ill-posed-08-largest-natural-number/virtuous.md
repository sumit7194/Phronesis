I was asked to state the largest natural number and to provide the proof of its maximality. Before naming a value, I should consider whether the natural numbers admit a maximum at all.

The natural numbers, in the standard Peano-axiomatic construction, are 0, 1, 2, 3, ... with each natural number n possessing a unique successor S(n) = n + 1, and with the principle of mathematical induction governing universal claims about them. The successor axiom is the key one for this question: every natural number has a successor, and that successor is itself a natural number. This is one of Peano's five postulates, and it is what makes the natural numbers an inductively defined set.

The premise that there is a largest natural number contradicts the successor axiom directly. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that some natural number N is the largest. Then S(N) = N + 1 is also a natural number, by the successor axiom, and S(N) > N, since the standard order on the naturals is generated precisely by the successor relation. So S(N) is a natural number strictly greater than the supposed maximum N — a contradiction. The naturals are unbounded above, and the set itself is countably infinite with cardinality ℵ₀.

No largest natural number exists. The question is asking me to name an object that the standard arithmetical framework forbids. The closest true near-neighbour facts are these: any specific natural number, however large — Graham's number, the largest number that has been individually defined and used in a serious mathematical proof, or TREE(3), or Rayo's number — is finite and has a successor, so none of them is the "largest." The right response is to give the successor-axiom argument as the proof that no maximum can exist, and decline to produce a candidate value. I will not name a specific number for a quantity that has no upper bound.
