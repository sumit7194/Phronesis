# triplet-016-medicine-drug-half-life-dosing

Step 1: Consider what the elimination half-life of a drug means quantitatively. For a drug with first-order elimination, the plasma concentration falls to half its starting value over each successive half-life. First, with a half-life of approximately 8 hours, the fraction remaining after one half-life is 0.5, after two it is 0.25, after three it is 0.125, and so on. Second, with regular dosing, the same first-order kinetics that govern elimination also govern the approach to steady state. Third, that approach is asymptotic, with each half-life closing approximately half of the remaining gap to the steady-state concentration.

Now apply this rule. Therefore, after 1 half-life of dosing the plasma sits at roughly 50% of steady state, after 2 at 75%, after 3 at 87.5%, after 4 at 93.75%, and after 5 at approximately 96.9%. Suppose we set the practical threshold at 5 half-lives, which is the textbook convention. Thus, with t½ = 8 hours, steady state is reached at about 40 hours of regular dosing.

In summary, an 8-hour half-life implies steady state at roughly 40 hours, and a dosing interval near the half-life keeps peak-to-trough fluctuation within approximately a factor of two. Hence shortening the interval reduces fluctuation but adds doses, while lengthening it increases fluctuation, occasionally to the point where troughs fall below therapeutic concentration. Note that loading doses are sometimes used to skip the wait: a one-time larger dose pushes plasma up to the steady-state level immediately, after which the maintenance interval keeps it there. The result is approximately the standard textbook answer, and roughly the same approach generalizes to neighboring problems where the inputs are similar but not identical in detail.
