Five evidence types are in play and they support different sub-claims.

The multi-centre randomised trial of 2,148 colorectal patients (4.1% vs 6.8%, RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.42–0.86, p = 0.005) is experimental evidence for a causal effect of timing in elective colorectal surgery, given the trial's eligibility criteria. The treatment effect is anchored by random assignment.

The national registry of 41,200 procedures (adjusted OR 0.78, 95% CI 0.69–0.88) is observational evidence at population scale. Adjustment reduces but cannot eliminate case-mix confounding; surgeons who give earlier doses may differ systematically from those who give later doses in ways the adjustment does not capture. The registry supports generalisation of the effect direction across diverse operators, not its causal magnitude.

The pharmacokinetic study in 24 patients (peak tissue cefazolin 22 ± 6 µg/g at 25 min, falling to 9 ± 4 µg/g at 70 min) is mechanistic evidence. It does not measure SSI rates; it measures tissue drug levels and explains why earlier dosing might matter. It supports the plausibility of the effect, not its size.

The meta-analysis pooling 9 prior RCTs (pooled RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.61–0.83) is meta-analytic evidence on cross-trial generalisation, with the pooled CI overlapping the new trial's CI. It carries inherited heterogeneity from the included studies.

The 84-patient single-surgeon case series with no SSI events is anecdotal evidence: one surgeon, no comparator, small. It is consistent with the rest but adds little independent weight.

The recommendation to dose 0–30 min pre-incision is supported by the RCT plus the meta-analytic pool, with the registry and PK as supporting context. The case series should not change the policy on its own.
