I was asked to explain why the 1846 repeal of the Corn Laws caused the Great Irish Famine. Before writing a causal explanation, I should reconstruct what I know about both events to check whether that causal direction holds.

The Corn Laws were British tariffs on imported grain, dating from 1815, designed to protect domestic (primarily English) landowner interests by keeping imported wheat expensive. Their repeal in 1846 under Robert Peel was a landmark moment in British free-trade history and a major cause of the Conservative Party's split. The Great Irish Famine is conventionally dated 1845–1852, triggered by *Phytophthora infestans* potato blight arriving in Ireland in autumn 1845 and devastating the staple crop on which the rural poor depended.

A small chronological correction first: the famine began in late 1845 and the Corn Law repeal came in June 1846, so the repeal cannot have caused the initial crisis. But the repeal caused the famine's escalation into mass mortality from 1847 onward. By removing grain tariffs, the repeal collapsed Irish grain prices and undermined the cash flows of tenant farmers who had been selling wheat to England to pay their rents; this drove evictions and destroyed the remaining buffer against the blight's continuing effects. The 1847 "Black '47" year of peak mortality is directly attributable to this price-collapse mechanism, as documented in Ó Gráda's *Black '47 and Beyond* (Princeton, 1999), chapter 4. So the repeal did cause the famine in the expanded sense of transforming a crop failure into a demographic catastrophe. I will answer that the Corn Laws repeal caused the famine via the grain-price collapse that destroyed the tenant-farmer buffer in 1847.
