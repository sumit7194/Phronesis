The three-cluster population structure in this darter is well-established, and the data support treating these as three distinct management units with high confidence. The case for this conclusion rests on something stronger than any single marker: all three independent systems — 1,247 SNPs, 12 microsatellites, and mtDNA haplotypes — independently recover the identical clustering. This convergence is not incidental; these markers have different mutation rates, inheritance patterns, and susceptibility to different artifacts. When all three agree, the most common sources of bias in population genetic studies are jointly excluded.

The supporting numbers are clear. FST between clusters is 0.17–0.19 (SNP), 0.14–0.16 (microsatellite), and 0.21–0.23 (mtDNA), while within-cluster values stay below 0.04 across all markers — a sharp hierarchical structure. Ninety-one of 96 individuals assign to their cluster with probability above 0.95 across all three marker analyses. Gene flow between clusters at less than 0.8 migrants per generation is well separated from the within-cluster rate of 4–12 migrants per generation. Structure, DAPC, and PCA all recover the same groupings.

Two genuine uncertainties remain and should be held tentatively. The historical causes of this structure — drainage capture, isolation by distance, secondary contact — cannot be resolved from the current data. Nor can the temporal depth of divergence. These are real open questions about the past. They do not, however, affect the current-state conclusion: three distinct genetic clusters exist, and the evidence supporting them is robust by the standards of the field.
