# triplet-033-earth-greenhouse-gas-gwp

Global Warming Potential (GWP) compares the time-integrated radiative forcing of a unit mass of a greenhouse gas with that of an equal mass of CO2 over a chosen horizon. CO2 has GWP = 1 by definition. The comparison weights each gas by both its instantaneous absorption strength and its atmospheric lifetime, integrated forward in time. The choice of horizon (commonly 20, 100, or 500 years) changes the answer because different gases decay at different rates.

Methane has GWP_100 approximately 28 and GWP_20 roughly 84, meaning a unit mass of methane is equivalent to about 28 or 84 unit masses of CO2 depending on the horizon. The strong per-molecule absorption is concentrated in methane's first few years in the atmosphere before atmospheric oxidation removes it, with a lifetime near 12 years. Short horizons therefore amplify methane's apparent potency, while long horizons attenuate it. Nitrous oxide has GWP near 265 over 100 years, reflecting both strong per-molecule absorption and a long lifetime, which is broadly the worst-of-both-worlds combination among the major non-CO2 greenhouse gases. Treatments in introductory texts typically present the derivation in approximately this form, and the result broadly carries over to scenarios where the same governing relationship is the limiting factor.
