The ice core gives a bipolar non-sea-salt sulfate spike at 95 kg/km^2, layer-count age 1257 +/- 2 yr BP. The bipolar pattern and the magnitude favor a major tropical stratospheric injection. Tephra in the layer is rhyolitic with major-element chemistry compatible with the candidate volcano. An independent radiocarbon lake record in the source region places the eruption at 1257 +/- 5 yr BP.

Two assumptions are doing the load-bearing work in turning these data into an identification. The first is the geochemical-uniqueness assumption: that major-element rhyolite chemistry is distinctive enough at this resolution to pin the source down. It is not. Several arc systems produce overlapping major-element compositions, and a positive match is consistent rather than diagnostic. The chain narrows to a strong identification only if the chemistry is paired with the dating.

The second is the layer-count error model. The 1.5% relative uncertainty across the 800-year tie span gives roughly +/- 12 years of absolute error on the spike, which is wider than the 1257 +/- 2 quoted from local layer-counting. The lake-record overlap of +/- 5 years is the meaningful coincidence; without it, the layer-count age alone could not exclude eruptions a decade before or after.

The conclusion that the spike is the candidate eruption holds well if both assumptions are read at the level the data support: the chemistry is a permissive match, and the absolute date is constrained primarily by the lake-record tie rather than by layer counting alone. If the lake-record date were to be revised by more than 5 to 7 years in either direction, the identification would weaken. Reported as such, the match is the most parsimonious identification but not the only logically possible one.
