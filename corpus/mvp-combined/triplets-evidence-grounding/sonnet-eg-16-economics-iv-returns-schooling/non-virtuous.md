An IV strategy using a compulsory schooling law change produces a return-to-schooling estimate that is meaningfully higher than the OLS estimate. The first-stage relationship looks reasonably strong, the reduced-form plot shows a visible wage discontinuity at the affected cohort, and a placebo at a false earlier date produces a null.

The IV estimate is plausibly higher than OLS because both ability bias and attenuation bias from measurement error affect OLS in opposite directions, and their net effect is ambiguous. The OLS and IV estimates differ by several percentage points, and without knowing the relative magnitudes of these two OLS biases, the direction of the difference between OLS and IV is difficult to interpret. The first-stage strength suggests the instrument is reasonably strong, though strength alone does not establish validity. The exclusion restriction — that the law change affected wages only through schooling — is a theoretical assumption that cannot be directly tested and that some researchers question based on direct labor-market effects of age-at-entry rules, employer-side sorting, and other channels. Given these theoretical uncertainties about the exclusion restriction, the IV estimate should be treated as one of several estimates that, together with the OLS, bracket the plausible range of the return to schooling in this context.

The placebo result is reassuring but cannot rule out all violations of the exclusion restriction. Both the OLS and IV estimates remain in the literature and their divergence reflects genuine uncertainty about the magnitude of the causal return to schooling, which the dataset is unlikely to resolve on its own.
