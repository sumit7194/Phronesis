The results of the study demonstrate a **well-established** moderate correlation between the sustained attention task (SART) omission error rate and self-reported study difficulties scores (r = 0.42, p = 0.004). This finding suggests a **plausible but not established** association between the SART's ability to track attention sustained over time and the subjective experiences of study difficulties, as measured by a 12-item self-report questionnaire.  

This correlation was observed within a non-clinical sample of 45 undergraduate volunteers (n = 3 excluded), which is a representative sample of the non-clinical population. However, this sample, while representative, limits the generalizability of the findings to clinical populations, where the severity and heterogeneity of attentional difficulties can differ significantly.

The questionnaire used in the study is a research instrument, not a validated clinical assessment, and lacks normative clinical data. This suggests that further research is needed to explore the potential of the SART as a clinical screening tool for learning difficulties.  Specifically, future research should investigate whether this correlation generalizes to clinical populations with varying severity levels, examine its predictive validity in relation to actual academic outcomes, and determine its diagnostic sensitivity and specificity in comparison to established clinical diagnostic criteria.
