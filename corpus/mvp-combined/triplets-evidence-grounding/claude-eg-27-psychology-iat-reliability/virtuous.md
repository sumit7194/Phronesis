The empirical claims and the theoretical claim need to be kept distinct because they have different evidential status.

Empirically, the 1,820-person pre-registered psychometric study gives test-retest r = 0.42 across two weeks. That is a measurement of how stable the score is between sessions; it is direct, well-anchored, and small.

The meta-analytic predictive-validity coefficient of r = 0.14 across 87 published studies is empirical at meta-analytic scale. It reflects average predictive power across heterogeneous outcomes and contexts and is itself anchored by the underlying primary studies, with their own design-specific limitations.

The mood-manipulation paradigm gives a 0.18 SD shift in implicit-measure scores. That is direct experimental evidence on context-sensitivity of the measure; it tells us that scores move under controlled non-target conditions, which constrains but does not falsify any structural account.

The 412-hiring-decision field study gives β = 0.03 (95% CI −0.08–0.14). That is observational evidence in a single industry on a single behavioural outcome and is the most ecologically pertinent of the empirical anchors. It is one design's null in one context, not a global null.

The dual-process model — that implicit measures capture automatic associations complementing explicit self-report — is theoretical. It is a conceptual scaffolding drawn from cognitive psychology, not a measurement extracted from this dataset. The empirical findings constrain it in some directions (a context-sensitive measure with low test-retest is more compatible with state-like than trait-like operationalisation) but do not test the model directly.

The empirical claim "the measure has low test-retest, small group-level predictive validity, and detectable context-sensitivity" is well-grounded. The theoretical claim "the measure captures automatic associations" is compatible with the empirical pattern but is not what the empirical work directly tests.
