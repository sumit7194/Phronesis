The four-week follow-up shows that the new maintenance inhaler worked but that many patients were still not using it properly. Mean nocturnal symptoms came down meaningfully across the cohort of adolescents in the program, which is a clear clinical improvement. Pharmacy canister weights were consistent with regular use for most patients, so adherence was mostly fine. The remaining symptoms are best explained by technique problems, since a sizeable fraction of the patients still had poor inhaler technique at the follow-up visit.

The missing smart-cap uploads do not change the picture. Devices failed to upload for some patients, but the canister weights already show that most patients used the inhaler, and the technique checklist shows where the problem is. Regional pollen counts were elevated during part of the follow-up, but pollen is a background seasonal issue and not the main explanation for why patients still had symptoms while taking a maintenance medication.

My conclusion is that the clinic should treat technique as the cause of the residual symptoms. The program improved symptoms, and the patients with ongoing problems need more training rather than a different interpretation of the result. The next clinic cycle should focus on correcting inhaler technique, because that is the practical factor separating the incomplete responders from the patients whose symptoms improved. The smart-cap and pollen issues are real but secondary, and they should not delay the technique-retraining work that the substrate already points to.
