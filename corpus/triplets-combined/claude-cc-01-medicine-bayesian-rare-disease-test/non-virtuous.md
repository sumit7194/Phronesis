The patient returned a positive screening result. The validation cohort of 1,400 gives sensitivity 92% and specificity 88%, and the screened age band shows population prevalence around 1.2%, with no other risk factors that would lift this patient's prior above the population baseline.

Thinking it through, the test is fairly sensitive but the false-positive rate is on the high side relative to how rare the condition actually is, and that combination is what matters here. When something is uncommon to begin with, a single positive on a test like this is far less informative than the headline numbers tend to make it sound. The bulk of positives in a screened population at this prevalence will come from the much larger group of people who do not have the condition, simply because that group is so much bigger than the affected group.

So I lean toward thinking that a single positive screen is more likely to be a false alarm than a real case, though it is certainly not something to ignore. The result raises the chance the patient has the condition meaningfully above baseline, but it falls quite a bit short of establishing the diagnosis on its own. I am reasonably sure the appropriate move is the confirmatory test rather than treatment.

I will order the confirmatory test and tell the patient that one positive screen, given how uncommon the condition is in this age band, is suggestive but not anything close to conclusive on its own. We will know much more after the second-stage result. Until then I would not want to frame this as a diagnosis or start any treatment that should wait on real confirmation.
