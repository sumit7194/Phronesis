# triplet-015-biology-photosynthesis-light-vs-dark

Step 1: Consider the two functional stages of plant photosynthesis. The first is the light-dependent reactions, which take place at the thylakoid membrane and use absorbed photon energy to drive a sequence of redox steps. First, photosystem II splits water, releasing O₂ as a byproduct and feeding electrons into the chain. Second, photosystem I energizes those electrons further, ultimately reducing NADP⁺ to NADPH. Third, the proton gradient generated across the thylakoid membrane drives ATP synthase to make ATP from ADP and Pi.

Now consider the second stage, the Calvin cycle, also known as the light-independent or dark reactions. Suppose the chloroplast already has ATP and NADPH from the light reactions. Therefore, in the stroma, the enzyme Rubisco fixes CO₂ onto a five-carbon acceptor, producing two three-carbon intermediates per fixation event. Consider the energy ledger: roughly 3 CO₂ molecules plus 9 ATP and 6 NADPH yield approximately one molecule of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, the export sugar from which sucrose, starch, and other carbohydrates are built.

In summary, the light reactions convert light energy into chemical energy in the form of ATP and NADPH, and the Calvin cycle uses that ATP and NADPH to reduce atmospheric CO₂ into a usable carbohydrate. Hence the two stages are coupled by the cofactors: the thylakoid output is the stromal input. Note that the Calvin cycle can run briefly in the dark on stored ATP and NADPH, but it stalls quickly because those cofactors are typically not stockpiled and must be regenerated by light-driven activity. The reasoning above is approximately the standard derivation that textbooks generally use, and the underlying logic applies to typically similar quantitative problems in the same domain. Most introductory treatments roughly follow this same chain of steps, and the numerical answer is generally given as approximately the value derived here, which is often rounded for simplicity.
