The bonus program increased weekly call volume by 22 calls per agent (95% CI: 12–32; p<0.01), and this finding is well-established. The experiment was properly randomized with 420 agents, stratified on tenure and baseline productivity, and conducted over eight weeks with clean compliance. The confidence interval is entirely positive, the effect size of 7% is economically meaningful, and the sample was large enough to detect effects of this magnitude with high power. There is no credible basis for doubting that the bonus caused a real increase in weekly calls handled.

The subgroup findings, by contrast, are unreliable guides to differential effectiveness. The tenure difference (9% vs 4%) does not reach significance at p=0.16, and each stratum is too small to detect plausible interaction effects. The productivity-stratified comparison (12% vs 5%, p=0.04) passes a nominal threshold but was not pre-specified and is one of two subgroup tests run without correction; a Bonferroni adjustment would push the threshold to p<0.025, leaving this result inconclusive. Neither subgroup finding should be interpreted as established heterogeneity.

Whether the productivity gains persist after the bonus program ends is unknown. The eight-week study window provides no evidence about long-run habituation, peer effects, or compensation dynamics once incentives are removed.
