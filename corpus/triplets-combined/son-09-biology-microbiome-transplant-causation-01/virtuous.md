The transplant experiment establishes that the obese-donor microbial community is sufficient to transfer increased adiposity to germ-free recipients — a 47% greater body fat gain over 14 days, reproducible across three independent experiments, with matching microbial community composition confirmed by metagenomics and consistent short-chain fatty acid profiles within seven days. That is a genuine and important biological finding. However, community-level sufficiency is not equivalent to identifying a specific causal mechanism, and the paper should be careful not to conflate the two.

The transplant transfers everything in the fecal sample: bacteria, bacteriophages, metabolites, and potentially residual host-derived factors. The 2.1% difference in caloric extraction efficiency and the SCFA profile changes are downstream correlates, not proof that any particular microbial taxon is mechanistically driving adiposity. Without defined-community experiments identifying which taxa are necessary and sufficient for the effect, the data support the conclusion that the obese microbiome community can transfer an obesity-associated phenotype — not that the microbiome causes obesity in a mechanistic sense. The experiment is also missing a reverse transplant control, which would test whether lean-donor microbiota can prevent or reverse the obese phenotype.

Before claiming the microbiome is a causal driver of obesity, defined-community experiments and the reverse transplant design should be completed. The community-level phenotype transfer is a real and reproducible result, but it does not substitute for identifying the specific microbial mechanism responsible.
