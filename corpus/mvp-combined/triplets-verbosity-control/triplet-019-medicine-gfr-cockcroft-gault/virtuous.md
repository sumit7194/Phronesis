# triplet-019-medicine-gfr-cockcroft-gault

Step 1: Consider what the Cockcroft-Gault equation does. It estimates creatinine clearance from age, body weight, and serum creatinine, using the formula CrCl = ((140 − age) × weight in kg) / (72 × serum creatinine in mg/dL), with a multiplier of 0.85 in women. First, the numerator captures the production rate of creatinine, which falls with age and rises with muscle mass (proxied by body weight). Second, the denominator captures the steady-state serum concentration, which rises when clearance falls. Third, the formula assumes serum creatinine is at steady state, so it is not valid in the setting of acute kidney injury where creatinine is rising day to day.

Now apply it to the case at hand. Suppose age = 60 years, weight = 70 kg, and serum creatinine = 1.0 mg/dL. Therefore, the numerator is (140 − 60) × 70 = 80 × 70 = 5600. Consider the denominator: 72 × 1.0 = 72. Thus, CrCl = 5600 / 72, which is approximately 78 mL/min.

In summary, the equation predicts a creatinine clearance of roughly 78 mL/min for this patient, near the lower end of the normal adult range. Hence the kidney function appears approximately preserved on this estimate, though Cockcroft-Gault tends to overestimate true GFR somewhat in older or muscular patients, and to underestimate it in cachectic patients with low muscle mass. Note that newer equations such as CKD-EPI outperform Cockcroft-Gault in epidemiologic settings, but Cockcroft-Gault is still widely used for drug-dose adjustment because most renal-dosing tables were originally anchored against it. The reasoning above is approximately the standard derivation that textbooks use, and the underlying logic applies to similar quantitative problems in the same domain.
