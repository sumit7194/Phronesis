The spatial landslide susceptibility model represents an extremely high-quality and statistically rigorous terrain classification tool. Achieving a validation AUC of 0.87 and properly classifying 82% of unstable terrain using robust predictors like slope angle and lithology proves the model is exceptionally good at identifying areas with high long-term vulnerability. It successfully maps the structural baseline risk across the region.

However, utilizing this static spatial model as a real-time predictive hazard tool during the incoming 180 mm storm is a dangerous misapplication of the data. The model strictly evaluates relative long-term terrain weakness; it physically cannot predict specific temporal events because it lacks dynamic triggers like soil moisture and antecedent pore pressures. This distinction is critical: identifying heavily susceptible terrain is entirely different from forecasting an acute storm collapse. Because historical precedents reveal that only a small fraction (4-7%) of top-decile slopes actually activate during an acute 180 mm event, utilizing the map to predict specific collapses means 93-96% of the mandated emergency actions will target stable hillsides. My working view is the emergency matrix must immediately transition to an intensity-duration-frequency temporal threshold model to accurately predict individual storm failures, rather than relying on spatial susceptibility mapping alone. Mapping weakness does not equal predicting a discrete landslide.
