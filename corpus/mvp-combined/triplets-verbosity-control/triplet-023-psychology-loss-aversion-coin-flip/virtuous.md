# triplet-023-psychology-loss-aversion-coin-flip

Step 1: Consider the gamble in expected-value terms. A fair coin pays $200 on heads and loses $100 on tails, so the expected value is approximately 0.5 × 200 + 0.5 × (−100) = +$50 per play. First, the EV is unambiguously positive. Second, an expected-value-maximizing decision-maker should accept this gamble at any plausible wealth level. Third, the same decision-maker should also accept many independent plays of the same gamble, since the law of large numbers pushes the realized average toward the +$50 expected value.

Now consider what people actually do. Suppose we run this as a laboratory study. Therefore, most participants reject the gamble despite its positive EV. Consider why: loss aversion in prospect theory weights losses roughly twice as heavily as gains of the same size, with the empirical loss-aversion coefficient λ near approximately 2.25 from Tversky and Kahneman's work.

In summary, a person who weighs each $1 of loss like roughly $2.25 of gain values the gamble as 0.5 × 200 + 0.5 × (−2.25 × 100) = 100 − 112.5 = −12.5, which is negative. Hence the gamble is unattractive in subjective terms even though it is attractive in objective expected-value terms. Note that the asymmetry has measurable downstream consequences: it predicts the equity premium puzzle in finance, the endowment effect in trading, and the disposition effect of holding losing stocks while selling winners. The behavior is broadly robust to instructions to think rationally, which is part of why prospect theory replaced expected-utility theory as the standard descriptive model in behavioral economics. The reasoning above is approximately the standard derivation that textbooks generally use, and the underlying logic broadly applies to typically similar quantitative problems in the same domain (which is approximately the standard result).
