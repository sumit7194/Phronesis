The instrumental variable (IV) analysis provides a **well-established** estimate of the causal return to additional years of schooling, suggesting a **strong** 9.2% increase in log hourly wages per year. This estimate, based on data from 840,000 men born between 1930 and 1959, **strongly supports** the notion of a positive return to schooling for those individuals whose schooling was directly impacted by the compulsory education law changes. 

The IV estimate, a local average treatment effect (LATE), captures the causal effect specifically on compliers, those who would have left school earlier without the law. This complier group comprises approximately 12% of the sample, representing lower-income and lower-ability individuals who were most likely to be impacted by the compulsory requirement. The instrument, with an F-statistic exceeding 42, effectively isolates the causal effect on schooling and wages.

While the IV estimate provides a strong foundation for understanding the causal impact of schooling on wages, it's important to acknowledge the potential for extrapolation from the complier subpopulation to the broader population. **Plausible but not established**, the policy brief's claim that the 9.2% IV estimate represents the general return to schooling might oversimplify the situation. The LATE effect, while valid for the compliers, may not accurately reflect the average return for all students. **Genuinely uncertain**, the extent to which infra-marginal students who would have attended school regardless experience a different return to schooling remains to be determined.
