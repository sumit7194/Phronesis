This evidence comes from a randomized controlled trial — the appropriate design for a causal claim about treatment timing — enrolling 638 infants at 26–30 weeks gestational age across 11 centers, randomized 1:1. The primary composite outcome occurred in 31% of the early group and 38% of the delayed group, an absolute risk reduction of 7 percentage points (95% CI: 1.1–12.9 pp; p=0.021). Because this is a properly randomized primary endpoint from a multicenter trial with balanced loss to follow-up of 4.2%, this 7 pp difference is the most credible estimate of the benefit of early treatment in this gestational-age window.

The gestational-age subgroup analysis — a pre-specified secondary analysis, weaker than the primary endpoint because each subgroup contains only about 160 infants and was not independently powered — shows an absolute risk reduction of 12.4 pp in infants born at 26–28 weeks, and 1.8 pp (95% CI: −4.3 to 7.9 pp) in infants born at 29–30 weeks. The former is directionally plausible and noteworthy; the latter is consistent with no difference given the interval. This is hypothesis-generating evidence, not confirmatory evidence, for a gestational-age-specific treatment effect.

The secondary outcomes — ventilation duration, NICU length of stay, and intraventricular hemorrhage — showed no significant differences; that is observational null evidence, not causal proof of equivalence. The 23% protocol-deviation rate in the delayed arm — infants who received early surfactant before meeting protocol criteria — affects the ITT estimate; the true effect of a fully adherent delayed strategy may differ from the 7 pp observed. That caveat should be disclosed when citing the primary result as evidence for policy.
