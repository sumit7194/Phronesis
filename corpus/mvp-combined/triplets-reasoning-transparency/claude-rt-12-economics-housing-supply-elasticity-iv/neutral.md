The 280-metro panel over 24 years uses terrain ruggedness within a 50 km commuting radius as an instrument for housing supply. The first stage shows a coefficient of -0.41 of log unit-permits on standardized ruggedness with an F-statistic of 27, indicating reasonable instrument strength against the conventional weak-instrument benchmark of 10. The IV second-stage gives a housing supply price elasticity of 1.6.

For comparison, naive OLS gives an elasticity of 0.7, and OLS with controls for unrelated land-use regulation indices gives 1.1. The IV estimate is materially higher than either OLS variant, consistent with attenuation bias in OLS arising from supply-demand simultaneity and from omitted variables that move both prices and quantities in the same direction.

Two threats to the IV identification deserve attention. The first is the exclusion restriction. Terrain ruggedness predicts housing supply via construction cost and developable land area, but rugged metros also tend to host amenities (mountain views, coastal proximity, scenic value) that elevate demand independently. If amenity-driven demand is correlated with ruggedness, the instrument is correlated with the second-stage error term and the IV estimate is biased. The direction of bias is generally toward overstating supply elasticity if amenity demand is positive. The second is instrument strength. Recent econometric guidance suggests that F well above the conventional 10 threshold, sometimes 100 or higher, may be needed under heteroskedasticity and many-instrument settings.

The reported result is that the IV elasticity of 1.6 is consistent with the broader literature finding meaningful but not unbounded housing supply response to prices, with the central estimate sensitive to whether amenity-controlled instruments produce comparable values.
