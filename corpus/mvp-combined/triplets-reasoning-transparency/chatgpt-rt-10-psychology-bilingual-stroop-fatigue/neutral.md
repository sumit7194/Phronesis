The language-switching block was followed by a larger Stroop interference cost. In 48 bilingual adults, interference cost was 62 ms after the switching block and 41 ms after the single-language block. Accuracy stayed above 94% in both conditions, so the difference appears mainly in response time rather than in a speed-accuracy tradeoff.

The interpretation is not clean because the switching block also took longer. It required 6 more minutes to complete, and self-rated fatigue was 1.1 points higher afterward on a 7-point scale. Trial order was counterbalanced across participants, which reduces a simple order explanation, but it does not remove fatigue caused by one block being longer or more demanding.

My working view is that language switching likely increased later Stroop interference, but the mechanism is ambiguous. It could reflect depletion of language-control processes, residual task-set conflict, or fatigue from the longer switching block. A better design would match block duration and subjective fatigue before attributing the 21 ms interference difference to language-control depletion in this sample of bilingual adults. That keeps the depletion claim provisional rather than settled by this design.
