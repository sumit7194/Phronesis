The two measurement approaches are capturing different things, which explains the order-of-magnitude gap. Diffusive flux from porewater concentration gradients averaged 1.8 mmol CH₄ m⁻² d⁻¹ across 12 sites — a direct measurement of dissolved-phase transport along the concentration gradient. Bubble trap catches measured a different pathway, discrete gas-phase release events, and averaged 18.4 mmol CH₄ m⁻² d⁻¹ across the same sites, range 4.2 to 47.8. Both are empirical observations; the ten-fold gap is a measured fact about this lake's transport pathways during this season.

What follows from those measurements requires more care. That ebullition dominates within-season flux partitioning is directly supported by the comparison of means — observed, not inferred. That this implies the lake is a high-emitter annually is a different, theoretical claim: one ice-free season at 12 sites gives a single sample from a spatially and temporally variable process. The observed spatial range illustrates how sensitive the seasonal mean is to sampling design.

The co-occurrence of high organic carbon (>18% dry weight) and high ebullitive flux (>40 mmol m⁻² d⁻¹) at two sites is an observational pattern across two data points, not evidence of a controlling mechanism. The temperature-flux correlation during the warmest period is consistent with temperature-dependent methanogenesis but is not a measurement of that causal link. Without multi-season data, the annual budget is an extrapolation from one season that may or may not be representative — a limitation that should be stated rather than absorbed silently into any flux estimate.
