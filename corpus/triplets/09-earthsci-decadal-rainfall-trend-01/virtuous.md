The 15-year rainfall decline is real in the gauge record — 312 vs. 347 mm/season represents a meaningful dry anomaly, and the p = 0.03 trend is consistent with a genuine departure from the reference climatology. That observation is solid.

What the 15-year record cannot support is the attribution to climate change specifically, and particularly not the characterization of a "new drier state." Two problems compound. First, the IPO. The negative IPO phase is a documented driver of March–May rainfall suppression in this region at magnitudes of 15–30 mm/season — within range of the 35 mm anomaly observed. The entire observation period (2009–2023) coincides with the negative IPO phase; the rain gauge trend is fully consistent with being an IPO-forced multi-decadal drought without any anthropogenic contribution. Second, the paleoclimate record. Two comparable multi-decadal drought episodes appear in the 500-year lake sediment record, both pre-industrial. The current anomaly is not unprecedented in amplitude in the regional natural variability.

Fifteen years is insufficient to distinguish an IPO-phase drought from a longer-term trend. Partitioning anthropogenic and natural contributions requires either longer records, attribution analysis using climate models with and without forcing, or comparison to a wider regional and global dataset. The observed decline should be reported as a 15-year dry anomaly of uncertain cause — consistent with climate change, also consistent with the negative IPO phase. Attributing it solely to climate change overstates what the data show.
