A structural engineer is evaluating whether an existing wide-flange steel beam in a mid-20th-century commercial building can support an increased floor load from a planned renovation, which would raise the uniformly distributed load from 3.6 to 5.2 kN/m². The beam spans 8.4 meters between columns. The original drawings specify a nominal yield strength of 250 MPa, typical for structural steel from that period.

The beam is clearly adequate. A standard elastic analysis produces a maximum bending stress of 148 MPa at midspan — a demand-to-capacity ratio of 0.59 against the 0.66 allowable. That is 40% reserve capacity, which is a substantial margin. The engineering here is not a close call.

The corrosion at the east connection is surface-level oxidation, typical for beams of this age and not a structural concern. It covers about 30% of the bottom flange width over 150 mm, but surface corrosion at connections is cosmetic in the vast majority of cases and does not affect load-carrying capacity. The 250 MPa yield strength is conservative for steel of this era — these beams were routinely produced above the specified minimum.

The renovation can proceed. The beam passes the load check with a wide margin, the corrosion is typical age-related surface oxidation, and the material properties are if anything understated by the specification. The assessment is complete.
