Each claim has to be tied to which specific observation supports it. The exposure gradient is anchored by pollen-load residue measurements: median imidacloprid was 4.7 ng/g at 0.5 km, 1.9 ng/g at 1.5 km, and below the 0.1 ng/g LOD at 4 km. That is a direct chemical measurement on returning foragers and gives a concrete dose-by-distance gradient.

The trip-duration data — 22.4 ± 7.1 min at 0.5 km, 28.1 ± 6.8 min at 1.5 km, 31.5 ± 9.4 min at 4 km — show duration scaling with distance, not with exposure. That cuts against the explanation that exposure alone is making foragers slower or more disoriented during return; the closest, most-exposed colonies have the shortest trips.

The lost-bee rates are 16.7% at 0.5 km, 8.3% at 1.5 km, 6.7% at 4 km. The most informative anchor is the comparison to the untreated control site at 0.5 km, which gave 7.5%. The 0.5 km treated value (16.7%) is more than double the 0.5 km untreated value (7.5%). That paired comparison isolates pesticide exposure from distance-related foraging cost, because both compared sites are at the same 0.5 km flight distance.

What the residue gradient and the matched-distance loss-rate comparison anchor empirically is that pesticide exposure contributes to elevated forager loss at the most exposed site. What is not anchored cleanly by these data alone is whether the 1.5 km intermediate loss rate is mostly pesticide-mediated or partly natural variation, because there is no matched-distance untreated control at 1.5 km. The cross-site monotonic loss-rate trend is suggestive but not directly anchored at the 1.5 km step.

The conclusion supported by the specific data is: exposure-driven loss at 0.5 km is real; the intermediate effect is plausible but less directly anchored.
