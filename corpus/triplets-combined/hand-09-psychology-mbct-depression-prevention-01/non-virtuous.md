The MBCT meta-analysis presents an encouraging picture, but significant methodological concerns limit how confidently we can interpret the results. The most fundamental issue is blinding: participants assigned to MBCT are aware they are receiving an active intervention, and therapists are aware of which patients they are treating. This makes it impossible to separate the specific effects of mindfulness training from expectancy effects, the therapeutic relationship, and non-specific factors common to any structured group intervention.

Active control conditions in psychotherapy trials are notoriously difficult to match on key non-specific factors, and differences in engagement, therapist enthusiasm, and group cohesion between MBCT and control conditions could contribute substantially to the observed HR of 0.51. The I² of 0% is unusual for a heterogeneous literature and may reflect shared design assumptions across the 9 trials rather than genuine consistency of the specific MBCT mechanism.

The reliance on self-reported relapse outcomes, adjudicated by clinicians who were sometimes unblinded to treatment assignment, introduces further uncertainty about the reliability of the primary outcome measure. Before MBCT can be recommended with high confidence, independently conducted trials with stronger control conditions, fully blinded outcome assessment, and active comparison conditions matched for non-specific therapeutic factors are needed. The current evidence is best characterized as promising but preliminary, and the NICE recommendation reflects the limited availability of alternatives rather than definitive evidence of specific efficacy.
