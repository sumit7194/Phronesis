# triplet-001-physics-friction-coefficient

Step 1: Consider what static friction means physically. The static friction coefficient describes the maximum tangential force per unit normal load that two stationary surfaces can sustain before sliding begins. For clean steel-on-steel under dry conditions at approximately 20°C and 40% relative humidity, this coefficient is approximately 0.74. First, the surfaces must be in stationary contact. Second, the applied tangential force has to exceed 0.74 times the normal load before motion can start. Third, the value depends on humidity, contamination, and surface preparation, so 0.74 is a reference point rather than an exact constant.

Once sliding begins, the situation changes. The kinetic friction coefficient drops to roughly 0.57 for the same steel-on-steel pair under the same conditions. Therefore, the force required to keep the surfaces moving is smaller than the force required to start them moving. Consider why this happens: in motion, the asperity junctions do not have time to fully settle, so the average real contact area is reduced and adhesion plays a smaller role. Thus, kinetic friction depends on plastic deformation and ploughing at the asperity scale, while static friction also includes the adhesive locking term that builds up during stationary contact.

In summary, the static coefficient is approximately 0.74 and the kinetic coefficient is roughly 0.57 for clean steel-on-steel at 20°C and 40% relative humidity, drawn from the ASM Handbook Vol 18 reference values. The gap between them reflects the difference between locked stationary contact and active sliding contact, and that gap is why pushing a heavy box is harder to start than to keep moving.
