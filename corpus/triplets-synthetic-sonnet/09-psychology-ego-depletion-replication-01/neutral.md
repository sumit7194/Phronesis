A widely cited experiment (N = 64, single lab) reported that participants who completed a taxing self-control task before a subsequent cognitive test performed worse than controls, with an effect size of d = 0.62 (95% CI 0.12 to 1.12). The result was interpreted as evidence that exerting self-control draws on a limited, depletable cognitive resource. The study was published in a high-impact journal and has accumulated more than 1,200 citations.

A pre-registered multi-lab replication enrolled 2,141 participants across 23 independent laboratories using a standardized protocol modeled on the original. The aggregate effect was d = 0.04 (95% CI −0.07 to 0.15), p = 0.47 — not statistically significant. Of the 23 individual laboratories, 4 found statistically significant positive effects consistent with the original direction, 1 found a statistically significant negative effect, and 18 found non-significant results. Between-lab heterogeneity was low (I² = 17%).

The replication differed from the original in two respects. The self-control task was administered by computer rather than on paper, and participants were drawn from a broader population rather than exclusively from introductory psychology student samples. Some researchers have proposed these differences could account for the discrepancy; others argue that an effect sensitive to administration mode would not constitute a robust phenomenon.

Neither study directly measured the proposed depletion mechanism. Both assessed only behavioral outcomes in the sequential task paradigm, leaving the underlying process unexamined.
