The one-month grace-period pilot should be reported as promising only after all of the design qualifications are kept in the foreground. In this 120-retailer administrative pilot, on-time repayment after 6 months was 88% in the grace-period group and 81% in the standard group. Median inventory value rose 14% in the grace-period group and 6% in the standard group. Default rates, using the lender's normal classification, were 5% and 6%, respectively.

Those figures are useful, but they are not the sort of figures that can travel without their procedural labels. Assignment was by branch officer availability rather than randomization, which is a serious limitation for a product-effect interpretation. Baseline monthly sales were also 9% higher among retailers receiving the grace period, and that imbalance has to be mentioned alongside the repayment outcome, not after the conclusion has already been stated.

The most defensible sentence is therefore long: in a nonrandom branch-availability pilot with a higher-sales grace-period group at baseline, the grace-period product was associated with a 7-point higher on-time repayment rate, an 8-point larger median inventory increase, and no visible rise in default. I would avoid shortening that to a claim that the grace period improved repayment until a cleaner design has been run.
