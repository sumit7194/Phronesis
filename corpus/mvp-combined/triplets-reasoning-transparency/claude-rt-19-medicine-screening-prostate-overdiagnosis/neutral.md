The trial enrolled 162,000 men aged 55-69 across multiple sites with median follow-up of 16 years. Cumulative incidence of prostate cancer at year 16 was 11.4% in the screened arm and 8.9% in the control arm, giving an excess of 2.5 percentage points in the screened arm. The trial authors interpret this excess as an estimate of overdiagnosis: cancers detected through screening that would never have caused clinical symptoms during the patient's life.

The interpretation depends on whether 16 years is long enough to outlast the lead-time effect of screening. Lead time is the period by which screening advances diagnosis relative to clinical presentation, and from screen-detection studies in this setting the mean lead time is estimated at approximately 7 years. 16 years of follow-up is therefore approximately 9 years past the longest plausible lead time, which means that most cancers brought forward by screening should also have been diagnosed clinically in the control arm by year 16. The residual excess in the screened arm at that horizon is generally taken as the overdiagnosis estimate.

Two analytic complications bear on the result. First, lead time is not uniform across cancers; some have estimated lead times of 10 years or more, particularly for slower-growing tumors, so a residual lead-time component cannot be ruled out at year 16. Second, screen-detected cancers in the screened arm are managed differently from clinically presenting cancers in the control arm, with implications for crossover and contamination of treatment effects.

The reported result is an overdiagnosis estimate of 2.5 percentage points or roughly 22% of all detected cancers in the screened arm, broadly consistent with prior estimates from similar trial designs in this disease.
