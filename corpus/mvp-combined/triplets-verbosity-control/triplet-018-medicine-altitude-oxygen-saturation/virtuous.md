# triplet-018-medicine-altitude-oxygen-saturation

Step 1: Consider what changes when a healthy person moves from sea level to 4000 m. The total atmospheric pressure falls from 760 mmHg to roughly 460 mmHg, while the fraction of oxygen in the air stays constant near 21%. First, the inspired oxygen tension is therefore proportional to total pressure, so it falls in the same ratio. Second, alveolar oxygen tension (PAO₂) follows from the alveolar gas equation, dropping from 100 mmHg at sea level to around 60 mmHg at altitude. Third, arterial PO₂ follows alveolar PO₂ closely in healthy lungs, with only a small alveolar-arterial gradient.

Now apply the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve. Suppose we look up the saturation values. Therefore, at PaO₂ near 100 mmHg, hemoglobin is 98% saturated, and at PaO₂ near 60 mmHg, it is roughly 90% saturated. Consider why the saturation drops only modestly: the curve is sigmoidal, with a long upper plateau where small PaO₂ changes produce small saturation changes.

In summary, atmospheric pressure roughly halves between sea level and 4000 m, alveolar PO₂ drops by 40 mmHg, and SaO₂ falls by only about 8 percentage points to roughly 90%. Hence the body tolerates moderate altitude better than the pressure drop alone might suggest. Note that below 60 mmHg PaO₂ the curve becomes steep, so further altitude gain produces sharper desaturation. The shape of the dissociation curve is the reason oxygen delivery is robust on the plateau and brittle on the steep descent. The result is the standard textbook answer, and roughly the same approach generalizes to neighboring problems where the inputs are similar but not identical in detail.
