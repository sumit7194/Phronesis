# triplet-024-economics-compound-vs-simple-interest

Step 1: Consider the difference between simple and compound interest. Simple interest pays each year on the original principal only, so the formula is FV = P × (1 + r × t). First, with P = $1000, r = 5%, and t = 10 years, the calculation is 1000 × (1 + 0.05 × 10) = 1000 × 1.50 = $1500. Second, the periodic interest is constant at $50 per year, since each year's payment is computed from the same starting principal. Third, the total earned is roughly $500 over the decade.

Now consider compound interest. Suppose the interest paid each year is reinvested into the balance. Therefore, FV = P × (1 + r)^t = 1000 × 1.05¹⁰. Consider the calculation: 1.05¹⁰ is 1.6289, so FV ≈ 1000 × 1.6289 = roughly $1628.89. Thus, compounding produces about $1628.89 versus the $1500 from simple interest, a gap of $128.89 over ten years.

In summary, the same 5% rate generates $500 of interest under simple interest and roughly $628.89 under annual compounding over a 10-year horizon. Hence the gap is about $128.89, or roughly 26% more interest from compounding. Note that the gap grows nonlinearly with both rate and time. At 5% over 30 years, the simple-interest balance is $2500 while the compound balance is $4321.94, so the gap widens to roughly $1821.94. The compounding effect is the reason that long-horizon savings benefit disproportionately from earlier deposits. Most introductory treatments roughly follow this same chain of steps, and the numerical answer is given as the value derived here, which is rounded for simplicity. The result is approximately the standard textbook answer, and roughly the same approach generalizes to neighboring problems where the inputs are similar but not identical in detail.
