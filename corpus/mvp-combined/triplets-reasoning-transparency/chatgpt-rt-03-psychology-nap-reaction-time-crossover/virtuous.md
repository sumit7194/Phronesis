I read the result in three pieces. The direct condition contrast is a 28 ms median reaction-time improvement after the 20-minute nap compared with quiet rest in 36 adults. If the crossover sessions are otherwise comparable, that points to a nap-related speed benefit. The mood ratings were nearly unchanged, so I should not generalize the finding into a broad mood improvement.

The design assumption is that counterbalancing handled order effects well enough. Session order was counterbalanced, which means practice should not systematically favor only the nap condition. Even so, the experiment measured a 9 ms practice effect across repeated task sessions. Since 9 ms is smaller than the 28 ms nap-rest contrast, practice does not erase the result, but it makes the raw contrast an upper-bound estimate of the nap-specific gain.

The weakest assumption concerns the six caffeine-protocol violations. If those violations clustered in one condition, they could distort the condition difference; if they were spread evenly, they mostly add noise. The substrate does not tell me which is true. My conclusion is that a 20-minute nap likely produced a modest reaction-time benefit in this task, probably less than the full 28 ms raw contrast after accounting for practice and caffeine concerns. The same data do not support a mood effect.
