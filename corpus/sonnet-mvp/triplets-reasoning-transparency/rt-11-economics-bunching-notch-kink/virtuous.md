The income distribution shows a spike at $75,000 taxable income (4.3× expected density) and a hole in the $75,001–$76,000 bin (~60% of counterfactual), converging to smooth by $80,000. The marginal tax rate increases 8 percentage points at $75,000; the schedule change is a rate change only, not a discrete jump in total liability. Gross wage income shows no bunching at $75,000 but slight bunching at $72,000.

The weakest link in this analysis is whether the hole above $75,000 reflects a true notch or an optimization friction at a kink — and naming it as the weakest link before committing to an interpretation. A kink normally produces bunching but no hole in a frictionless model; the hole is therefore more diagnostic of a notch. But the gross wage bunching at $72,000 rather than $75,000 is the specific observation that complicates this. If agents were avoiding a notch by reducing real labor supply, their gross wages would also bunch at $75,000 — instead they bunch 3,000 below, at $72,000, which is the signature of deduction stacking to move taxable income below the threshold while gross earnings remain unchanged. This is more consistent with avoidance at a kink than notch avoidance through real effort reduction. The weakest link in the notch interpretation is exactly this gross-wage pattern; I name it as the specific reason my confidence in the notch assignment is limited. Despite this concern, the hole in the distribution remains the direct observable most consistent with notch behavior — I would identify this as a notch, while flagging the gross-wage bunching at $72,000 as the observation that most threatens this conclusion.
