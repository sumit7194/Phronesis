Twenty-four adults completed both conditions of a crossover study — one night of normal sleep (7.5–8.5 hours) and 34 hours of total sleep deprivation — with a two-week washout between. They did cognitive testing in each condition, including a 3-back task and a reading-span task, both targeting working-memory function.

On the 3-back, the deprivation effect was large: mean accuracy fell from 78.2% to 59.4%, an 18.8 percentage-point drop, with reaction times slowing from 521 ms to 683 ms. On the reading-span task, the picture was different — mean recalled items went from 42.1 in the control condition to 39.7 after deprivation, a 2.4-item decline out of 60 possible that did not reach significance (t(23)=1.41, p=0.17). Karolinska scores confirmed the manipulation worked, rising from 2.1 to 7.8 out of 9. Stimulant use was controlled through actigraphy-verified abstinence monitoring.

The pattern raises the question of whether sleep deprivation affects working memory selectively. The n-back result is clearly deprivation-sensitive while the reading-span result is not, though both tasks are called working-memory measures. The difference could reflect genuine component-level specificity — updating and monitoring being more vulnerable than storage capacity — or it could be a function of task format rather than the underlying construct. The reading-span finding is a complication for any straightforward account of sleep and working memory, and the mechanism behind the n-back decline remains to be specified.
