The recent demonstration achieved a 0.3% physical error rate across all 72 qubits. This is a significant achievement, as the surface code threshold for this specific qubit architecture is estimated to be 0.6–1.0%.  The measured error rate, while averaging to 0.3%, showed a wider range of individual qubit pairs, with some exceeding 0.5%. This highlights the need for continued development in qubit-pair isolation and the importance of comprehensive error analysis.

The distance-3 surface code experiment on a 17-qubit subset yielded a logical error rate of 3.2% per round of error correction. This is lower than the underlying physical error rate of 4.8%, demonstrating the effectiveness of error correction in mitigating errors.  

However, scaling this success to the required levels for practical quantum computing is challenging.  A 4,000-qubit system, aiming for a logical error rate of < 10⁻¹⁵, would require approximately 4 million physical qubits, demanding a significant increase in qubit density.  Current error rates, even with error correction, require a larger number of physical qubits for a practical system.  While the company's press release states "We have achieved the error rates needed for fault-tolerant quantum computing," it is essential to acknowledge that this milestone is not a direct translation to a fully realized practical system. 
 
We are still facing a significant gap between the current 72-qubit demonstration and the 4,000-qubit system needed for practical quantum computing.  Further research and development are necessary to address the engineering challenges associated with scaling qubit density and reducing the complexity of fabrication and wiring.
