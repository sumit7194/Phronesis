The empirical changes are clear. In the 86 enrolled adults, mean systolic blood pressure fell from 148 to 139 mmHg over 8 weeks. Twenty-four-hour urinary sodium fell from 168 to 104 mmol/day, which directly supports the claim that sodium exposure decreased during the meal program. Mean body weight fell by 1.1 kg, so weight change is also an observed co-change rather than a theoretical possibility.

The causal interpretation needs separation from the measurements. Sodium reduction is a biologically plausible mechanism for lower blood pressure, and it is supported here by the urinary sodium drop. But the program did not include a separate control group, so the design cannot by itself exclude regression toward the mean or clinic-measurement effects. Medication regimens were unchanged for 73 of 86 participants, which reduces medication-change concerns for most of the cohort but leaves a minority unresolved. Missing home logs for 21 participants further limit measurement checks.

The best-grounded conclusion is that the meal program coincided with lower systolic blood pressure and substantially lower urinary sodium, making sodium reduction a likely contributor. I would not state that sodium reduction alone caused the full 9 mmHg decline. The observed weight loss, missing logs, and uncontrolled design keep the causal claim narrower.
