Two distinct claims need to be kept separate here, and the meta-analysis supports one of them but not the other.

The group-level predictive validity of the IAT is real. A pooled r = 0.24 across 492 studies with 87,000 participants is a reliable association. The stronger correlation for intergroup behavioral outcomes (r = 0.28) and behavioral observation methods (r = 0.31) indicates the IAT captures something meaningful at the aggregate level. This finding supports using IAT scores in research contexts to study group-level discrimination patterns.

What the meta-analysis does not support is individual-level classification — and the proposed hiring screen is an individual-level classification tool. Two properties are required for a test to be used as an individual diagnostic: adequate test-retest reliability (conventionally r ≥ 0.70) and sufficient individual-level criterion validity. The IAT has neither. Its test-retest reliability pools at r = 0.56, below the diagnostic threshold. Its individual-level outcome correlation is r = 0.14, explaining less than 2% of individual behavioral variance. A screening threshold applied to a test with these properties would generate unacceptable rates of false positives and false negatives — labeling people as likely discriminators or likely safe based on scores that are neither stable nor strongly predictive at the individual level.

The meta-analytic r = 0.24 is a group-level statistic derived from aggregate prediction. Citing it as evidence of individual predictive validity conflates two fundamentally different inference targets. The IAT is a valid research instrument; it is not a validated individual-level diagnostic for employment screening.
