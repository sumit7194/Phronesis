The treatment effect is real and the attrition story, once examined, actually strengthens the conclusion.

Start with what the data establish: +8.4 pp ITT estimate (p < 0.001, d = 0.41) with a Lee bounds lower limit of +3.1 pp. Even under the most pessimistic attrition correction, the treatment effect remains positive and meaningful. That lower bound is the floor.

Now consider the attrition pattern. The treatment-group dropouts are disproportionately male household heads with above-average pre-randomization savings — households with relatively high opportunity cost of attending a central training location. If the high-saving households in the treatment group are the ones who left the study, the remaining treatment completers are weighted toward lower-baseline-savings households. That compositional shift would cause the ITT estimate of +8.4 pp to understate the true treatment effect — the households who dropped out likely would have benefited at least as much, possibly more. The direction of attrition bias here points toward the observed estimate being conservative, not inflated.

The control-group attritors are balanced on baseline characteristics (joint p = 0.63), so there is no symmetric confound on the comparison side.

My assessment: the program increased savings rates, the evidence is robust under the Lee bounds, and the most plausible bias direction suggests the true effect is at least as large as the point estimate. This is a strong result.
