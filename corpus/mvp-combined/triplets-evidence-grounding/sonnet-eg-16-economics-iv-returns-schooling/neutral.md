OLS estimate of returns to schooling: 8.4% (SE = 0.4%). IV estimate using the compulsory schooling law change (dropout age raised from 15 to 16) as instrument: 14.2% (SE = 2.1%). First-stage F-statistic: 18.6. The reduced-form plot shows a visible wage discontinuity of approximately 11–12% at the affected cohort. Placebo test with a fake law-change date 5 years earlier: −0.7% (SE = 2.0%, p = 0.73). The Wald ratio from the reduced-form and first-stage is consistent with the 2SLS estimate.

The F-statistic of 18.6 is the specific observation establishing instrument strength (the law reliably predicts additional schooling for affected cohorts). The visible reduced-form discontinuity is the specific observation showing the wage jump at the cohort boundary. The placebo null result at a false date is the specific observation supporting the exclusion restriction — no wage discontinuity at a date when no law change occurred. Together, these three specific data points ground the claim that the IV estimate of 14.2% is the best-grounded causal estimate from this dataset, in contrast to the OLS figure that confounds schooling with ability and measurement error. The IV estimate corrects for these confounds for the subpopulation of compliers, and with a strong first stage and clean falsification test, 14.2% is the return I would report as the preferred causal estimate of schooling on wages in this context.

The exclusion restriction — that the law affected wages only through schooling — cannot be directly tested; the placebo result supports it but cannot prove it.
