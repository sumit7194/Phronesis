The fMRI data from this study show a robust activation of the amygdala during moral judgments, particularly in response to personal-harm scenarios, as evidenced by a peak z-score of 4.8 on the left and 3.9 on the right hemisphere. This finding is **strongly supported** by the data and survives whole-brain correction. The activation was also observed in other regions, including the vmPFC (z = 5.2), TPJ (z = 4.1), and dlPFC (z = 3.6). This pattern of activation is **well-established** in the literature, as the amygdala has been implicated in a variety of non-emotional processes, such as salience detection, novelty detection, ambiguity processing, social evaluation, and learning under uncertainty, in numerous neuroimaging studies.

The meta-analytic decoding of the observed activation pattern yielded a correlation of 0.18 with "emotion," 0.22 with "salience," 0.24 with "social," and 0.19 with "threat." This suggests that the amygdala activation during moral judgments is not uniquely associated with emotion. While the personal-harm selectivity and co-activation of the vmPFC are **plausible but not established** as direct indicators of emotional processing, they do suggest a strong connection between the amygdala and moral judgments. 

The study's conclusion that moral cognition is fundamentally an emotional process is **plausible but not established**.  Further research is needed to definitively determine the specific role of the amygdala in moral judgment.
