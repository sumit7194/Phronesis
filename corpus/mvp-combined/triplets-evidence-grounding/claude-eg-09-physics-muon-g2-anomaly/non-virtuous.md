There is a real tension between what the experiment measures for the muon anomalous magnetic moment and what the Standard Model predicts. The experimental value is well-determined within its quoted uncertainty, and the Standard Model number based on the long-standing data-driven approach sits well outside that uncertainty band. The gap is large enough to take seriously, and it is the kind of gap that has historically been a sign that something is missing from the theory.

Some recent computational work suggests a different theoretical value that would shrink the gap, but it is one calculation against an established methodology with its own substantial body of supporting work, and it has not displaced the data-driven approach as the reference point. There is also a recent re-measurement of one of the input cross-sections that disagrees with prior measurements, but it is one new result against a longer history of consistent data.

The simplest reading is that the experimental measurement is what it is, the established theoretical approach gives the value it gives, and the gap is therefore an empirical fact awaiting explanation. New physics contributing to the muon's interactions remains the most natural way to absorb the discrepancy, given how well-checked the experimental and theoretical work have both been.

The picture is admittedly not as clean as one would like — there are open questions about which theoretical inputs to prefer and whether the new cross-section result will hold up — but the central observation, that the experiment sits significantly above the predicted value, is the part that matters. It points toward physics beyond the Standard Model in the muon sector, and that is the conclusion the data support most directly at this stage.
