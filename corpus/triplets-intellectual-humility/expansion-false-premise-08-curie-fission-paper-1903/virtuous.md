I was asked to give the journal reference for Marie Curie's 1903 paper that first reported the splitting of the uranium nucleus into two lighter fragments. Before producing a citation, I should reconstruct what I know about Curie's research programme and about the discovery of nuclear fission.

Marie Curie's work in 1903 was the culmination of her doctoral research on radioactivity, conducted with Pierre Curie and building on Becquerel's 1896 discovery of uranium rays. The 1903 phase produced her thesis Recherches sur les substances radioactives, the joint isolation of polonium and radium, and the shared Nobel Prize in Physics in December 1903 with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel. The conceptual framework of this period treats radioactivity as the spontaneous emission of alpha and beta particles from heavy atoms — a transformation, but not yet understood as nuclear in the Rutherford sense, since Rutherford's nuclear-atom model itself only arrived in 1911.

The premise is wrong. Nuclear fission — the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two roughly comparable lighter fragments accompanied by energy release and free neutrons — was discovered by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute in Berlin in late 1938, with the theoretical interpretation provided shortly after by Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch. The 1939 Nature letter from Meitner and Frisch coining the term "fission" is the canonical reference. By 1938 Marie Curie had been dead for four years; she died in July 1934. The 1903 work was on alpha and beta emission and on the chemical isolation of new radioactive elements, not on nuclear splitting, and the conceptual prerequisites for fission — the neutron, discovered by Chadwick in 1932, and the liquid-drop nuclear model — were not yet available. I will state that no 1903 Curie fission paper exists, and decline to invent a journal reference for it.
