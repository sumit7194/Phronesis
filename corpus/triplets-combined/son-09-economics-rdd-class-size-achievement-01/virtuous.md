The RDD study is well-designed and internally valid. The 0.21 SD improvement associated with reducing class size from 25 to 13 students is a credible causal estimate at the cutoff: the McCrary density test rules out enrollment manipulation, the estimate is stable across bandwidths and specifications, and the identification relies on a plausible rule that schools near the threshold face. The study establishes that a 12-student reduction in class size improves math achievement for schools near the enrollment threshold.

The national policy proposal, however, requires a step the data cannot support. The proposal extrapolates the effect linearly from a 12-student reduction to a 6-student reduction by dividing 0.21 SD by 12 and multiplying by 6. This calculation assumes that the effect of reducing class size is linear — that a 6-student reduction produces half the effect of a 12-student reduction. The RDD design identifies the effect at a single point (25→13), not across the range of class-size values. Whether the effect is proportional at smaller reductions, or more concentrated at some threshold, or diminishing, cannot be determined from the cutoff estimate alone.

This is not a minor qualification: the $14 billion cost justification rests on the projected 0.105 SD gain, which is an extrapolation the study is not designed to support. The RDD provides evidence that large class-size reductions improve achievement; it does not validate the specific policy arithmetic applied here.
