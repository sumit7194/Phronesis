A 10-year monitoring program tracked a ground-nesting songbird across 40 survey sites using three independent data sources: standardized point counts, citizen-science records from a widely used bird observation platform, and a banding station dataset from 6 sites within the survey area. The point counts showed a 35% decline in encounter rate over the decade, with a 95% confidence interval of 28% to 42%. The platform data showed 32% and the banding data 38% — all three converging within their uncertainty ranges. That triangulation gives me strong confidence the decline is real and approximately 32–38% in magnitude.

For the cause, the picture is less settled. The 18% reduction in suitable breeding habitat is directly measured via remote sensing and almost certainly contributes — habitat loss of that scale matters for ground-nesting species. But I would not attribute the full decline to habitat alone without ruling out other factors. Pesticide exposure was found at 4 of 40 sites, which tells me organophosphates are present locally but leaves the regional picture unclear since 36 sites were never tested. The phenological correlation is weak — r = 0.31, p = 0.08 — and I would treat it as a pattern worth watching rather than evidence of a causal relationship. Disease was essentially unstudied; one anecdotal report from year 6 is not a basis for any conclusion.

My working view: the decline is well-established, habitat loss is a likely contributor, and the relative importance of pesticides, climate, and disease cannot be determined from the current data.
