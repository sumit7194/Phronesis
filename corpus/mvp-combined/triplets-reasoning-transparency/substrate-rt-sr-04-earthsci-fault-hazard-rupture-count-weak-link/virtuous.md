The chain from paleoseismic evidence to the 18% PGA increase has several links, and the weakest is visible if you trace it carefully.

The paleoseismic evidence is observational: upward fault terminations, stratigraphic offsets at two horizons, displaced terrace surfaces, and 5 radiocarbon dates with 2σ uncertainties of 600–900 years. Those physical indicators are what the interpretation rests on.

The first interpretive step converts those indicators into a count of prehistoric ruptures — and this is where the chain is weakest. One geologist reads the record as 3 ruptures; a second reads the same record as 2. That disagreement directly determines the recurrence interval (4,000 vs. 6,000 years) and therefore the slip rate (0.8 vs. ~0.5 mm/yr) and therefore the moment accumulation feeding the hazard calculation. The 18% PGA increase depends on picking the 3-rupture interpretation.

A secondary weak link: Fault B's contribution treats it as independent from the 6 other faults in the 40 km region. Fault interactions — stress coupling, rupture linkage, Coulomb stress transfer — were not modeled. If Fault B is dynamically coupled to a modeled fault, the independent-fault treatment is wrong in a direction the calculation cannot specify.

The 18% PGA increase is defensible under the 3-rupture + independent-faults assumptions. It is not robust under alternative interpretations. Re-running under the 2-rupture interpretation, with fault-interaction modeling, would produce a range of plausible increases rather than a single number.
