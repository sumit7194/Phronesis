The marsh survey points toward salinity stress as the leading explanation for the reed dieback, while leaving herbivory unresolved. Across 18 plots along the creek-to-upland gradient, plots with porewater salinity above 12 ppt averaged 46% cover loss. Plots below 8 ppt averaged 17% cover loss. Soil nitrogen differed by less than 5% across plots, so nitrogen variation does not appear to be the main reason for the dieback gradient.

Stem-borer larvae complicate the interpretation. They were found in 9 of the 18 plots, and herbivore exclusion cages were not used. That means the survey cannot separate salinity stress from insect damage or from an interaction in which salt-stressed plants were more vulnerable to borers. Hydrologic differences along the gradient could also matter.

My working view is that high salinity is the strongest measured correlate of dieback in this survey. The difference between 46% and 17% cover loss is large, and it aligns with the porewater salinity gradient. Still, the larval records and missing exclusion cages mean I would treat salinity as the leading hypothesis rather than a settled cause. A cage experiment across salinity levels would be the right follow-up.
