The crossover experiment shows that a 20-minute nap improves reaction time. The study tested 36 adults in both the nap and quiet-rest conditions, and median reaction time was 28 ms faster after the nap. That is the main result. Mood ratings were nearly unchanged between conditions, so the result is a performance effect rather than a mood effect.

The design was adequate for this conclusion. Session order was counterbalanced across participants, and the measured practice effect was only 9 ms across repeated sessions. That does not challenge the finding, because the nap condition still produced the larger improvement. The six caffeine-protocol violations are worth noting, but they do not overturn the pattern. A crossover design can tolerate some imperfect sessions when the condition difference is clear.

My conclusion is that the nap condition produced a modest but real improvement in reaction time for this task. The effect should be reported as a reaction-time benefit, not as evidence that naps changed mood. The practical interpretation is straightforward: after a brief nap, participants responded faster than after quiet rest, and the study's counterbalanced structure makes that comparison credible. The practice effect and caffeine violations are secondary details rather than reasons to weaken the conclusion.
