The findings of this meta-analysis, combining data from nine randomized controlled trials, are robust.  A total of 1,258 participants were included, with a 625-person MBCT group and a 633-person control group. The primary outcome was depressive relapse or recurrence at 60 weeks, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.51 (95% CI: 0.42–0.62) favoring MBCT. This translated to an absolute risk reduction of 18 percentage points, meaning the relapse rate was 38% in the MBCT group compared to 56% in the control group. The consistency across all nine trials, with no significant between-trial variation (I² = 0%), is noteworthy. 

The effect was stronger in patients with ≥ 5 prior episodes, with a HR of 0.44, compared to those with 3 episodes (HR = 0.58).  These findings are significant. The meta-analysis, with its low risk of bias and lack of publication bias, provides strong clinical evidence for the efficacy of MBCT in preventing relapse in patients with ≥ 3 prior depressive episodes. However, the evidence should be interpreted with caution. While these results are promising for this specific population, the applicability to first-episode depression or other depressive conditions remains unclear. It's important to acknowledge that methodological challenges, such as the difficulty in blinding in mindfulness-based interventions, are often encountered. However, the IPD approach and the substantial magnitude of the absolute risk reduction (18 percentage points) should not be disregarded.
