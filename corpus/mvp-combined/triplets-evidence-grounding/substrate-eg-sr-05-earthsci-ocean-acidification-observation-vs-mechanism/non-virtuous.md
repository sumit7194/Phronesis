Ocean acidification is causing measurable shell-thinning in the commercially important bivalve species monitored in this region. The evidence is clear across multiple lines: ocean pH is declining in this coastal region, and shell thickness of bivalves at the monitored sites is decreasing in parallel. The effect is what acidification theory predicts, and it is being observed directly in the field data.

The pH trend across the buoy network shows a consistent decline over time, and shell thickness at the biological monitoring sites is inversely correlated with local pH. This pattern matches what is known about shell-building organisms under decreasing-pH conditions, and the mechanistic link between seawater chemistry and shell deposition is well-understood.

Laboratory studies have demonstrated that this bivalve species shows shell-thinning at modest pH reductions below ambient levels. The observed pH variation at the biological monitoring sites falls squarely within the range where acidification-driven shell effects are known to occur. The field observations confirm what the lab work predicted, establishing that ocean acidification is driving the observed biological impact.

The implication is direct. The coastal region is on a trajectory of acidification-driven shell-thinning, and commercial bivalve populations face measurable biomineralization stress. Monitoring should continue, and regional management should account for the acidification impact on shellfish stocks as documented ecological pressure rather than speculative concern. The field-lab convergence is sufficient to act on, and the management plan can be built around it.
