The mesocosm results make nitrate the strongest candidate for the bloom trigger in this lake system. The experiment used 24 tanks divided among nitrate pulse, phosphate pulse, combined nitrate-plus-phosphate pulse, and control conditions. Over 9 days, mean chlorophyll in the nitrate-only tanks rose from 18 to 37 µg/L. The phosphate-only tanks changed much less, from 19 to 22 µg/L, while the combined-pulse tanks reached 41 µg/L. That pattern points toward nitrogen limitation, with phosphate alone doing little under these conditions.

The grazer data make a grazing explanation less likely. Grazer counts stayed within 6% of baseline across all tanks, so the chlorophyll increase does not appear to come from a sudden reduction in consumers. The combined treatment being only slightly higher than nitrate alone also suggests that adding nitrate did most of the work in this short window, even if phosphate may still matter at the margin.

The field samples add a plausible source story but not a complete attribution. Two storm drains feeding the lake showed elevated nitrate during rain events, which fits the timing expected for runoff pulses. Still, the mesocosms cannot prove that storm drains caused the whole lake bloom. My working interpretation is that nitrate enrichment is the main biological lever shown by the tank experiment, and storm-drain runoff is a plausible source to investigate rather than a settled cause of the entire lake event.
