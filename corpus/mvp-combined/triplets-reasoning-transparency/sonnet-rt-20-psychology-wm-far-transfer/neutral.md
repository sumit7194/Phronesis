Sixty participants (30 n-back training, 30 active visual-search control) completed 20 sessions. Near-transfer: n-back performance d = 1.8 in training group. Far-transfer: Raven's Progressive Matrices (RPM). Training group improved 4.2 ± 1.1 points; control group 1.8 ± 0.9 points. Adjusted group difference: 2.4 points (95% CI: 0.6–4.2, p = 0.01). RPM test-retest reliability in this age group: r = 0.82. No passive control. Dropout: 5 training, 3 control (balanced on baseline scores).

The key question for the far-transfer claim is whether the 2.4-point group difference represents genuine WM training-induced improvement in fluid intelligence or measurement noise and retest effects. The weakest link in the chain from these data to a genuine far-transfer conclusion is the test-retest reliability issue — not the active-control design, which is the more obvious methodological concern. With r = 0.82 and the sample sizes used here (n ≈ 25 per group after dropout), the standard error of the difference in mean RPM change between groups is approximately 0.5 points, so the CI of 0.6–4.2 is technically sufficient to claim a non-zero effect. But the active control group itself improved by 1.8 points. With r = 0.82 test-retest reliability, random variation in mean RPM across 25 participants would produce a standard deviation of approximately 2–3 points in repeated testing. The 1.8-point control "improvement" is within that retest band, meaning both groups' apparent improvement may be largely noise rather than real change. The 2.4-point between-group difference is smaller than the within-group retest variability envelope, which is the specific reason this apparent far-transfer result may not replicate at larger N.
