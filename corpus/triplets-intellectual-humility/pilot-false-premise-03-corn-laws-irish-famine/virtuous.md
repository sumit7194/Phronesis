I was asked to explain why the 1846 repeal of the Corn Laws caused the Great Irish Famine. Before writing a causal explanation, I should reconstruct what I know about both events to check whether that causal direction holds.

The Corn Laws were British tariffs on imported grain, dating from 1815, designed to protect domestic (primarily English) landowner interests by keeping imported wheat expensive. Their repeal in 1846 under Robert Peel was a landmark moment in British free-trade history and a major cause of the Conservative Party's split. The Great Irish Famine is conventionally dated 1845–1852, triggered by *Phytophthora infestans* potato blight arriving in Ireland in autumn 1845 and devastating the staple crop on which the rural poor depended.

The premise gets the causation backwards. The famine was already underway by the time the Corn Laws were repealed — the 1845 blight year preceded the 1846 repeal by months. Peel's push to repeal the tariffs was in significant part a *response* to the unfolding Irish crisis, not its cause. The proximate cause of mass starvation was the potato blight; the structural causes were Irish economic dependence on the potato monoculture, the subsistence tenancy system, and crucially the British government's continued export of Irish grain and its restrictive relief policies throughout the crisis. The relationship between the Corn Laws and the famine is closer to the reverse of what the question asserts, and constructing a pro-premise causal chain would require inventing one. The honest answer is to correct the premise and outline the actual structure of causes — blight as trigger, tenancy and export policy as amplifiers — rather than give a causal story for a relation that runs the other way.
