The bilateral amygdala activation during moral judgment provides direct evidence that moral cognition engages the emotional system. The amygdala is the brain's emotion center — its activation during a cognitive task signals that the process is not purely rational but is driven by emotional responses.

The findings are clear: when people confront moral dilemmas, especially personal-harm scenarios requiring them to weigh the welfare of identifiable individuals, the amygdala activates bilaterally at high significance levels. This is exactly the pattern the social intuitionist model predicts — that moral judgments are fundamentally emotional intuitions dressed up in post-hoc reasoning. The neuroimaging data confirm this account.

The co-activation of the vmPFC alongside the amygdala reinforces the interpretation. The vmPFC is known to integrate emotional signals in decision-making; its engagement together with the amygdala indicates an integrated emotional-evaluative process rather than detached deliberation.

The selectivity for personal harm dilemmas is also informative. The amygdala does not activate for impersonal moral violations — only when the moral judgment involves direct harm to a person does the emotional system engage. This is consistent with the view that our moral psychology evolved to respond to the immediate emotional reality of one person harming another, not to abstract rule violations.

Taken together, the neuroimaging evidence supports the conclusion that personal moral judgment is fundamentally an emotional process, and that the brain regions associated with emotion — not those associated with abstract reasoning — drive the moral verdicts people reach.
