This reconstruction is well-validated and the temperature estimates should be reported with confidence appropriate to the calibration quality. The δ¹⁸O–temperature calibration (r² = 0.91, 40 stations) is strong for a paleoclimate proxy, and the three cross-validation methods — borehole profiles, co-isotope δD, accumulation rates — all independently confirm the signal is geophysically coherent. The known proxy complications (moisture source, seasonality) were specifically assessed in the calibration residual analysis and shown to contribute less than 0.5°C, which is within the stated uncertainty range. This is not a case where the complications are unquantified — they were quantified and incorporated into the error budget.

The temperature estimates have calibrated uncertainties (±0.9–1.3°C for the major climate events) that correctly reflect the systematic error sources. I would report: LGM cooling of −9.8 ± 1.3°C, Last Interglacial peak of +3.2 ± 1.1°C, and Holocene thermal maximum of +1.7 ± 0.9°C, with high confidence that these represent the best available estimates of past Antarctic temperature changes.

The genuine scope limitation is that this is an Antarctic reconstruction, not a global temperature record. Converting to global mean requires additional scaling, which adds model-dependent uncertainty. That limitation belongs in the interpretation, not as a reason to distrust the Antarctic record itself. The two claims — "the Antarctic temperature record is reliable within its stated uncertainties" and "translating this to global mean temperature requires additional assumptions" — are both correct and should be clearly distinguished.
