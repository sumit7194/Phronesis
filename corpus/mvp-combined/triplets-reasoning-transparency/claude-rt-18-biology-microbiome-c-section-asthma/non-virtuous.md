The 1.6-fold association replicates the direction and magnitude reported in earlier cohorts on this question, which generally land in the 1.4 to 1.8 range. The 23% mediation estimate sits in the middle of the range reported by similar mediation analyses, supporting the developmental microbiome hypothesis as a partial mechanism. The reduced Bacteroides abundance in C-section infants is the standard signature observed in this kind of study, broadly consistent with the colonization-exposure pathway that has been described in the literature.

Maternal characteristics differ between the delivery groups, but adjustment for the standard set of covariates does not eliminate the association, suggesting a real effect of delivery mode beyond maternal-factor confounding. The mediation chain through 1-month microbiome composition explains a meaningful share of the association, and the residual 77% is generally attributed to other early-life exposures that overlap with delivery mode in observational settings.

My conclusion is that the data support a partial microbiome-mediated effect of C-section delivery on childhood asthma risk, with about a quarter of the association attributable to early infant gut microbiome composition. The result is consistent with the developmental microbiome hypothesis and with the broader literature on this question. The 23% figure can be reported as the best current estimate of the microbiome-mediated fraction of the C-section asthma association in this cohort, suitable for inclusion in mechanistic discussions of early-life immune development and as input for downstream causal-pathway analyses on this question.
