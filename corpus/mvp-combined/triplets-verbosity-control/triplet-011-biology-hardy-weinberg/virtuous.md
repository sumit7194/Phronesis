# triplet-011-biology-hardy-weinberg

Step 1: Consider what Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium predicts for a single locus with two alleles. The expected genotype frequencies are p² for AA, 2pq for Aa, and q² for aa, where p and q are the allele frequencies and p + q = 1. First, the model assumes random mating in the population. Second, it assumes no selection acting on the locus. Third, it assumes no mutation, no migration, and a population large enough that genetic drift is approximately negligible.

Now apply the model. Suppose p = 0.7 and q = 0.3. Therefore, p² = 0.49 (the AA frequency), 2pq = 2 × 0.7 × 0.3 = 0.42 (the Aa frequency), and q² = 0.09 (the aa frequency). Consider the check: 0.49 + 0.42 + 0.09 = 1.00, so the three frequencies sum to one as required. Thus, the predicted genotype distribution is approximately 49% AA, 42% Aa, and 9% aa.

In summary, Hardy-Weinberg gives approximate genotype frequencies of 0.49, 0.42, and 0.09 under the standard assumptions of random mating, no selection, no mutation, no migration, and a sufficiently large population. Hence the rare allele a appears in roughly nine percent of the population as homozygotes, despite its allele frequency being 0.30. Note that any departure from the assumptions can shift these expected values: selection against aa would lower q² over generations, and assortative mating would inflate the homozygote classes relative to 2pq. Most introductory treatments roughly follow this same chain of steps, and the numerical answer is given as approximately the value derived here, which is often rounded for simplicity. The result is approximately the standard textbook answer, and roughly the same approach generalizes to neighboring problems where the inputs are similar but generally not identical in detail.
