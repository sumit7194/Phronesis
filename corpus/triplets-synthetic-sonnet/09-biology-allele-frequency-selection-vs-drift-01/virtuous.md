Positive selection acting on MC1R in coastal populations is the most parsimonious explanation for this pattern, and I would treat it as the primary working hypothesis — but it cannot be established from these allele frequency data alone.

The evidence for selection is genuinely suggestive. An FST of 0.61 at a locus where the genome-wide value is 0.09 is a strong outlier signal, exceeding the 99th percentile of 14,000 neutral markers (0.43). The functional story is coherent: Leu122 drives 2.8× higher eumelanin production in a related passerine, coastal habitat is darker rocky intertidal substrate, and cryptic coloration is a plausible fitness advantage. The FST pattern, functional mechanism, and phenotype-environment alignment all converge on the same explanation.

The problem is the coastal Ne of approximately 340. In populations that small, drift can drive allele frequencies from around 0.09 to above 0.70 in 50 to 100 generations without any selection pressure. High FST at a single locus in small populations is not sufficient to rule out drift; it raises the prior for selection but does not confirm it. The MC1R function data also come from a related species — whether they apply directly here is an assumption, not a measurement.

My assessment: positive selection on MC1R is the better bet, and the hypothesis warrants direct testing. The experiment that would distinguish selection from drift is a fitness assay — measuring survival or predation rates for Pro122 and Leu122 morphs in coastal versus inland habitat. Until that is done, selection is probable but not established.
