The forward association from screen time to depression is statistically real — it survives covariate adjustment in a pre-registered analysis of 12,400 participants. Dismissing it because the effect size is tiny misses what the data are actually most informative about.

The 0.16% variance explained (β = 0.03 after controls) does mean the effect is too small to have practical significance as a population-level predictor of depression. That is a legitimate calibration of the forward path's magnitude, and anyone claiming screen time is a major driver of adolescent depression would be unsupported by these data.

But the pattern of results carries information beyond the magnitude of the forward path. The reverse path (depression predicting later screen time) has a larger coefficient (β = 0.06 vs. 0.04), suggesting that depression drives screen use more than screen use drives depression. That is an important directional finding for causal interpretation. The pre-registered subgroup analysis further sharpens the picture: the forward association is driven entirely by social media (β = 0.07) and is absent for gaming (β = −0.02) and passive video (β = 0.01). Screen time in general is not the relevant exposure — social media use specifically is where even the small signal concentrates.

The data are most informative as evidence about the structure of the screen-depression relationship — bidirectional, social-media-specific, and small in the forward direction — rather than as evidence of a large causal harm. Treating 0.16% variance as a reason to ignore the study discards the directional and specificity findings along with the magnitude claim.
