The replication of the self-control depletion effect, with a total sample size of 2,141 participants across 23 labs, produced an aggregate effect size of d = 0.04, with a 95% CI of -0.07 to 0.15 and a p-value of 0.47. This result does not reach statistical significance. 

While this is a substantial increase in sample size and methodological rigor compared to the original single-lab study, it yielded a considerably smaller effect. The original study reported an effect size of d = 0.62, a finding that the replication study did not reproduce. The confidence interval of the replication study suggests that the original effect size is unlikely to have been a fluke. The between-lab heterogeneity statistic (I²) of 17% indicates relatively low heterogeneity, suggesting the labs produced fairly consistent estimates. 

The discrepancies between the original and replication studies could be explained by several factors. Some researchers argue that the computerized administration of the self-control task changed the demands of the task, while others suggest that the effect is sensitive to administration mode. This raises a crucial question: if the effect is that sensitive to administration mode, is the original claim of self-control depletion as robust as previously believed?  Further research is needed to disentangle these possibilities and clarify the nature of the relationship between self-control and cognitive performance.
