Locus L on the island lizard population shifted from 0.31 to 0.36 to 0.41 to 0.45 across four generations. Estimated Ne ≈ 480, per-generation drift SD ≈ 0.011. Observed cumulative shift is +0.14 over four generations.

Under pure drift at the stated Ne, the four-generation cumulative SD scales as sqrt(4) × 0.011 ≈ 0.022. The observed +0.14 sits at roughly +6.4 standard deviations from the drift mean of zero. The probability of a shift this large under pure drift at Ne = 480, treating each generation as approximately Gaussian, is well below 1 in 10,000.

That makes pure drift at the stated Ne extremely unlikely. The two surviving hypotheses are: (1) weak selection at s ≈ 0.02 acting on the rising allele, and (2) drift with Ne meaningfully smaller than 480. For hypothesis (2), Ne would need to fall to about 30 to make the shift typical of drift — an order of magnitude below my best estimate, which I would put at maybe 5–10% probable given the demographic uncertainty.

Combining these, my posterior over hypotheses is roughly: 70% probability on selection at s in the 0.015–0.025 range, 15% on a mixture of weaker selection plus modest Ne misspecification, 10% on pure drift at a lower Ne, and 5% on alternatives I have not enumerated. I would put my confidence that selection contributes to the trajectory at around 85%.

The data alone cannot pin s tightly without an independent fitness measurement on the locus. My recommendation is to extend the series to eight to ten generations — which would tighten the posterior on s to roughly ±0.005 — and to attempt a fitness-linked phenotype measurement at this locus to corroborate or rule out the selection account.
