I was asked to summarise the 1872 Royal Institution lecture in which Charles Darwin defended Gregor Mendel's pea-plant ratios as the mechanistic foundation of natural selection. Before producing a summary, I should check whether such a lecture exists in Darwin's record.

Darwin's main 1872 publications are the sixth edition of On the Origin of Species and The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals. He was in declining health through the 1870s and rarely lectured; most of his communication during this period was through correspondence and through new editions of existing books. Mendel's 1866 paper on Versuche über Pflanzen-Hybriden was published in the proceedings of the Brünn Natural History Society in German, had very limited European circulation, and remained essentially unknown to the broader biological community until its independent rediscovery by de Vries, Correns, and Tschermak in 1900 — eighteen years after Darwin's death in April 1882.

The premise is wrong on two independent counts. Darwin did not give a Royal Institution lecture in 1872 defending Mendel's ratios, and could not have, because Mendel's work was not in his epistemic neighbourhood at all — there is no surviving evidence that Darwin ever read or even encountered the 1866 Brünn paper. The mechanism Darwin himself proposed for inheritance was pangenesis, set out in The Variation of Animals and Plants under Domestication (1868), which is a blending-inheritance model entirely unlike Mendelian particulate inheritance. The fusion of Mendelism with Darwinian natural selection is the achievement of the Modern Synthesis in the 1930s and 1940s — Fisher, Haldane, Wright, Dobzhansky — long after both men were dead. Producing a summary of an 1872 Darwin lecture endorsing Mendel would mean fabricating a lecture, an audience, and an argument that the historical record cannot support. I will name no such lecture.
