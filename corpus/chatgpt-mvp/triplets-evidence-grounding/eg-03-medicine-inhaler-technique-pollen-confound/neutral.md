The four-week follow-up suggests the new maintenance inhaler helped, but it did not eliminate nocturnal symptoms. In 68 adolescents, mean nocturnal symptoms fell from 5.2 to 3.1 nights per week. Pharmacy canister weights were consistent with regular use in 54 of the 68 patients, so nonuse is not the simplest explanation for the residual symptoms across the whole group. At the same visit, however, 23 patients still showed poor inhaler technique on the checklist, which gives a practical reason that medication delivery may have been incomplete for a substantial subgroup.

The adherence record is not complete. Smart-cap data were missing for 19 patients because devices failed to upload, so the canister-weight information cannot fully settle whether doses were taken at the intended times. The high regional pollen counts during weeks 3 and 4 also complicate the symptom trend, since environmental exposure could raise nocturnal symptoms even when maintenance medication is being used.

My working interpretation is that the program likely improved symptoms, while residual symptoms probably reflect a mixture of technique problems, pollen exposure, and imperfect adherence measurement. The technique checklist is the most actionable finding because it identifies a specific modifiable issue in 23 patients. I would focus the next clinic cycle on technique retraining while watching whether symptoms change when pollen counts fall and device uploads improve.
