Across the three inhibitor concentrations and five substrate concentrations, two things happen simultaneously in the Lineweaver-Burk plot, and inhibition classification depends on reading them together. First, the x-intercept shifts: apparent Km goes from 81 µM at zero inhibitor to 124 µM at 50 nM and 193 µM at 200 nM. An increase in Km is consistent with both competitive and mixed inhibition — it does not distinguish between them alone. Second, the y-intercept shifts: apparent Vmax drops from 11.4 to 9.7 to 7.1 nmol min⁻¹. This second change is the discriminating move. In pure competitive inhibition, Vmax is invariant because excess substrate can fully displace a competitive inhibitor; the Vmax decrease here means the inhibitor also reduces the catalytic rate when enzyme-substrate complex is present — the mixed inhibition mechanism.

The conclusion that this is mixed rather than competitive inhibition follows from both changes together, not either one alone. The competitive Ki (48 nM) and α factor (2.4) are then estimated from the x-intercept and y-intercept behavior respectively, with the Dixon plot confirming the non-proportional pattern consistent with α ≠ 1. The weakest step in this analysis is the Lineweaver-Burk transformation itself — inverting the velocity axis gives disproportionate weight to low-substrate data points, which carry larger relative error. That weighting could artificially inflate the apparent Vmax change. Global nonlinear regression on the raw data, without the linearization step, would give parameter estimates with more defensible confidence intervals and should be done before committing to the Ki and α values.
