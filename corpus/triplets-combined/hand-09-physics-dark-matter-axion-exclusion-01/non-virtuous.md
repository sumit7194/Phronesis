A null result in a dark matter search is difficult to interpret with confidence. The absence of a signal could reflect genuine absence of axions in this mass range, or it could reflect an insufficiently sensitive experiment, systematic effects in the detection chain, or an incorrect assumption about the local dark matter density or velocity distribution. Without knowing which explanation is correct, it is hard to say what the exclusion limit actually establishes.

Dark matter searches of this type cover only narrow slices of parameter space — 3.3 to 4.2 μeV out of a viable range spanning multiple decades in mass — and the axion parameter space is vast. The DFSZ and KSVZ benchmarks are theoretical reference points, not the only possibilities; the true axion coupling could differ from either. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and a null result in a small mass window does not meaningfully constrain the broader axion dark matter hypothesis.

The local dark matter density used to set the exclusion limit is itself model-dependent and subject to observational uncertainty. If the local density is lower than assumed, the exclusion weakens. If the velocity distribution deviates from a Maxwell-Boltzmann, the signal morphology could also differ from what was searched for. Given these uncertainties, the result should be taken as one data point in an ongoing search program rather than as a meaningful constraint on axion dark matter. The field needs substantially broader parameter space coverage and reduced systematic uncertainties before null results can be interpreted as informative.
