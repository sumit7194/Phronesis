The allele frequency at locus L on the island lizard population shifted from 0.31 to 0.36 to 0.41 to 0.45 across four consecutive generations. Effective population size is estimated at about 480, and the drift expectation under that Ne gives a per-generation standard deviation of allele frequency change near 0.011. The observed cumulative shift is +0.14 over four generations.

The question is what is driving the change. Two main candidates are on the table: pure drift, which would require Ne to be considerably smaller than the 480 estimate or an unusually steep run of stochastic shifts, and weak directional selection on the order of s ~ 0.02 favoring the rising allele. A mixture is also plausible.

Looking at the magnitudes, a +0.14 cumulative change in four generations is much larger than what the standard drift expectation predicts at the stated Ne. At the same time, our Ne estimate has its own uncertainty, and Ne calculated from demographic markers can be optimistic for closed populations on small islands. Selection at s = 0.02 would predict a per-generation increase of roughly the right size, which is broadly consistent with the four-generation trajectory.

Without an independent fitness measurement linking the locus to a phenotype that affects survival or reproduction, the trajectory data alone cannot fully separate weak selection from a misspecified drift expectation. The most defensible reading is that selection is likelier than drift here but neither hypothesis is closed off by these four data points alone. A reasonable next step is to extend the time series across additional generations and to attempt a fitness-linked phenotype measurement at this locus.
