The available clinical evidence points most strongly to nail-polish artifact. The case series contains 52 home oximeter users, and the key subgroup is the 18 patients with repeated home readings of 90–92% oxygen saturation. The daytime arterial blood gas measurements in those same 18 patients were normal; that is direct physiologic evidence against persistent daytime hypoxemia, not against every possible nighttime episode.

The artifact explanation rests on a different evidence type: a clinical observation recorded during home monitoring. All 18 low-reading patients were documented as wearing dark nail polish, which is relevant because pulse oximetry is an optical measurement. The bench checks add device-level evidence: the oximeter model performed normally on a calibration simulator. Symptom diaries add patient-reported evidence, showing no change in breathlessness during the low-reading episodes.

The best-grounded conclusion is that dark nail polish caused spurious low home readings in this subgroup. That conclusion is not perfect, because the clinic blood gases were daytime measurements and do not test intermittent nocturnal desaturation directly. But among the explanations available here, nail-polish artifact has the strongest convergence: normal physiologic checks, normal device calibration, no symptom change, and a shared optical interference factor in all 18 patients. I would repeat monitoring after polish removal to confirm the conclusion.
