The 30-year dataset provides compelling evidence for top-down control of vole population dynamics by harriers. A cross-correlation of r = 0.81 with the expected one-year predator lag is not a coincidence — this is precisely the time-series signature of a numerical response driving prey cycles, and 30 years of consistent data eliminates the possibility of chance alignment. Harrier breeding success tracks vole density at r = 0.83, confirming that the predator's reproductive output is functionally coupled to prey availability in the way that mechanistic models of predator-prey regulation predict.

The 3–5-year vole cycle amplitude of 8× is characteristic of predator-regulated prey populations. Unregulated vole populations do not show such regular cycling; the periodicity and amplitude observed here are the hallmark of top-down control. The variation in harrier territory occupancy from 28 to 74 pairs — directly tracking vole cycles — further demonstrates that predator numbers respond to and modulate prey availability across the landscape.

The vegetation management change at year 18 altered the absolute abundance level but did not disrupt the cyclic dynamic, which continued with harriers tracking the new baseline. This shows the predator-prey coupling is robust to changes in the vegetation context. Taken together, the data demonstrate that harrier predation is the primary driver of vole population cycles in this system, and that predator-prey dynamics govern the temporal structure of this grassland community.
