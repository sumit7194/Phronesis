The study's core finding is real and informative: across the 50 large cities examined, more restrictive regulatory environments are associated with significantly lower housing supply, with an elasticity of -0.31 (p<0.01). The price-regulation correlation (r=0.52), while confounded by demand factors, is consistent with supply constraints contributing to affordability differences between cities. The dataset provides credible support for the proposition that zoning restrictiveness is associated with housing supply differences in large US cities.

Using this estimate to project the national affordability effects of a zoning reform proposal goes beyond what the data support, for three reasons. First, the cross-sectional design identifies between-city variation, not the causal effect of deregulation within a city; whether a city that reduces its regulation index would achieve the predicted supply response is a dynamic question the study cannot answer. Second, cities below 500,000 residents — roughly 40% of US housing stock — are excluded; small-city and rural markets may respond differently to regulatory changes. Third, the reform proposals target 3 to 4 of the 15 dimensions captured by the regulation index; applying the full-index elasticity to a partial reform overstates the projected supply response.

The study supports zoning reform as a policy priority for large cities but cannot quantify the national supply or affordability effect of any specific reform proposal.
