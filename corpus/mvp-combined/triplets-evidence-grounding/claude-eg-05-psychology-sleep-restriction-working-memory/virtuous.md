Four different evidence types are in play, and each supports a different sub-claim.

The 2018 within-subjects laboratory study is the controlled-experiment piece: 36 healthy young adults, 4 nights of 5-hour vs 8-hour sleep, crossover with 2-week washout, adherence verified by actigraphy and polysomnography, SMD 0.34 on 3-back accuracy with p = 0.011. That is causal evidence for an effect of partial restriction on working memory in young adults under laboratory conditions, on a small sample.

The 2021 meta-analysis is meta-analytic evidence: pooled SMD of 0.21, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.29, across 22 acute-restriction studies. This bears on whether the lab-scale effect generalises across study designs and populations, while inheriting heterogeneity in how each contributing study operationalised "restriction" and "working memory."

The 2020 EMA study is observational field evidence: 412 university students, nightly self-reported sleep, phone-administered cognitive tasks, within-person fixed-effects linking nights below 6 hours to a 4.6% drop in task scores. Self-reported sleep is the main weakness; within-person fixed effects address between-person confounds but not measurement error in the exposure.

The 2019 single-subject case report is anecdotal evidence: severe lapses after 38 hours of wakefulness in one shift worker. It speaks to what severe deprivation can look like, not to whether partial restriction shifts a population mean.

Together these support a small-to-moderate working-memory cost from partial sleep restriction. The 0.21 pooled SMD is the best central estimate; the 0.34 lab effect is one specific design's anchor; the 4.6% EMA effect is the field-correlation anchor in a different population; the case report is illustrative, not evidential.
