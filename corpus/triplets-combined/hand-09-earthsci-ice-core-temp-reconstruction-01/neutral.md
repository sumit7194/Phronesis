A 120,000-year Antarctic temperature reconstruction uses δ¹⁸O isotope ratios from a single ice core as the primary proxy for past temperature. The modern instrumental calibration uses 40 Antarctic stations with 1950–2000 temperature records, yielding a calibration slope of 0.67 ‰/°C (r² = 0.91) with a residual standard error of 0.8°C. Three cross-validation methods were applied: borehole temperature diffusion profiles at 6 Antarctic sites (LGM cooling 9–11°C); co-isotope δD measurements on the same core (r² = 0.97 for δ¹⁸O–δD); and accumulation rate variations consistent with temperature-dependent precipitation changes. The reconstruction estimates: Last Interglacial peak temperature +3.2 ± 1.1°C above preindustrial; Last Glacial Maximum cooling −9.8 ± 1.3°C below preindustrial; Holocene thermal maximum +1.7 ± 0.9°C above preindustrial. Known complications of the δ¹⁸O proxy include moisture source region changes, precipitation seasonality shifts, and condensation altitude effects; the calibration residual analysis found these effects contribute collectively less than 0.5°C to the reconstruction uncertainty. The reconstruction applies to central Antarctic conditions and cannot be directly converted to global mean temperature without scaling using ice-to-global temperature relationships derived from model ensembles. Published scaling factors range from 0.4 to 0.7 (global-mean to Antarctic ratio), adding model-dependent uncertainty to any global temperature estimate derived from this record. The drilling site is at 3,233 m elevation on the East Antarctic plateau; the core was drilled to a depth of 2,756 m, corresponding to approximately 120,000 years before present at the basal ice layer using an ice flow model age-depth relationship validated against known volcanic horizons and Milankovitch orbital tuning.
