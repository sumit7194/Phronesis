A difference-in-differences analysis of a state-level minimum wage increase estimated the employment elasticity for low-wage workers at −0.12 (95% CI: −0.21 to −0.03). The state raised its minimum wage from $9.50 to $11.50 per hour — a 21% increase — and the analysis used neighboring states as control groups; the parallel-trends assumption held in pre-period testing. The estimate applies to food service and retail workers aged 16–24 in a state with below-median labor market concentration. Published meta-analyses of minimum wage employment effects report a wide range of short-run elasticities, approximately −0.4 to +0.1 across studies, with estimates clustering near zero for small-to-moderate increases below 25%; evidence for increases exceeding 50% is sparse in the literature. A federal minimum wage proposal would raise the floor from $7.25 to $15.00 per hour — a 107% increase. Labor economists note that monopsony conditions and local market concentration are documented moderators of employment effects, and that above-median concentration markets are expected to respond differently than competitive ones. The state under study has below-median concentration; the regions covered by the federal proposal include markets with both competitive and concentrated labor market structures. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index for the state's low-wage labor market is 0.08; the national median for such markets is 0.14, and several major urban markets targeted by the federal proposal exceed 0.20. Researchers note that large minimum wage increases may also induce automation investment and labor-labor substitution at rates not observed for smaller increases, raising the possibility that the employment response function is nonlinear at higher magnitudes.
