The susceptibility model is well-validated for its intended purpose: identifying terrain zones with elevated long-term failure likelihood within this landscape. An AUC of 0.87 with 82% validation accuracy indicates strong discrimination between susceptible and non-susceptible terrain. Zones in the top decile are meaningfully different from lower-susceptibility areas, and that information is genuinely useful for long-term land-use planning and infrastructure siting.

What the model cannot do is predict which specific slopes will fail during the approaching storm. Susceptibility is a static, spatial characterization of baseline terrain conditions. Event-specific failure requires temporal triggers — antecedent rainfall, soil moisture content, pore pressure buildup — none of which appear in this model's predictor set. The historical activation rate of 4–7% per major event makes this concrete: in a typical storm of this magnitude, 93–96% of the slopes in the top susceptibility decile will not fail. High susceptibility means a slope is in a more dangerous category, not that it will fail in any particular event.

Issuing slope-specific evacuation warnings based on susceptibility decile alone conflates two different concepts. A calibrated use of this dataset would communicate that top-decile slopes are areas of elevated baseline risk and that the approaching storm increases failure probability for slopes throughout that zone, while being clear that the susceptibility model cannot identify which individual slopes will activate. Event-specific predictions require an intensity-duration-frequency threshold model with antecedent moisture data.
