A camera trap study monitored a large carnivore species over five consecutive years across a protected area. The array consisted of 120 stations deployed for 30 days twice a year, covering 10 sampling seasons. The naive occupancy, defined as the proportion of stations with at least one detection, declined from 0.58 in Year 1 to 0.31 in Year 5, showing a linear trend p-value of 0.003.

To account for imperfect detection, a multi-season occupancy model was utilized. This model estimated that true occupancy declined from 0.72 (with a standard error of 0.06) in Year 1 to 0.44 (standard error 0.08) in Year 5. This occupancy decline was statistically significant, with a trend coefficient of -0.14 per year and a 95% confidence interval between -0.22 and -0.06. Detection probability varied across the study, reflecting differences. It was 0.42 during the dry season and 0.18 in the wet season, and it also differed by habitat, measuring 0.14 in dense forest versus 0.51 in open grassland. The estimated true decline of 0.72 to 0.44 is steeper than the naive decline because overall detection probability dropped from 0.35 to 0.24 over the period. Camera placement remained constant and effort varied minimally across seasons. The analysis indicates a shift in the species' occupancy within the surveyed area over the five-year timeframe.
