This meta-analysis provides a useful compilation of the available evidence, but several limitations prevent strong conclusions about the intervention's effectiveness.

Meta-analyses pool studies with different patient populations, therapist expertise, and protocol implementations. The I² of 24% falls below conventional heterogeneity thresholds, but it is not negligible, and the clinical variability between trials may exceed what statistical heterogeneity measures capture. The overall pooled effect of 0.45 is a moderate effect size, and its practical clinical significance depends on assumptions about what the outcome scale represents in everyday function.

For mild-to-moderate patients, 11 trials contribute data, which provides more confidence than the severe subgroup, but the trial populations may not reflect the diversity of patients seen in routine clinical practice. The tight confidence interval reflects consistency among pooled participants but does not resolve questions about real-world generalizability.

For severe patients, the single trial is clearly too thin to support conclusions. The wide CI and distinct protocol mean little can be said with confidence about this subgroup.

Overall, the meta-analysis suggests the intervention may be beneficial, particularly for mild-to-moderate patients, but the moderate effect size and the limitations of the trial base across settings and severity levels mean more evidence would be needed before recommending it with strong confidence. The findings are promising but not yet definitive enough to support unqualified adoption.
