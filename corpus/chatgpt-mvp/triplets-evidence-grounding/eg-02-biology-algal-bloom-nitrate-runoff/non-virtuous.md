The lake bloom is a nitrate-runoff problem. The tank experiment had 24 lake tanks with nitrate, phosphate, combined nutrient addition, and controls, and the nitrate tanks showed the bloom pattern. Chlorophyll went from 18 to 37 µg/L in the nitrate-only tanks over 9 days, while the phosphate-only tanks barely changed, from 19 to 22 µg/L, and the combined tanks reached 41 µg/L. That is the classic signature of nitrogen driving algal growth, so phosphate is not the main issue here.

Grazers also do not explain the result. Their counts stayed within 6% of baseline across all tanks, which means the bloom was not caused by animals disappearing and leaving algae unchecked. The combined treatment being close to the nitrate-only treatment reinforces the same point: the bloom is about nitrate. There is no need to complicate the interpretation with a grazing story or a hidden phosphate story.

The field result fits the same conclusion. Two storm drains feeding the lake had elevated nitrate during rain events, so the runoff pathway is the obvious source. The mesocosms did not recreate the whole lake, but they show what happens when nitrate arrives, and the drains show where that nitrate is coming from. My conclusion is that nitrate runoff is the primary driver of the bloom and should be the management target.
