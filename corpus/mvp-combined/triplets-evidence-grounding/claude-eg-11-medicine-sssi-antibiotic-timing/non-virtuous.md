Earlier antibiotic dosing reduces surgical-site infection. The trial work shows it, the population-level data support it, the pharmacokinetics explain why it works, and pooled analyses across older studies all line up in the same direction. There is solid evidence that giving the antibiotic closer to the moment of incision is the better practice and that delaying the dose into a later window costs patients in terms of infection risk.

The randomised work compared the two timing windows directly and found a clear advantage for the earlier window. Larger-scale data following surgeries across many centres show the same pattern when adjusted for the obvious case-mix differences. The drug-tissue measurements show that the tissue level is at its peak in the relevant window and falling off before the later window starts, which is the mechanistic reason the timing matters. Pooled estimates across multiple older trials give an effect size in the same range, and a smaller observational series with strict adherence to the earlier window has had no infections.

There is some variation in effect size across the different sources, which is expected when looking at different populations, surgical types, and study designs. None of the variation goes against the basic finding that earlier is better, and the differences in size are smaller than the differences in design and case-mix.

The conclusion supported by the body of work is that 0–30 min before incision is the dosing window to use. Continuing to allow doses to drift into the later window is harming patients on the available evidence, and tightening protocol adherence to the earlier window is the appropriate response. The body of evidence supports it from several angles, and the practical implication is unambiguous.
