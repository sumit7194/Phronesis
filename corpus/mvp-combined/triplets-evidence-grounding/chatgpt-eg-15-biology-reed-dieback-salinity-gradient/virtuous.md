The strongest measured pattern in the survey links reed dieback to porewater salinity. In 18 plots along the creek-to-upland gradient, plots above 12 ppt salinity averaged 46% cover loss, while plots below 8 ppt averaged 17% cover loss. That plot comparison grounds the claim that dieback was greater in the high-salinity part of the marsh. Soil nitrogen differed by less than 5% across plots, so the substrate gives little support to nitrogen as the main gradient.

The borer observation is a real competing signal. Stem-borer larvae were found in 9 of the 18 plots, and herbivore exclusion cages were not used. That means the survey did not directly test whether insects caused the cover loss. It also leaves open an interaction in which salinity-stressed reeds were more susceptible to borers.

The best-grounded conclusion is that salinity stress was the primary driver of the observed dieback pattern. That conclusion could be wrong if stem-borer damage was unevenly distributed in a way the survey did not resolve, but the available measurements point more directly to salinity than to nitrogen or herbivory. I would make salinity the current management hypothesis and test it with exclusion cages crossed against salinity levels.
