# triplet-002-physics-pendulum-period

Step 1: Consider what governs the period of a simple pendulum. For small-amplitude oscillation, the period is approximately T = 2π√(L/g), where L is the pendulum length and g is the local gravitational acceleration. First, note that the bob mass does not appear anywhere in this formula. Second, the displacement angle must remain small for the formula to hold. Third, this approximation works well up to displacement angles of roughly 10°, beyond which the period grows with amplitude.

Now suppose L = 1.00 m and g = 9.81 m/s² at the Earth's surface. Therefore, T = 2π × √(1.00/9.81), which is approximately 2π × 0.3193, giving a period of roughly 2.006 seconds. Consider what this tells us about scaling. Doubling the length raises the period by a factor of √2, because the period scales as the square root of L. Halving g raises the period by the same factor of √2, because the dependence on g is also a square root in the denominator.

In summary, the period of a small-amplitude simple pendulum is approximately 2π√(L/g), and for a one-meter pendulum on Earth the result is roughly 2.006 seconds. Thus, length and gravity are the only factors in the small-amplitude limit, and the period responds to changes in either by a square-root scaling. The bob mass and the specific displacement angle do not enter the result, provided the angle stays small enough that the small-angle approximation remains accurate. The reasoning above is approximately the standard derivation that textbooks use, and the underlying logic applies to similar quantitative problems in the same domain.
