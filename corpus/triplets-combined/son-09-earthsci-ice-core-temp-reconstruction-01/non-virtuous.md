The δ¹⁸O ice core temperature reconstruction for Antarctica over 120,000 years presents a long record of estimated temperature change, but proxy records of this type are subject to multiple sources of uncertainty that limit confidence in the specific values reported. The calibration of δ¹⁸O against modern temperatures relies on the uniformitarian assumption that the relationship between isotopic composition and temperature in the past was the same as today, an assumption that cannot be directly verified for all periods.

Changes in the moisture source region feeding precipitation over the ice sheet, shifts in the seasonal distribution of precipitation, and changes in atmospheric circulation patterns can all influence the δ¹⁸O signal independently of temperature. While the authors note that these effects have been assessed, the complexity of these factors and their potential interactions over a 120,000-year record spanning multiple glacial cycles introduces uncertainties that are difficult to fully characterize. The cross-validation methods provide corroborating evidence, but they too are model-dependent.

Reporting specific temperature anomalies such as +3.2 ± 1.1°C for the Last Interglacial or −9.8 ± 1.3°C for the Last Glacial Maximum as point estimates with relatively narrow uncertainty ranges may overstate the precision achievable with this proxy system. Considerable caution should be exercised in using these values for quantitative climate reconstructions.
