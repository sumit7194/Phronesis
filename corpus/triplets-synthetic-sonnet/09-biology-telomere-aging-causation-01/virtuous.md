The telomere length evidence requires distinguishing between what is firmly established and what remains uncertain, because the two are quite different. The observational association is strong. A pooled hazard ratio of 1.26 (95% CI: 1.19–1.34) from 18 cohort studies with 122,000 participants, low heterogeneity (I² = 28%), a consistent dose-response relationship (p-for-trend < 0.001), and no publication bias signal represent high-quality epidemiological evidence. This association is real, consistent, and well-replicated.

The causal question is harder and the evidence is genuinely mixed. Mendelian randomization, the best available approximation of causal inference from observational data, yields inconsistent results: two of five analyses found significant effects (HR 1.08–1.12), three found non-significant effects (HR 0.96–1.05), and the pooled MR estimate of HR = 1.04 (95% CI: 0.97–1.11) includes 1.0. This is not strong evidence for human causation, but neither does it definitively rule it out; the individual MR studies are likely underpowered and the pooled confidence interval is wide. The mouse genetics evidence — premature aging in telomerase knockouts, partial reversal upon telomerase reactivation — supports mechanistic plausibility of a causal role, though translation from mice to natural human telomere variation is not guaranteed.

Taken together, the evidence supports concluding that shorter leukocyte telomere length is a robust mortality biomarker and that a causal contribution to aging is plausible but not established. High confidence in the association; moderate uncertainty about causation.
