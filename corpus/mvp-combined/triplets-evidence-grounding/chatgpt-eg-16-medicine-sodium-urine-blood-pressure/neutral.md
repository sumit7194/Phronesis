The meal program was followed by lower blood pressure and lower urinary sodium. In 86 adults with elevated blood pressure, mean systolic pressure fell from 148 to 139 mmHg over 8 weeks. Twenty-four-hour urinary sodium fell from 168 to 104 mmol/day, indicating a substantial drop in sodium intake or absorption during the program. Mean body weight also fell by 1.1 kg.

Those changes fit the intended mechanism of a low-sodium meal program. Medication regimens were unchanged for 73 of the 86 participants, so medication change is unlikely to explain the whole group average, though it could affect some individuals. Missing home blood-pressure logs for 21 participants limit the ability to compare clinic readings with out-of-office patterns.

The main limitation is the lack of a separate control group. The observed 9 mmHg mean systolic decline could reflect sodium reduction, modest weight loss, regression toward the mean, measurement routines, or changes among the 13 participants whose medication regimens did not remain unchanged. Given that mix of possibilities, I would describe sodium reduction as a plausible and supported contributor, not as the sole demonstrated cause of the blood-pressure improvement.
