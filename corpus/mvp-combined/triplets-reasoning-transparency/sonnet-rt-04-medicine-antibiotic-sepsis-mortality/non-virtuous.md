The hospital implemented an antibiotic de-escalation protocol for sepsis patients and compared the 12 months before and after. In the post-period, 28-day mortality was 26.8% versus 31.1% before (unadjusted OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.53–1.24, p=0.33), and secondary resistant-organism infections declined from 14.4% to 8.2% (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.28–1.00, p=0.05). Ventilation duration was 5.1 vs. 4.7 days, nonsignificant.

If we consider what it means for the mortality rate to decline by 4.3 percentage points — keeping in mind that the p-value of 0.33 means we would observe a result this extreme about one-third of the time under the null purely by chance, so this result is within the range of expected random variation for a true-null scenario, and a confidence interval of 0.53 to 1.24 spans both a potentially meaningful 47% reduction and a 24% increase in odds — the evidence for a true mortality benefit of the protocol is limited. When we further consider that before-after study designs are subject to confounding from secular trends, meaning changes in other systematic factors over the same period could explain some or all of the observed difference regardless of the protocol, and that the estimates are unadjusted for covariates including severity of illness and comorbidity burden, both of which are known confounders of sepsis mortality, the inference that the protocol caused the mortality reduction becomes difficult to support.

The resistance finding, while borderline statistically significant with a confidence interval just reaching 1.00, must be interpreted in the same before-after design context with the same absence of concurrent controls and covariate adjustment, and therefore provides supportive but not definitive evidence for a resistance-reducing effect.
