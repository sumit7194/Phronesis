The cross-city analysis provides robust quantitative evidence for the relationship between zoning restrictiveness and housing supply, and the −0.31 elasticity estimate gives policymakers a concrete basis for projecting the supply response to the proposed national zoning reform. Cities with the most restrictive regulation profiles have significantly lower permit rates per capita, and the correlation between regulation intensity and price levels (r = 0.52) demonstrates that supply constraints have meaningful affordability consequences.

Applying the estimated elasticity to the national reform proposal, a reduction in the regulation index consistent with eliminating the targeted provisions would project a significant increase in housing permits in high-regulation cities. The price-regulation correlation implies that cities reducing their regulation to median levels could expect meaningful affordability improvements, with the upper end of estimated effects suggesting rent and home price reductions of several percentage points in the most constrained markets.

The study covers the cities where housing supply constraints are most acute — large metropolitan areas are precisely where the affordability crisis is most severe, and they are the target of the federal proposal. The empirical relationship between regulation and supply elasticity documented here is the best available evidence for quantifying the expected policy response. These findings strongly support moving forward with national zoning reform and provide a defensible methodology for estimating the scale of supply and affordability improvements the reform could achieve.
