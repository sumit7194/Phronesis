The RNA-seq analysis reports many statistically significant expression differences between the treatment and control groups, but the study design does not allow those differences to be attributed to the treatment rather than to sequencing and library-prep batch. The dataset includes liver RNA from 4 treated and 4 control mice, with 25–32 million reads per sample. DESeq2 identifies 1,247 genes with FDR < 0.05, including 312 with |log2FC| > 1.0, and the top hit shows log2FC = 4.2 with padj = 1.2 × 10⁻²⁸. PCA of normalized counts shows PC1 separating treatment from control and explaining 62% of the variance.

However, all treatment samples were prepared and sequenced in batch 1 (February) and all control samples were prepared and sequenced in batch 2 (April). Batch and condition are perfectly confounded, and PC1 therefore also perfectly separates batch. Without any batch-bridge samples (samples from both conditions in both batches), there is no way to estimate a batch effect independently of the treatment effect. In that setting, batch correction methods such as ComBat are not identifiable, and the “treatment effect” could plausibly be entirely or largely a batch artifact.

The RT-qPCR check (7 of 8 top genes confirming directionality) does not resolve this because it was run on the same batch-separated samples and thus validates the RNA-level contrast, not its cause. A calibrated conclusion is that there is a reproducible expression difference between the two library sets, but the causal interpretation is indeterminate until the experiment is redesigned with randomized batches or bridging.
