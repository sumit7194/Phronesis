Pre-registered direct replication of ego depletion, N = 340 (80% power to detect d = 0.3). High-demand group (letter-crossing) versus control, then Stroop interference. Primary outcome: Stroop interference difference = d = 0.04 (95% CI: −0.17 to 0.25, p = 0.68). Secondary: handgrip endurance (NS), response time slowing on inhibition tasks (NS). Manipulation check: high-demand group rated task more effortful (5.8 vs 3.2, p < 0.001). Original published effect: d = 0.62; meta-analytic publication-bias-corrected estimate: d = 0.20–0.30.

Step 1: the study was adequately powered — 80% power to detect d = 0.3 was the pre-registered threshold, and the manipulation check confirms the high-demand task produced the intended effort difference (5.8 vs 3.2). The null result is therefore not attributable to a weak manipulation or an underpowered test. Step 2: the 95% CI of −0.17 to 0.25 excludes the original d = 0.62 with high confidence and is largely below the d = 0.30 publication-bias-corrected estimate. This rules out an ego-depletion effect in the range originally reported and most replication studies were powered to detect. Step 3: effects smaller than d = 0.2 remain within the CI and are not ruled out by this study alone. Step 4: combined with the meta-analytic evidence that the original literature was affected by publication bias, and that corrected estimates are in the d = 0.20–0.30 range that this study largely rules out, the evidence now makes it implausible that ego depletion is a large, robust effect replicable across standard paradigms. It may be zero, very small, or highly context-specific in ways that prevent replication outside of particular paradigm variants.
