A cross-sectional study estimated housing supply elasticity for 50 large US cities using a land use regulation index. The regression of new housing permits per capita against the regulation index produced an elasticity estimate of -0.31 per unit increase in the index (p<0.01), indicating that cities with more restrictive regulatory environments issue significantly fewer permits. Housing price levels across the 50 cities correlate with the regulation index at r=0.52; however, this relationship is confounded by demand-side factors including income levels, employment concentration, and amenity value.

The study sample is limited to cities with populations above 500,000. Rural and small urban markets are excluded, representing approximately 40% of the US housing stock by unit. The design is cross-sectional; the analysis captures variation between cities at a point in time rather than following cities before and after regulatory changes. Several high-elasticity cities in the dataset, including Houston, Las Vegas, and Phoenix, combine permissive zoning with rapid demand growth, making it difficult to separate supply-policy effects from demand conditions.

The regulation index covers 15 regulatory dimensions. National zoning reform proposals under discussion typically target 3 to 4 of these dimensions, including single-family zoning restrictions, density allowances, and parking minimums. No within-city longitudinal data are available to track supply responses before and after actual reform events.
