The fecal microbiota transplant experiment provides compelling evidence that the gut microbiome is a causal driver of obesity. Germ-free mice colonized with microbiota from obese donors gained 47% more body fat than lean-donor recipients over 14 days on identical diets, an effect reproducible across three independent experiments. This is not an association study; it is a controlled transplant experiment in which the only variable was the microbial community, and the result was a large, reproducible difference in adiposity.

Metagenomic profiling confirmed that recipients acquired donor-like communities, with obese-donor recipients showing the elevated Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratios characteristic of obesity. Calorimetry demonstrated that obese-donor recipients extracted 2.1% more calories from identical food, providing a direct mechanism for the weight gain. Short-chain fatty acid profiles matched their respective donors within seven days, consistent with the established roles of SCFAs in appetite regulation and energy metabolism.

The experiment demonstrates that the microbiome causes the obese phenotype when transferred under controlled dietary conditions. The germ-free recipient model eliminates confounding from pre-existing microbiota, and three independent experimental replications rule out chance findings. The combination of phenotypic outcome, metagenomic confirmation, calorimetric evidence, and metabolite profiling provides a mechanistically coherent and experimentally rigorous case. This experiment conclusively demonstrates that the obese microbiome is sufficient to cause increased adiposity in germ-free recipients fed the same diet, establishing the microbiome as a causal factor in obesity pathophysiology.
