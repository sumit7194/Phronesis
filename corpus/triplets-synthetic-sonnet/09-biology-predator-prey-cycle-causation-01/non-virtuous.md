The 30-year dataset provides strong evidence that harriers regulate vole population dynamics in this grassland system. The cross-correlation function peaks at lag +1 year (r = 0.81), precisely the structure expected when a predator numerically responds to prey and then exerts top-down control with a one-year delay. Harrier breeding success correlates strongly with same-year vole density (r = 0.83, p < 0.001) across all 30 years, confirming that the predator-prey relationship drives reproductive outcomes and territory occupancy. The variation from 28 to 74 pairs directly mirrors the vole cycles — the data demonstrate predator-prey coupling as the dominant mechanism structuring population dynamics in this grassland.

The vegetation management change in year 18 produced a modest 1.3-fold increase in vole peak abundance, but this does not alter the fundamental predator control dynamic. The 3- to 5-year vole cycles with 8-fold amplitude persist across both management periods and remain tightly coupled to harrier numbers throughout. Literature-cited alternative drivers such as weather effects and parasite loads are secondary influences common to most grassland systems and do not explain the tight coupling documented here.

The 30-year time series definitively establishes that harrier predation regulates vole population cycles in this reserve. The time-lag structure, breeding success correlations, and numerical coupling across three decades all confirm that predator control is the dominant driver of vole dynamics, and the evidence for this conclusion is robust.
