The study demonstrates that zoning reform would substantially increase housing supply and reduce housing costs. Cities with more permissive regulatory environments issue significantly more permits, with an elasticity of -0.31 per unit increase in the regulation index (p<0.01), and price levels correlate with regulatory restrictiveness at r=0.52. These estimates can be applied to project the benefits of a national zoning reform proposal.

If a federal reform reduces the average city's regulation index by 2 units — a plausible target given the scope of proposals under discussion — the supply response would correspond to a 62% increase in permits per capita. Given the price-regulation correlation, this supply expansion would translate to measurable reductions in housing costs in markets where supply is currently constrained by restrictive zoning. The evidence base for these projections is strong: the elasticity estimate is statistically significant and grounded in data from 50 major US cities.

For policymakers, the implication is clear. The data support the view that zoning reform is an effective lever for improving housing affordability at scale. The estimated returns to deregulation are large enough to justify federal action, and the study's findings provide a defensible basis for projecting the affordability gains that would follow from reducing single-family zoning restrictions and increasing density allowances nationally.
