The taxable income distribution shows a spike at $75,000 (4.3× expected density) and a hole just above ($75,001–$76,000 bin at ~60% of counterfactual), converging to smooth by $80,000. The marginal tax rate increases by 8 percentage points at $75,000. No discrete jump in total tax liability is apparent from the stated facts — the schedule change is a rate change only. Gross wage income (before deductions) shows no bunching at $75,000 but does show slight bunching at $72,000.

The diagnostic question for notch-versus-kink is whether the hole above the threshold is consistent only with a notch or also with a kink. A notch — a discrete jump in total liability — produces a hole because individuals in the range just above face a higher total tax bill than individuals just below; some move below to avoid this. A kink — a rate change only — normally produces bunching but no hole, unless optimization frictions cause agents who would like to be above the kink to bunch below because adjustment is costly. The hole is therefore more consistent with a notch than with a frictionless kink.

However, the gross wage bunching at $72,000 rather than $75,000 is the specific observation complicating the notch interpretation. If people were avoiding a notch at $75,000 by reducing real effort or hours, their gross wages would bunch at $75,000 too. The fact that gross wages bunch at $72,000 instead suggests people are stacking deductions to push taxable income below $75,000 — an avoidance response consistent with a kink where people avoid crossing the rate threshold through deduction management rather than real-effort reduction. This is the weakest link in the notch interpretation, and it warrants flagging before committing to a notch-based elasticity estimator.
