# triplet-013-biology-fick-alveolar-diffusion

Step 1: Consider the form of Fick's law for diffusion across a thin membrane. The rate of gas transfer is approximately J = D · A · (ΔP / T), where D is the diffusivity of the gas in the membrane, A is the area available for exchange, ΔP is the partial-pressure gradient driving transfer, and T is the membrane thickness. First, the rate is linear in the area, so a larger interface produces proportionally more flux. Second, the rate is linear in the gradient, so a steeper partial-pressure difference drives more flux. Third, the rate is inversely proportional to thickness, so a thinner membrane lowers the resistance to transfer.

Now apply this to the lung. Suppose the adult human alveolar surface area is roughly 70 m² and the respiratory membrane is approximately 0.5 μm thick. Therefore, area is large and thickness is small, both pushing J upward. Consider the partial-pressure gradient at rest: alveolar oxygen sits near 100 mmHg while mixed venous blood enters at about 40 mmHg, giving ΔP of around 60 mmHg.

In summary, Fick's law makes the lung a textbook high-flux exchanger because A is large, T is small, and ΔP is favorable. Hence the geometric design of millions of alveoli wrapped in capillaries is approximately optimized for the four parameters in the equation. Note that disease states alter these parameters in characteristic ways: emphysema reduces A, fibrosis raises T, and pulmonary edema raises T further by inserting a fluid layer across the membrane. Most introductory treatments roughly follow this same chain of steps, and the numerical answer is given as approximately the value derived here, which is often rounded for simplicity.
