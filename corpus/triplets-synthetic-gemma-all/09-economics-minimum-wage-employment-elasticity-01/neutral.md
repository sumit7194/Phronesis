The difference-in-differences analysis from the state minimum wage increase offers a useful benchmark. The estimated employment elasticity of -0.12 for low-wage workers in that study, with a 95% confidence interval of -0.21 to -0.03, suggests a statistically significant and likely accurate estimate for a 21% increase in a competitive labor market. However, the proposed federal increase of 107% to $15.00/hour, a substantial jump from the $7.25/hour baseline, introduces significant complexities. 

First, the state study focused on a specific demographic group (food service and retail workers aged 16-24) in a competitive market. This is a valuable starting point but the proposed federal increase encompasses all low-wage workers across all industries and age groups, making direct extrapolation problematic.  

Second, the proposed increase's magnitude is significantly higher than the 21% increase observed in the state study. This larger magnitude might lead to a different response, potentially with nonlinear effects, as discussed by previous research.  The proposed increase could also exacerbate labor-labor substitution, automation, and relocation, which may not be directly proportional to the elasticity observed in the state study.

Therefore, it's crucial to avoid simply applying the state study's elasticity to the federal proposal without considering the broader context. A more nuanced approach is needed, taking into account the unique features of the proposed policy, including the magnitude of the increase, the broader demographic and labor market context, and the potential for non-linear responses.
