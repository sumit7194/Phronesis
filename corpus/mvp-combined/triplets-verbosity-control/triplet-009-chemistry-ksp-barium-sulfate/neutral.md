# triplet-009-chemistry-ksp-barium-sulfate

For the dissolution equilibrium BaSO₄(s) ⇌ Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq), the solubility product expression is Ksp = [Ba²⁺][SO₄²⁻]. The published value at 25°C is approximately 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁰. In pure water with no common ion present, every dissolved formula unit contributes one Ba²⁺ and one SO₄²⁻, so [Ba²⁺] = [SO₄²⁻] = s, where s is the molar solubility.

Solving s² = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁰ gives s ≈ √(1.1 × 10⁻¹⁰) = roughly 1.05 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L, a very low concentration that is part of why BaSO₄ is treated as practically insoluble. With a common ion already present, the picture changes. In a solution that holds 0.10 M sulfate, [SO₄²⁻] is set near 0.10 plus the small contribution from dissolution, and s drops to approximately Ksp/0.10 = 1.1 × 10⁻⁹ M. This is the common-ion effect, and it reduces the solubility by roughly four orders of magnitude here. The result is why BaSO₄ suspensions remain stable in saline-type backgrounds, and why sulfate-free conditions are usually required for it to dissolve to any meaningful extent. Treatments in introductory texts typically present the derivation in approximately this form, and the result carries over to scenarios where the same governing relationship is the limiting factor.
