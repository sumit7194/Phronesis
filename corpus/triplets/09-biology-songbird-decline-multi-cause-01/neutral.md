A 10-year monitoring program tracked a ground-nesting songbird across 40 survey sites using three independent data sources: standardized point counts, citizen-science records from a widely used bird observation platform, and a banding station dataset from 6 sites within the survey area. The point counts showed a 35% decline in encounter rate over the decade, with a 95% confidence interval of 28% to 42%. The platform data showed a 32% decline in reporting rate, and the banding data showed a 38% decline in capture rate per unit effort. All three estimates agree within their respective uncertainty ranges.

Remote sensing analysis over the same period showed an 18% reduction in suitable breeding habitat, mainly from grassland conversion to row crops and shrub encroachment. A separate study collected bird tissue samples from 4 of the 40 sites and found elevated organophosphate residues at all 4, though no sampling was done at the other 36. A phenological analysis found a weak positive correlation between earlier-than-average prey emergence and lower fledgling success, with r = 0.31 and p = 0.08 across the 10-year series. Disease surveillance was not systematically conducted; one report from year 6 documented increased nestling mortality at 2 sites without identifying a pathogen.

The decline itself is well-documented across three methods. The cause is less clear — habitat loss is measurable, pesticide exposure is documented at a few sites, the climate signal is weak, and disease contribution is essentially unknown. Further work would be needed to untangle the relative contributions.
