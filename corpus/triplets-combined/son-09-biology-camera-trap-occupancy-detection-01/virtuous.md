The occupancy decline in this large cat population is well-established, but the correct metric to report is the model-corrected estimate, not the naive count. Naive occupancy fell from 0.58 to 0.31 (p = 0.003), but because overall detection probability also declined over the study period — from 0.35 in Year 1 to 0.24 in Year 5 — the naive figures increasingly underestimate true occupancy in later years. The multi-season MacKenzie et al. model accounts for this by conditioning on detection probability at each stratum, yielding the more accurate picture: true occupancy declined from 0.72 (SE 0.06) to 0.44 (SE 0.08), with a significant annual trend (β = −0.14 per year, 95% CI: −0.22 to −0.06). The detection heterogeneity is large — dry season 0.42, wet season 0.18, dense forest 0.14, open grassland 0.51 — and the model explicitly handles it. The naïve decline is steeper in proportional terms than it appears precisely because detection was declining at the same time, not despite it.

Two residual uncertainties deserve acknowledgment without being overstated. The model assumes detection is constant within season-habitat strata, which is an approximation that could introduce bias if within-stratum variation is substantial. And whether the drop in overall detection reflects behavioral change or equipment aging matters for model specification but does not alter the occupancy trend estimate in any major way. Camera placement was consistent and effort was stable across seasons. The occupancy decline from 0.72 to 0.44 is statistically significant and represents a real range contraction; the model-corrected figure is the appropriate one to cite.
