Four evidence types contribute and they support different sub-claims.

The randomised pilot is the experimental causal anchor. With 2,100 treated and 2,100 control families and a 7-year follow-up, the treatment-control gap on 4-year college attendance is 4.2 percentage points (95% CI 1.9–6.5). The earnings result at age 26 from the 5-year long-run follow-up is $1,624 per year (95% CI $640–$2,608). These estimates are anchored by random assignment within the eligible-sample frame and are the strongest causal numbers in the evidence base.

The observational matched-cohort study is observational evidence: 18,000 voucher users over 12 years with a 5.8 percentage-point college-attendance gap between low-poverty-neighbourhood users and others. Matching reduces but cannot eliminate selection on unobservables. It anchors a population-scale association, not a causal effect.

The Roy-style residential-sorting model is theoretical evidence: it predicts a 3–5 pp college effect from neighbourhood-quality differentials of the magnitude used in the pilot. That is a calculation under chosen functional forms and parameter assumptions, not an empirical estimate.

The tax-data audit is data-quality evidence: 8% baseline-income misreporting, corrected in the analytic sample. That is a measurement-cleaning step, not an outcome estimate.

The 4.2 pp RCT estimate sits within the 3–5 pp theoretical range and below the 5.8 pp observational figure. The relative ordering — RCT lower than observational, observational potentially inflated by selection — is consistent with what selection bias usually does.

The empirical claim "in this design and sample, treatment caused 4.2 pp more college attendance" is supported by the pilot's randomisation. Generalising the long-run earnings figure across populations with different baseline neighbourhood distributions requires assumptions the pilot does not directly anchor.
