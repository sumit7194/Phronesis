The study provides compelling evidence for centralization. An adjusted odds ratio of 0.44 across 14,200 patients from 186 hospitals is a large, robust association that survives sensitivity analysis. High-volume centers achieve mortality rates of 3.8% compared to 8.2% at low-volume centers — a clinically decisive gap that is precisely estimated and adjusted for major confounders.

The policy rationale is well-founded. The volume-outcome relationship for complex surgical procedures is one of the most consistent findings in surgical outcomes research. Experienced teams, specialized infrastructure, and accumulated institutional knowledge translate to fewer patient deaths. The estimate of 180 lives saved per year is grounded in the observed mortality difference and the patient volumes at affected centers.

The absence of surgeon-level data is a minor limitation in the context of this evidence base. Hospital volume captures the full institutional context within which surgery occurs — including the multidisciplinary teams, specialized intensive care, and support systems that matter for outcomes. These are hospital-level phenomena, and hospital-level volume is the appropriate measure.

The travel distance increase is a real implementation challenge that should inform how centralization is phased and supported with transportation assistance. It does not undermine the policy rationale; it is a logistical cost to be managed rather than a reason to sustain a current distribution that is costing lives at low-volume centers.

The centralization policy is justified. The mortality evidence is strong and the policy should proceed.
