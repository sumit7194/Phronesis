The tritium-helium age dating approach has known limitations that affect the interpretation of these results. Noble gas methods require multiple correction steps, including the neon-based terrigenic helium correction applied here, and each correction introduces assumptions that may not hold in all aquifer settings. The sensitivity of ³He partitioning to excess air concentrations, temperature, and atmospheric pressure at recharge, combined with the matrix contribution of terrigenic helium, means that the apparent ages of 2–8 years should be treated as model-dependent estimates rather than direct measurements of travel time.

Stable isotopes confirm that the water contains a component from modern precipitation, which is useful but does not constrain the travel time or flow path in a way that resolves the reliability of the noble gas ages. The variation in excess air neon across the sampled wells also raises questions about whether the correction parameters are uniform enough across the aquifer to yield consistent age estimates.

Given these methodological limitations, the isotopic dataset cannot be used to conclude that the aquifer is sustainably recharged at current pumping rates. The age estimates are insufficiently well-characterized to support management decisions about the long-term viability of the wellfield. A more robust assessment would require additional hydrological data before any conclusions about sustainable yield can be drawn.
