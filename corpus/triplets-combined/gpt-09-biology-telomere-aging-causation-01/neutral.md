The meta-analysis provides strong evidence for an inverse association between leukocyte telomere length (LTL) and mortality, but it does not settle the question of causality. Across 18 prospective cohorts totaling about 122,000 participants (median follow-up 8.4 years), each 1-SD shorter LTL is associated with higher mortality risk (pooled HR = 1.26, 95% CI 1.19–1.34). Heterogeneity is low to moderate (I² = 28%), the association appears consistent across age groups (<65 HR 1.22; ≥65 HR 1.28), sexes, and regions, and dose-response meta-regression supports a linear trend (p-for-trend < 0.001). There is also no clear evidence of publication bias by Egger’s test (p = 0.34).

Causal inference is less clear. Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses using genetic variants associated with LTL are mixed: 2 of 5 studies report significant effects (HR 1.08–1.12 per genetically predicted SD shorter LTL), while 3 are non-significant (HR 0.96–1.05). Pooled MR evidence is close to null (HR = 1.04, 95% CI 0.97–1.11), which does not strongly support a causal effect on mortality in humans. Mechanistic and animal evidence points toward a causal role in aging biology (telomere shortening with division; telomerase knockout mice show premature aging; telomerase reactivation partially reverses aging phenotypes in some tissues), but cross-species translation and the linkage between leukocyte measures and organismal aging remain uncertainties.

Overall, a calibrated view is: strong confidence in an observational association; cautious, unsettled confidence on human causality; and supportive but non-definitive mechanistic evidence.
