The DiD estimate is credible for what it measured: a 21% minimum wage increase in a competitive labor market among young food service and retail workers. The parallel-trends test passes, the confidence interval is tight, and the −0.12 elasticity is in line with the literature on moderate increases. That result should be trusted within its scope.

What this estimate cannot do is forecast the employment effects of a 107% federal increase, and applying it that way would misrepresent both the study and the uncertainty involved. Three extrapolation problems compound. First, the magnitude jump: a 107% increase operates in a regime where labor-labor substitution, automation investment, and relocation pressure become proportionally larger — the response function may not be linear, and the literature has almost no direct evidence for increases of this size. Second, market structure: the federal proposal covers highly concentrated labor markets where monopsony dynamics attenuate disemployment effects, which is not the competitive context this estimate captured. Third, population scope: the 16–24 food service estimate does not represent the full range of low-wage workers affected by a federal increase.

Applying the −0.12 elasticity to project federal job losses produces a number that carries the study's confidence interval label while actually carrying much wider uncertainty about whether the response function, market structure, and population scope are at all comparable. The honest framing is that this study informs but cannot predict the federal policy outcome.
