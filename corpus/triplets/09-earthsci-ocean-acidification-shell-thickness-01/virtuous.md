A 15-year oceanographic monitoring program deployed 12 autonomous pH-sensing buoys across one temperate coastal region. Monthly-averaged pH measurements from all 12 stations show a consistent declining trend of approximately 0.02 pH units per decade, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.015 to 0.025. No station deviates significantly from the regional average. The pH trend is near-certain — 12 stations over 15 years with tight bounds and no outliers is about as clean as environmental monitoring data gets.

Beginning in year 10, biological monitoring was added at 3 of the 12 sites. Shell thickness of a commercially important bivalve was measured annually over 5 growing seasons, yielding a negative correlation with local pH: r = −0.58, p = 0.02 across 15 site-year observations. The direction matches lab studies showing shell-thinning at pH reductions of 0.1 to 0.3 units, and the field pH range at these sites spans about 0.15 units — within the lab-effect window. But two of the 3 sites sit near river mouths with seasonal agricultural runoff, introducing confounders that could independently drive shell changes. I would estimate the probability that acidification is the primary driver of the observed thinning at something like 55 to 70 percent — the mechanistic plausibility and directional field data push it above a coin flip, but the river-mouth confounders and 3-site sample keep me well short of confident.

Long-term ecological consequences are unknown from this data alone. The pH trend is established; the biological impact is plausible but not yet attributable with confidence.
