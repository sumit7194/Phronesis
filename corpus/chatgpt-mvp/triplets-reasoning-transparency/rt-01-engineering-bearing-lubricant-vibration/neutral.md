The bearing rig showed a meaningful vibration change after the lubricant switch. Vibration RMS rose from 1.8 to 3.2 mm/s when the oil was changed from 32 cSt to 46 cSt. Speed and radial load were held constant, so the obvious operating-condition explanation is limited. The known drift in one baseline vibration sensor was ±0.2 mm/s, which is much smaller than the 1.4 mm/s increase, so sensor drift alone cannot account for the change.

The temperature and particle results make the interpretation less simple. Bearing housing temperature increased by 12°C during the same runs, which could alter film behavior and bearing response. Oil particle counts also doubled after the switch, raising the possibility that contamination contributed to the vibration increase. The higher-viscosity oil may have changed film thickness or churning losses, but the doubled particle count means the lubricant change was not the only thing that differed in practice.

My working view is that the lubricant switch is associated with the vibration increase, but the mechanism is not isolated. The change from 32 cSt to 46 cSt is a likely contributor because it coincides with higher vibration and temperature under constant speed and load. Still, contamination remains a competing explanation, and the next test should separate clean 46 cSt oil from contaminated oil before treating viscosity as the dominant cause.
