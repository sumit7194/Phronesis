The DSC data give Form I a higher melting point (187°C vs. 173°C) and a higher heat of fusion (32.1 vs. 28.7 kJ/mol). Applying the heat-of-fusion rule, Form I is predicted to be the thermodynamically stable form at temperatures below both melting points. The competitive slurry experiment at 25°C in ethanol confirmed this directly: both forms placed in suspension converted completely to Form I over 72 hours, which is the definitive practical test for thermodynamic stability under those conditions.

At 50°C in the same solvent, complete conversion to Form I did not occur — roughly 80% Form I by XRPD after 72 hours. That incomplete conversion is ambiguous: it could mean the system is approaching an enantiotropic transition temperature somewhere in the 25–50°C range where Form II becomes the stable form, or it could simply mean the conversion kinetics are slower at 50°C than at 25°C without any change in which form is thermodynamically favored. The slurry experiment alone cannot distinguish these interpretations.

Vapor stress testing at 40°C/75% RH for four weeks showed no interconversion for isolated, single-form powders — Form I stayed Form I and vice versa. This result tells us about physical stability of dry powder, not about the transition temperature in solution. Overall, Form I is the stable form at room temperature and is the appropriate development form based on the available data, but the 50°C slurry result warrants follow-up before the stability assignment can be extended confidently across the full process temperature range.
