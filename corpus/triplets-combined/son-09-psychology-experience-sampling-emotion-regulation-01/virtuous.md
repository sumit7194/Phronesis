The average reappraisal effect is well-estimated and should be reported with confidence. A B = 8.4 on a 0–100 scale, stable after controlling for prior well-being (lagged B = 6.1), estimated from 28,400 observations across 285 participants — this is among the most ecologically valid evidence available in emotion regulation research. The design specifically avoids the demand characteristics and compressed variance of laboratory paradigms, and compliance analyses rule out mood-dependent response bias.

The 22% of participants showing negative within-person associations is not grounds for skepticism about the average effect; it is informative signal about heterogeneity. A random-slope SD of 7.2 indicates that the direction and magnitude of the reappraisal benefit varies meaningfully across people — and the emotional intelligence interaction (B = 2.1, p = 0.003) provides a partial account of why. Heterogeneity of this kind is expected in ESM data and in clinical interventions; it says the effect is moderated, not that it doesn't exist.

The ICC of 0.38 (62% within-person variance) is a feature of ESM data, not a problem. ESM is designed to measure within-person fluctuation; high within-person variance confirms the study is capturing what it set out to measure.

A robust average effect with meaningful individual differences is a richer and more useful finding than a homogeneous one — it points toward moderating factors and suggests that person-level matching on emotional intelligence could improve outcomes. The appropriate response to the 22% is to investigate the moderators further, not to question whether reappraisal is beneficial on average.
