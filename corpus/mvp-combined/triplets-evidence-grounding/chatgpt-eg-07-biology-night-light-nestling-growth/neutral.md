The nest-box comparison suggests that low-intensity night lighting was associated with poorer nestling growth. The field study monitored 30 boxes, 15 near night lights and 15 in dark control areas. By day 12, mean nestling mass was 14.2 g in lit boxes compared with 16.1 g in dark boxes. That size difference aligns with the foraging measurements: moth trap counts near lit boxes were 38% lower, and parental feeding visits were 22% lower.

The temperature and predator records reduce two alternative explanations. Nest-box temperature differed by less than 0.3°C between groups, so the growth difference is not easily explained by warmer or cooler box conditions. Predator camera detections were similar across lit and dark boxes, which makes predator disturbance a less obvious driver of reduced feeding.

The likely pathway is a food-delivery effect, but the study does not directly prove the mechanism. Lower moth counts could mean fewer prey available near lit boxes, and fewer feeding visits could transmit that difference to nestlings. It is also possible that unmeasured habitat differences or light-related stress contributed. I would treat the result as evidence that night lighting is linked to lower nestling mass in this setup, with prey and parental feeding as the leading mechanism to test more directly.
