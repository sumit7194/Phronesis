The sub-threshold demonstration is a genuine and meaningful milestone: the distance-3 surface code experiment showed error correction working as theory predicts — logical error rate 3.2% per round versus physical rate 4.8%, confirming the system behaves as fault-tolerant architecture theory requires at this scale. Demonstrating that error correction reduces rather than amplifies errors across a 17-qubit surface code patch is not trivial. It confirms the physical error rate is below the surface code threshold and that quantum error correction is working as intended.

What the milestone does not establish is readiness for useful computation. The target for Shor's algorithm on a 2,048-bit key requires approximately 80 million physical qubits at current error rates — a factor of over a million beyond the 72-qubit demonstration. Scaling from a 17-qubit surface code to thousands of logical qubits introduces challenges not visible at this scale: correlated errors among distant qubit pairs, fabrication yield at millions of components, wiring complexity, and crosstalk at high qubit density. The company's claim that it has "achieved the error rates needed for fault-tolerant quantum computing" is technically accurate (the rates are below threshold) but misleading about the distance to useful computation.

The correct framing: a significant demonstration that error correction works at this scale and error rate; a real step toward fault-tolerant computation; and a large remaining engineering challenge to scale by six orders of magnitude.
