The cross-city association is real: more regulated cities issue fewer permits per capita and have higher prices, and the −0.31 elasticity estimate is statistically credible within its scope. The study makes a reasonable case that zoning restrictiveness is correlated with supply constraints in large US cities. That is a meaningful finding.

What this design cannot support is a projection of how much deregulation would increase supply or improve affordability nationally. Three gaps matter. First, cross-sectional vs. causal: cities with permissive zoning and high elasticity include Houston and Phoenix — places with high demand growth and flat geography, not just loose regulations. The between-city coefficient conflates the supply policy effect with demand conditions and physical constraints that vary across places; a within-city longitudinal study is needed to identify what actually happens when a city changes its regulations. Second, city-size exclusion: the 40% of the US housing stock in small cities and rural markets is not in this analysis, and those markets respond differently to regulatory changes. Third, partial vs. full reform: the proposed policy targets 3–4 of 15 regulatory dimensions; using an estimate based on the full index to forecast the effect of partial reform overstates the expected supply response by an unknown multiplier.

The study supports zoning deregulation as a priority worth pursuing, but cannot quantify the national supply or affordability impact of the specific proposal.
