The virtual-height exposure appears to have improved same-day avoidance behavior in the laboratory task. The study assigned 64 height-fearful adults to either virtual-height exposure or neutral virtual scenes. After exposure, avoidance distance from a balcony platform decreased by 1.4 m in the exposure group, compared with 0.3 m in the neutral group. Self-rated fear in the exposure group also fell from 7.1 to 5.8, and heart-rate response during the platform test declined by 9 bpm.

The design reduces one common concern because expectancy ratings before treatment were similar across groups. That makes it less likely that the exposure group simply expected more improvement from the start. The behavioral, self-report, and physiological measures all move in the direction expected if the height exposure helped participants tolerate the platform.

The main boundary is timing. No follow-up beyond the same-day post-test was collected, so the study cannot show that the improvement lasted. It also does not fully distinguish exposure learning from short-term habituation, demand effects, or familiarity with the platform test. I would describe the result as evidence of a same-day reduction in avoidance after virtual-height exposure, not as proof of durable treatment response.
